Mock exam · Question A1 · 10 marks

Globalization and its new direction

ProblemDefine globalization and say how its direction has changed since 2000, using decoupling and the multipolar world.
Big ideaBarriers fall, so business now flows both ways, and the world has several centers of growth instead of one.
How (tools)The textbook definition, the before-and-after-2000 contrast, Exhibit 1.2, decoupling and the multipolar world.
ResultA short answer that moves from what globalization is, to how it moves now, to the names for the new world order.

The question: Define globalization as the textbook does, and explain how its direction has changed since 2000. Use the concepts of decoupling and the multipolar world in your answer.

The logic behind the answer

  1. Define it firstGlobalization is the removal of five kinds of barriers between countries: trade, investment, cultural, information technology and political. Everything else in the answer is about what happens once those barriers fall.
  2. Say what it wasBefore 2000, business mostly expanded from developed countries into developing or emerging ones. This is the "before" picture the change is measured against.
  3. Say why it changedEmerging economies began using more open trade and free-market policies to grow. That is the reason business now flows in both directions, and more and more from one developing country to another.
  4. Show the resultThe world's center of economic gravity is moving toward emerging economies, especially China. Exhibit 1.2 gives the proof: China first in 2050, India third and Indonesia fourth.
  5. Name the new orderThe question asks for two terms, and each names one side of the same shift. Decoupling is about the world having many engines of growth; the multipolar world is about developing economies growing on their own strengths.

Easy answer · about 170 words

Write this

Globalization is the process of removing trade, investment, cultural, information technology and political barriers between countries. When these barriers fall, goods, services, investment and even cultural and belief systems move more freely, and countries become more connected and dependent on each other.

Its direction has changed. Before 2000, business mostly moved from developed countries to developing or emerging countries. Today emerging economies use more open trade and free-market policies to grow, so business moves in both directions, and more and more from one developing country to another. The center of the world economy is moving toward emerging economies, especially China. Exhibit 1.2 shows China as the largest economy in 2050, with India third and Indonesia fourth.

Two ideas describe this. Decoupling means the world economy has several engines of growth, such as the United States, the European Union, China, India, Brazil, Russia and South Africa, not the United States alone. A multipolar world means developing economies now grow on their own economic strengths. So the world now has several centers of growth instead of one.

Remember it as BARRIERS DOWN → BOTH WAYS → MANY ENGINES

Barriers down is the definition, both ways is the change since 2000, and many engines is decoupling and the multipolar world.

The IMF, the World Bank and the WTO

ProblemExplain what each of the three big international institutions does for globalization.
Big ideaAfter World War II countries chose to cooperate, and gave three bodies three jobs: money, development and trade rules.
How (tools)The four roles of the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank's old and new focus, and the World Trade Organization's functions and five principles.
ResultOne short paragraph of origin, then one paragraph per institution.

The question: Explain the roles of the IMF, the World Bank and the WTO in facilitating globalization.

The logic behind the answer

  1. Start with why they existGlobalization gained momentum after World War II, when the governments of the free world saw that they needed international cooperation and coordination. That shared reason is where all three institutions come from, so it opens the answer.
  2. Give each one its jobThe International Monetary Fund (IMF) is about global financial stability, the World Bank about rebuilding and then development, and the World Trade Organization (WTO) about trade. Naming the job first makes the lists that follow easy to remember.
  3. Fill in the rolesUnder each job, list what the slides say the institution does: four roles for the IMF, five current focus areas for the World Bank, six functions for the WTO.
  4. Close with the WTO's roots and rulesThe WTO's history began in 1948 under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), which lowered tariffs, quotas and subsidies. Its five principles are the foundation it stands on, so they finish the answer.

Easy answer · about 180 words

Write this

After World War II, the governments of the free world saw that countries had to cooperate. This led to three international organizations that help globalization.

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) supports global financial stability. It is a forum for cooperation on money problems, it helps trade that creates jobs, growth and less poverty, it supports stable exchange rates and open payments, and it lends foreign exchange to countries with balance of payments problems.

The World Bank first helped rebuild Europe after the war. Today it supports trade liberalization, gives policy advice to strengthen free markets and infrastructure, supports international financial standards, shares knowledge with developing countries for sustainable development, and fights communicable diseases. It works through five Group institutions, from low-interest loans to the poorest members to political risk cover for private investment.

The World Trade Organization (WTO) began in 1948 under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), which lowered tariffs, quotas and subsidies. The WTO runs trade agreements, hosts negotiations, settles disputes, reviews trade policies, helps developing countries and works with the IMF and the World Bank. Its five principles are trade without discrimination, freer trade, predictability, fair competition and economic reform in developing countries.

Remember it as MONEY · DEVELOPMENT · TRADE RULES

One job word per pillar, in the order the slides teach them: the IMF, the World Bank, the WTO.

Mock exam · Question A3 · 10 marks

Mercantilism, specialization and factor endowments

ProblemTell three trade theories apart and say why mercantilism fails.
Big ideaMercantilism wants a surplus; the later theories ask who makes what most efficiently, and why.
How (tools)Mercantilism and its flaw, absolute and comparative advantage, and the Heckscher–Ohlin theory with its four factors and two assumptions.
ResultThree short steps that follow the order of the slides: mercantilism, specialization, factor endowments.

The question: Distinguish between absolute advantage, comparative advantage and the Heckscher–Ohlin theory, and explain why mercantilism fails as a basis for trade policy.

The logic behind the answer

  1. Start with mercantilismIt is the oldest theory, and the slides list the theories in the order mercantilism, specialization, factor endowments. Starting there lets the answer explain the flaw before the better ideas.
  2. Show the flawMercantilism measures wealth in precious metal, so it says export as much as possible and import as little as possible. If every country does that, the world has too many exports, prices fall and exporters earn less. One country can run a surplus; all countries cannot.
  3. Separate absolute from comparative advantageAbsolute advantage compares two countries making the same good. Comparative advantage looks inside one country that is better at two or more goods and asks which one it makes relatively more efficiently. Both lead to specialization.
  4. Explain where the advantage comes fromThe Heckscher–Ohlin (H–O) theory says the source is a nation's factor endowments: land, labor, capital and technology. So countries export goods that use their abundant factors.
  5. Add the two assumptionsH–O assumes perfect competition and that factors cannot move between countries. Naming them shows you know the theory's limits.

Easy answer · about 160 words

Write this

Mercantilism is the oldest trade theory, practiced from about 1500 to 1750. It said wealth is the amount of precious metal a country owns, so a country should export as much as possible and import as little as possible to have a trade surplus. It fails because it ignores the big picture: if every country exports more and imports less, there is a surplus of exports, prices fall, exporting countries earn less and goods are undersold.

Absolute advantage means one country can produce a good or service more efficiently than another. Comparative advantage means a country with an absolute advantage in two or more goods produces one of them relatively more efficiently than the other. Both encourage countries to specialize in what they produce most efficiently.

The Heckscher–Ohlin (H–O) theory says comparative advantage comes from a nation's factor endowments: land, labor, capital and technology. Countries export goods that use their abundant factors. It assumes perfect competition and that factors of production cannot move between countries.

Remember it as HOARD → SPECIALIZE → USE WHAT YOU HAVE

Hoard is mercantilism, specialize is absolute and comparative advantage, and use what you have is H–O's land, labor, capital and technology.

Mock exam · Question A4 · 10 marks

The balance of payments in three accounts

ProblemDescribe the three accounts of the balance of payments and explain net errors and omissions.
Big ideaEach account records a different kind of transaction, and the gap left by imperfect data is net errors and omissions.
How (tools)The definition, the current, capital and financial accounts with the sign rule, and the formula for errors and omissions.
ResultA definition, one paragraph per account, and the gap explained at the end.

The question: Describe the three accounts of the balance of payments and explain what net errors and omissions are.

The logic behind the answer

  1. Define the whole firstThe balance of payments (BOP) shows all transactions between one country and the rest of the world in a given period. The three accounts are the three parts of that record.
  2. Current account: everyday trade and incomeIt shows what consumers and businesses do: trade in goods and services, primary income and secondary income. The sign rule explains a deficit: exports bring money in (+), imports send money out (−).
  3. Capital account: small assetsIt records non-produced nonfinancial assets and capital transfers, such as land sold to embassies or leases and licenses. The amounts are small, which is why the slides call it the least significant item.
  4. Financial account: investmentIt holds foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, financial derivatives and other investments such as hot money.
  5. Explain the gap lastIn practice the accounts do not balance, because the data are imperfect. The gap is net errors and omissions, so it can only be explained once the three accounts are known. It reflects timing and illegal trade.

Easy answer · about 190 words

Write this

The balance of payments (BOP) shows all transactions between one country and the rest of the world for a given period. It has three accounts.

The current account shows the activities of consumers and businesses. It has the trade balance in goods, the trade balance in services such as tourism, banking and software, the primary income balance (labor and investment income earned from abroad minus what is paid abroad) and secondary income (current transfers). Exports get a plus sign because money comes in, and imports a minus sign because money goes out, so importing more than exporting gives a goods trade deficit.

The capital account records non-produced nonfinancial assets and capital transfers, such as land sold to embassies or sales of leases and licenses. It is the least significant account because the amounts are usually small. The financial account covers foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, financial derivatives and other investments such as hot money and trade credit.

In practice the accounts do not balance, because the data are imperfect. The gap is net errors and omissions: the financial account balance minus the balance of the current and capital accounts. It reflects timing and illegal trade.

Remember it as TRADE · SMALL ASSETS · INVESTMENT · THE GAP

Current is trade and income, capital is small assets, financial is investment, and errors and omissions is the gap.

Mock exam · Question B1 · 20 marks

Why cultural adaptation decides success abroad

ProblemUse the eight elements of culture and the country notes to show that adapting to culture can decide success abroad.
Big ideaCulture is learned, so it differs by country; a firm that adjusts to it can succeed, and one that ignores it can fail.
How (tools)The definition of culture, the eight elements, at least three country notes and the slide on managing and marketing.
ResultA four-part answer: the claim, the checklist, the proof from three or more markets, and what it means for management and marketing.

The question: Using the eight elements of culture and the country notes from Session 4, explain why a company's degree of cultural adaptation can decide its success abroad. Illustrate with at least three of the markets the course describes, and explain how culture should shape the firm's management and marketing there.

The logic behind the answer

  1. State the claimCulture is learned behavior, so it is different in every country. The slides then say that how far a company adapts to a culture is often the difference between success and failure abroad. That sentence is the whole argument, so it comes first.
  2. Give the checklistThe eight elements of culture tell the firm what to look at: language, religion, values and attitudes, manners and customs, material elements, aesthetics, education and social institutions. Name all eight, each with a few words.
  3. Prove it with marketsThe country notes show the elements at work. Japan shows manners and customs, China's guanxi shows a social institution, Latin America shows values about work and time, and Arab countries show customs that can offend. The question needs at least three.
  4. Turn it into actionThe last slide of the session says where culture changes the firm's work: attitudes toward work, management styles, new products, advertising and communication styles, with Exhibit 5.4's jargon as the example.
  5. Close on the claimEnd by tying the markets and the management points back to the first sentence: adapting can decide success or failure.

Easy answer · about 320 words

Write this

Culture is learned behavior, the way of life of one group of people living in one community. Because it is learned, it is different in every country. The textbook says that the degree to which a company adapts to a culture is often the difference between success and failure in international markets. So a global firm must find out which parts of culture matter in each market and adjust its operations to them.

The eight elements of culture show what to check. Language is what a message says and its nonverbal signals, and backward translation checks a message. Religion affects products, holidays and working hours. Values are basic beliefs, and attitudes are feelings and opinions. Manners and customs are the usual way things are done. Material elements show a society's technology and infrastructure. Aesthetics is its taste in color, form and music. Education decides whether local staff can work or be trained. Social institutions are how people relate in groups.

The country notes show this. In Japan, a visitor gives a slight bow and a handshake, follows business card etiquette, avoids looking directly into the hosts' eyes, and must arrive prepared and able to make decisions. In China, guanxi is friendship among unequals built on exchanging favors, so a foreigner may need the favor of lower-ranked people to reach the decision makers. In Latin America, customers want a personal relationship first, are flexible about time and say, "We work to live." In Arab countries, showing the sole of your shoe or using the left hand can offend your hosts.

Culture also shapes management and marketing. It affects attitudes toward work, and management styles can create problems or lead to success. New products must take management styles and other parts of marketing into account, advertising must be tailored to local culture, and communication styles differ: U.S. jargon such as "reinvent the wheel" (Exhibit 5.4) would not be understood. This is why a company's cultural adaptation can decide its success or failure abroad.

Remember it as L · R · V · M · M · A · E · S

The eight elements in the slides' order: language, religion, values and attitudes, manners and customs, material elements, aesthetics, education, social institutions. One way to hold the letters is "Let Real Values Make Many Artists Even Smarter".

Choosing an entry strategy for a new market

ProblemAn inexperienced firm must choose how to enter a large emerging market, from export-import to a wholly owned subsidiary.
Big ideaMore risk can bring more return, so a firm with no experience starts at the low-risk end and moves up as it learns.
How (tools)Risk profile, the risk–return trade-off, Exhibit 8.1 and its three groups, and the economic and political risks of Session 5.
ResultA recommendation built on the course's own risk ladder, with a reason for every step.

The question: A mid-sized consumer electronics firm is considering entering a large emerging market where it has no experience. Using the course's entry strategies (Exhibit 8.1) and its discussion of political and economic risk, recommend how the firm should weigh its options, from export-import to a wholly owned subsidiary. (This scenario is invented for practice.)

The logic behind the answer

  1. Start with the rule that decidesThe choice depends on the firm's risk profile (the loss it is willing to take) and the risk–return trade-off (more risk, more possible reward). Risk comes from ownership, operation and asset transfer. Every later step is judged by this rule.
  2. Line up the optionsExhibit 8.1 puts the seven strategies on one line from low risk and low return to high risk and high return, in three groups: minimize risk, share risk, higher risk.
  3. Add the country's riskThe market itself is risky. Economic risks and political risks (micro or macro) add to the risk of the strategy, so the firm needs planning and, for U.S. firms, political risk insurance.
  4. Match the firm to the start of the lineThe firm has no experience, so the strategies that minimize financial risk fit first: export-import, licensing, franchising. Give one course reason for each.
  5. Show the next stepsIf the market proves attractive, the firm can share risk through an alliance or a joint venture, whose local partner knows the economy, culture and politics. Acquisitions and green field subsidiaries come last, because they need the most capital.
  6. RecommendStart at the low-risk end and move along Exhibit 8.1 as the firm learns and its risk profile allows.

Easy answer · about 340 words

Write this

The choice depends on the firm's risk profile, the financial loss its owners are willing to take, and on the risk–return trade-off: the more capital a firm is willing to risk, the greater the reward it may get. Risk comes from ownership, operation and asset transfer. Exhibit 8.1 lines up the entry strategies from low risk and low return to high risk and high return: export-import, licensing, franchising, strategic alliances, joint ventures, foreign acquisitions and wholly owned subsidiaries.

The country adds risk too. Economic risks include limits on moving or exchanging the currency, devaluation, doubtful loan repayment, labor problems, inflation and corruption. Political risk can hit one industry (micropolitical) or all businesses (macropolitical). Careful planning reduces these risks, and U.S. firms can buy political risk insurance from the U.S. International Development Finance Corporation (DFC) in countries that have a bilateral investment treaty with the United States.

Because the firm has no experience, it should begin with the strategies that minimize financial risk. Export-import ties up little capital and is easy to enter and leave, and trade finance limits the risk. Licensing lets a local partner make the product with lower transport, labor and material costs, without the firm building a plant, and penalty clauses give an exit. Franchising carries more risk than licensing, but local owners use local capital, so the firm enters without a big investment; it must keep quality the same everywhere.

If the market proves attractive, the firm can share risk. A strategic alliance earns revenue through cooperation without a jointly owned company, but a partner may quit early. A joint venture with a local firm shares commercial risk, and the local partner knows the economy, culture and politics and can handle logistics and bureaucracy.

The higher-risk strategies come last. An acquisition gives instant access to existing production, marketing and distribution, while a wholly owned "green field" subsidiary needs large capital and heavy marketing as a newcomer. So the firm should start at the low-risk end and move along Exhibit 8.1 as it learns.

Remember it as START LOW, MOVE RIGHT

Start low is the three strategies that minimize risk, and move right is the walk along Exhibit 8.1 toward sharing risk and then taking more of it.

Scandals, controls and the law

ProblemShow how failures of ethics damage markets, and judge what internal controls, the FCPA and the Sarbanes–Oxley Act do about it.
Big ideaMarkets run on trust; the scandals broke it with false numbers, and the course answers with controls, laws and ethics education.
How (tools)The definition of ethics, four scandals, preventive and feedback controls, the two parts of the FCPA and three rules of SOX.
ResultAn answer that moves from the damage, to the cases, to the three defenses.

The question: Using the financial scandals covered in Session 6, explain how failures of ethics damage markets, and evaluate the role of internal controls, the FCPA and the Sarbanes–Oxley Act in preventing such failures.

The logic behind the answer

  1. Say why ethics matters to marketsEthical business needs mutual trust, fair dealing and honest communication, and the slides say unethical behavior destroys fair investment markets. That link is the "how" the question asks for.
  2. Prove it with the scandalsEnron, WorldCom, Vivendi and Parmalat each hid the truth about their finances. Give each one what it hid and how it ended, so the damage is concrete: WorldCom's bankruptcy wiped out its stockholders and bondholders.
  3. First defense: internal controlsControls keep financial information accurate. Preventive controls stop problems before they happen; feedback controls find them afterwards. WorldCom's fraud was found by its internal auditors.
  4. Second defense: the lawThe Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) bans bribery and makes firms keep internal controls over their books. After Enron, the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (SOX) of 2002 raised prison sentences and made top managers sign for the accounts.
  5. Third defense: ethics educationSession 6 also teaches that ethics can be taught, through five fundamentals. Ending here completes the evaluation: controls find fraud, laws punish it, and education builds people who know what is right.

Easy answer · about 350 words

Write this

Ethics is the branch of philosophy about the rightness and wrongness of actions and the goodness and badness of their intent and results. Ethical business needs mutual trust, fair dealing and honest communication. Unethical behavior, such as using inside information, destroys fair investment markets.

The scandals show this. Enron used misleading accounting, including mark-to-market practices, to hide unprofitable operations. WorldCom recorded line costs as assets instead of expenses and overstated revenues to show false growth; its bankruptcy wiped out its stockholders and bondholders. Vivendi hid cash flow and debt problems to raise its stock price and make acquisitions; its chief executive had to resign and pay a $1 million fine, and the company paid $50 million. Parmalat used risky derivatives and bought its own debt to hide losses, then went bankrupt. In each case investors were given false financial information.

Internal controls are rules and procedures that keep financial information accurate and reliable. Preventive controls stop problems before they happen: hiring competent and ethical staff, following written policies, securing assets and keeping good records. Feedback controls, such as quality control and internal auditing, find problems afterwards so they can be corrected. WorldCom's fraud was found by its internal auditors.

The law adds pressure. The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) applies to U.S. firms and foreign firms doing business in the United States, and has two parts: a ban on bribery and a duty to keep adequate internal controls over books and records. After Enron, the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (SOX) of 2002 raised prison sentences for fraud to 25 years, set 20 years for destroying or faking records, and made the chief executive officer and chief financial officer certify that the financial statements are accurate.

Session 6 also teaches that ethics can be taught, through personal integrity, the responsibility of business in society, ethical decision making, ethical leadership and corporate governance. So the course gives three defenses: controls that prevent and find fraud, laws that punish it and make top managers responsible, and education that builds integrity, so that people know what is right and have the courage to do it.

Remember it as TRUST BROKEN → CONTROLS · LAW · ETHICS

Trust broken is the scandals; controls, law and ethics are the three defenses, in the order the answer gives them.
