Two open-access papers, told so that each can be understood on its own. Paper 1 zooms in: a single experiment showing why adding a scapegoat to an apology backfires. In plain words

Paper 2 describes the habits of the field, and Paper 1 shows those habits at work. Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT) is the theory most articles in the field apply, and Paper 1 builds on it. Experiments are a growing share of quantitative work, and Paper 1 is one. The field calls for new theory, and Paper 1 adds the Theory of Communicative Responsibility (CRT) to SCCT. Only one pattern runs the other way: 71% of the articles come from authors at institutions in the United States, while Paper 1 studies South Korean adults and so answers the non-Western gap.

The order of reading is a free choice. Paper 2 comes first for readers who want the context, because it shows who studies crisis communication, how, and with which theories. Paper 1 comes first for readers who want a concrete example of how SCCT research is done. Each paper has its own tab, which opens with the whole article told as one story. A finding that links them

Paper 2 even cites a related finding (Zhou et al., 2022): an apology with corrective action works better than an apology alone, and blaming an insider hurts more than blaming an outsider. The scapegoat in Paper 1 was an outside subcontractor, the milder kind, and participants still punished it.

In plain words

The two papers answer different questions, so they are used for different jobs. Paper 1 provides evidence on apology design and culture. Paper 2 provides background, guidance on theory choice and a list of research gaps. Together they show one mechanism in detail and the wider field in which that mechanism was tested.

Their lessons also fit together. Paper 2 finds that transparency, words that match actions and fast disclosure recur across the field. Paper 1 shows one precise way an organisation can fail that standard: an apology that says one thing while the next sentence says another.

Five takeaways

  1. Consistency beats a bare “sorry”. A rebuild apology plus a deny move reads as insincere. Pair an apology with action (compensation, correction), not with blame.
  2. Two responsibilities, not one. Even a company that did not cause the crisis may still be expected to explain it. That expectation decides how harshly its message is judged.
  3. Reputation is won through perception of the message. In Paper 1, apology type had no significant direct effect on reputation; everything ran through whether the apology seemed appropriate.
  4. SCCT is the default lens, applied in 92 articles in Public Relations Review, but the field explicitly wants extensions (like CRT) and non-Western evidence (like Korea).
  5. The practice consensus: prepare before, be transparent and fast during, tell a human story, and watch social media constantly.

An open question

Would an Indonesian company’s mohon maaf (a formal “we apologise”) plus blaming a supplier land the same way? This remains open. The mechanism in Paper 1, the norm of collective responsibility and a high expectation that the company explains, may transfer to other collectivist cultures, but the authors tested only Korea and call for cross-cultural replications.
