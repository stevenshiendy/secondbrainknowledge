Paper 2 · Meta-research · Public Relations Review

24 years of crisis communication research

Elgueta-Ruiz, Á., Sadi, G., Gonçalves, G., & Martínez-Ortiz, J. (2026). A review of every crisis communication article in one journal from 2000 to 2023. The first chapter tells the whole article as one story; the chapters after it go through the map section by section.

Paper 2 · The whole article

The article as one story, and how to read its counts

ProblemDespite the growing literature, a systematic approach to crisis communication research is still limited.
Big ideaAnalyse all articles on the topic in the leading journal of the field.
How (tools)A qualitative and quantitative content analysis of 391 of the 1,906 articles published from 2000 to 2023.
ResultAuthors, universities, networks, objectives, methods, theories, results and conclusions, with their theoretical and practical implications.
In plain words

Paper 2 is research about research. Instead of testing a message on people, the authors read every crisis communication article published in one journal over 24 years and recorded what each one did: who wrote it, how it was done, which theory it used and what it concluded. The result is a map of a field’s habits.

The map tells a simple story. Crisis communication has grown into the most frequent topic in the journal. The work is centred on the United States, leans on two theories and is slowly widening toward social media and new models. Read across all these articles, the conclusions come down to four pillars. The authors themselves note that the focus on one journal restricts how far the results generalise to the broader field.

The story in five steps

  1. The questionWho studies crisis communication, how, with which theories, and what has the field concluded?
  2. The corpus391 crisis articles out of 1,906 in Public Relations Review (PRR), 2000–2023, each coded by hand.
  3. The profileA booming, United States-centred field, with quantitative work in the lead and experiments rising.
  4. The theoriesSituational Crisis Communication Theory and Image Repair Theory dominate, while alternatives gain ground.
  5. The advicePreparedness, transparency, narrative and digital agility.

How the counts nest

Each count in the article sits inside a larger one. The paper reports the 66 experiments in two ways: as 16.87% of all articles, and as more than one-third of all quantitative studies.

What the authors say, and their reasoning

What they sayWhy they say it
Crisis communication research is largely an “American enterprise”Authors from 29 countries appear, but United States academics contribute over 70% of the manuscripts, mostly with fellow nationals. They take this as confirming Xifra’s (2003) assertion about public relations.
The rise of experiments signals methodological maturationMore than one-third of quantitative studies are experiments, which reflects a shift toward hypothesis-driven, causally oriented research able to isolate how crisis messages work.
There is still room to develop new knowledgeAssuming that synthesis and evaluation objectives contribute most to advancing knowledge, only one in four objectives reaches them, and six in ten objectives are descriptive. Against this, more than three quarters of those descriptive objectives are of medium-high or high complexity.
The search for new crisis theories remains low-profileFew articles are theoretical only, and the data show a tendency to use recognised theories as lenses for practical cases. The larger set of concepts that could not be classified also suggests broad interest and a more diverse future.

Where the authors say research should go

  • Beyond one journal: use the framework across a broader range of publications to improve representativeness.
  • Beyond communication alone: integrate crisis management to give a more holistic understanding of organisational crises.
  • Across cultures and platforms: investigate strategies in diverse cultural contexts, and the distinct characteristics of social media platforms.
  • Toward prescription: move from primarily descriptive research to more interpretive and prescriptive studies, including predictive models and artificial intelligence.

Paper 2 · Study and growth

One journal, 24 years: the study and the growth it found

ProblemSince very little comparable systematic review has been done in the past 25 years, the authors set out to document all the published studies in one journal.
Big ideaCode every crisis article in the longest-standing public relations journal.
How (tools)A systematic content analysis of 391 articles on 16 categories.
ResultCrisis communication is the most frequently studied topic in the journal.
In plain words

Paper 2 is a systematic content analysis of every crisis communication article in Public Relations Review (PRR), the oldest public relations journal. Out of 1,906 articles published from 2000 to 2023, 391 dealt with crises once editorials and reviews were removed. Each article was hand-coded on 16 categories in Excel, then re-checked by two analysts.

The first finding is simple growth. Crisis communication is the most frequent topic in the journal at 20.51% of all articles, ahead of social media at 17.94%. Output climbed from a handful of articles a year to 30 a year in 2021 and 2022. In 2020 and 2022, more than a third of everything PRR published was about crises.

Three research questions

  • RQ1, the profile of the field: authors, countries, universities, networks, topics, objectives, research questions, hypotheses, methods, techniques and research type.
  • RQ2, beyond the big two: do the theories used go beyond Benoit’s Image Repair Theory and Coombs’ Situational Crisis Communication Theory?
  • RQ3, what should be done: a qualitative reading of results and conclusions to give prescriptive advice, as Avery et al. (2010) urged.

Paper 2 · Authors

Who writes it: mostly Americans, mostly in small teams

ProblemThe first research question asks about the main authors, countries, universities and networks.
Big ideaCount countries, authors and collaboration types across all 391 articles.
How (tools)Coding the institution of each author and the type of each collaboration.
ResultAn “American enterprise”: 71.35% from the United States, and only 14% crossing borders.
In plain words

Twenty-nine countries appear in the corpus, but one dominates. Institutions in the United States produced 279 of the 391 articles, or 71.35%. South Korea and Hong Kong follow with 20 each. Asian output is often co-authored with United States partners, in 24 of 33 articles. The University of Maryland leads the institutions with 26 articles, followed by Georgia, Missouri and Tennessee with 16 each.

Most work comes from teams, but those teams stay close to home. Single authors wrote 113 articles, colleagues at the same university 120, and colleagues at different universities in one country 103. Only 55 articles, 14%, are international collaborations. The authors read this as an “American enterprise” with local, inward-looking networks. This is the gap a Korean study such as Paper 1 begins to fill.

Paper 2 · Methods

How the field studies crises: methods and ambition

ProblemThe first research question also asks about objectives, research questions, hypotheses, methods and techniques.
Big ideaClassify each article by method, technique and the level of its objectives.
How (tools)Method counts, technique counts and Bloom’s taxonomy of objectives.
ResultQuantitative work leads and experiments are growing, yet few objectives reach synthesis or evaluation.
In plain words

Quantitative work leads with 180 articles, 46% of the corpus. Experiments make up 66 of those, which is 16.87% of all articles and over a third of quantitative work, and their share is growing. Qualitative research follows with 110 articles, theory and literature work with 53, and mixed methods with 48. Content analysis is the most common technique at 161 articles, ahead of surveys at 133, and an article can use several.

Bloom’s taxonomy sorts learning or research goals into six levels, from knowledge up to evaluation. Applied to 844 stated objectives, it shows a field centred on analysis at 38.15%. Only about one objective in four reaches synthesis or evaluation, the levels that build new knowledge. By type, 59% of objectives are descriptive, 21% correlational and 19% causal.

Paper 2 · Theories

The theory landscape: the big two and the alternatives

ProblemThe second research question asks whether the theories applied go beyond the two dominant ones.
Big ideaSeparate using a theory from naming it or merely citing its author.
How (tools)Three codes for each theory: applied or explained, only named, author cited.
ResultSCCT is applied in 92 articles, almost twice Image Repair Theory, and alternatives are gaining ground.
In plain words

The authors separate using a theory, by explaining or testing it, from merely naming it or citing its author. That distinction shows how deep the dominance really is. Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT), from Coombs, is applied in 92 articles, named in 50, and its author is cited without the theory in a further 142. Image Repair Theory (IRT), from Benoit, is applied in 47, named in 55, and its author cited without the theory in 57.

Around these two sit general public relations theories, with 149 uses in total, and a long list of theories borrowed from other fields, with 553 “other” uses. Of those, 180 could not be classified at all. The authors read this larger set of concepts as a sign of broad interest and a more diverse future for the research.

Paper 2 · Findings

What 24 years of findings add up to: four pillars

ProblemAvery et al. (2010) urged the field to become more prescriptive, and the third research question follows that suggestion.
Big ideaRead the results and conclusions together, then draw prescriptive advice from them.
How (tools)A qualitative reading of the results and conclusions of the articles.
ResultFour pillars: preparedness, transparency, narrative and digital agility.
In plain words

Four themes recur across the results. More than 60% of articles report that crisis plans reduce harm, about 50% that transparency restores trust, more than 45% that social media amplifies the crisis, and about 40% that human narratives rebuild image. These shares are approximate, because the paper gives them without a coding table.

The conclusions turn these themes into four pillars. Prepare with plans, trained teams, set spokespersons and simulations. Disclose quickly and accurately, keeping words in line with actions, because evasion costs more later. Humanise the organisation through testimonies, empathy and a proactive tone. Monitor online talk and answer rumours quickly, with prediction and artificial intelligence (AI) as the future direction.

Paper 2 · Analysis

Strengths, limits and numbers to read carefully

ProblemThe authors acknowledge limits: the focus on one journal and the absence of an inter-rater reliability assessment.
Big ideaA census of one journal maps that journal, and printed figures need reading carefully.
How (tools)The limits the authors state, and the checks recorded in the study notes.
ResultUse it for background, theory choice and research gaps.
In plain words

As the study notes read it, the strength of Paper 2 is that it is a census of 24 years rather than a sample. Its coding is also rich: objectives by two taxonomies, hypotheses by two, and theory use separated from theory mention. The notes add that it answers a real question for students, namely which theory to pick and what is under-studied, and that it is recent and open access under a Creative Commons licence (CC BY).

The limits are these. One journal shows the taste of Public Relations Review (PRR) and not the whole field. No inter-rater reliability statistic is reported, such as Cohen’s Kappa, which would show that independent coders agree; disagreements were settled by discussion. And by focusing on organisational crises, the paper excludes disaster and public-health crisis research by design. The authors also leave out crisis management, as a broader managerial field, on purpose.

Read the numbers carefully

  • Corpus size: 391 in the abstract and methods, but 386 in the introduction.
  • Team-authored articles: 278 of 391 is 71.1%, but the text says 70.09%. Similarly, Fig. 3 prints 35.4% where the text says 35.04%.
  • “SCCT n = 142”: this appears as the count for Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT) in one place, and as the count of Coombs citations without the theory in another. The safer figure for “SCCT used” is 92.
  • Result themes: percentages such as “more than 60%” and “about 50%” come without a coding table, so they are approximate.
  • Annex 1: the full list of crisis theories, models and stages is in an annex that is not in the PDF.
Do not quote 142 as SCCT use: 142 counts articles citing Coombs without using the theory. The paper separates using the theory (92), merely naming it (50) and citing the author only (142).
