How the export/import structure grows

There is no single correct organization chart. The slides give three variables that decide what the structure looks like: company size, trade volume, and which tasks are done in-house versus outsourced.

Division of labour: as volume rises, it becomes cheaper to have specialists who handle one task many times than generalists who handle many tasks once.

The logicThe same personnel can also hold other roles in the company (for example, a purchasing officer who also files import entries). Starting an import/export business, and the import cycle

Part of the deck uses an Indian example (an online guide on starting an import/export business). The steps are general; only the agency names are Indian.

Step zero: create the legal identity

Before any trade, the business needs (1) a brand name or legal entity, kept separate from other ongoing businesses to make customs procedures easier, and (2) a government registration code that identifies it as a trader. In the Indian example this is the IEC (Import Export Code), which requires a tax number (PAN (Permanent Account Number)) first.

Indonesian equivalent (added context, not on the slides): the business identity number is the NIB (Nomor Induk Berusaha), issued through the OSS (Online Single Submission) system, and the tax number is the NPWP (Nomor Pokok Wajib Pajak, Taxpayer Identification Number). The ordering logic is the same: tax identity → business identity → trading permissions. The Indian "CHA" (Customs House Agent) corresponds to a licensed customs broker, in Indonesia a PPJK (Pengusaha Pengurusan Jasa Kepabeanan, licensed customs broker).

Export order processing: four flows, one pattern

Bade's textbook breaks export order processing into four stages. Every stage follows the same hub pattern: the export department receives something, sends it out to the departments that hold the needed information, then consolidates and passes it on.

Abbreviations in this figure: L/C Letter of Credit

The logic behind the sequence
  • Quote before order: the quotation (or pro forma invoice) fixes price and terms; the purchase order is then checked against it ("match to quotation"). Without a quote, there is nothing to verify the order against.
  • Finance checks the L/C before shipment: once goods leave, the exporter loses its leverage. Confirming the buyer's letter of credit is opened is how the exporter makes sure it will be paid before giving up control of the goods.
  • Collection documents are prepared at shipment, used at collection: stage ③ creates what stage ④ needs.

Recordkeeping compliance and the penalty formula

Exporters and importers have always had to keep records of their trade transactions, but these obligations are now mandatory by law. Electronic documents (emails, web purchases, online confirmations, e-invoices) make it more important to show that the customer actually accepted the terms, for example by written email confirmation or a required click-through link before the order completes. Agencies have moved to electronic filing, but that does not remove the duty to keep hard-copy records.

Layers of law: which law governs what

The textbook (U.S. setting) separates two questions: may these goods cross the border? and is this sales contract valid? Different levels of law answer them.

Plus two outer layers named in the chapter title: international law (treaties such as the WTO (World Trade Organization) agreement and trade agreements) and foreign law (the importing country's own rules, which the exporter must also satisfy).

Exam trapA single transaction is governed by several layers at once. Example: an Indonesian exporter selling to a U.S. buyer must comply with Indonesian export rules, the contract terms (governed by whichever contract law the parties chose or that applies), U.S. import rules (foreign law from the exporter's view), and WTO commitments that shaped both countries' rules.

What "export" means legally

Definition from the slides: export is the activity of releasing goods from the customs area. The definition is about crossing a legal boundary, not about selling to a foreigner.

Abbreviations in this figure: EEZ Exclusive Economic Zone (Zona Ekonomi Eksklusif, ZEE)

The port is the legally defined place (land plus surrounding waters with set boundaries) where ships dock and goods are loaded, with safety facilities, so it is where the customs release normally happens.

The logicBecause export is defined as a release from the customs area, the customs export declaration (Pemberitahuan Pabean Ekspor, PEB) is the legal proof of when an export happened. Permendag 11/2024 uses exactly this: mineral concentrates "may only be exported until 31 December 2024 ... as proven by the number and registration date of the customs export declaration". The date of the sale contract does not matter; the date of the customs release does.

Why every export sale should be in writing

Sales come in two kinds: isolated transactions (one-off orders) and ongoing transactions (a continuing relationship). For both, the sales agreement is the most important document in the deal. The slides give three reasons it must be written:

1 · Legal enforceability

Under the U.S. Uniform Commercial Code, a sale above US$500 is enforceable by the seller (so it can claim payment) only if it is in writing. There are exceptions, but writing is the safe practice.

2 · Memory fails

"The thinnest ink is better than the brightest memory." Disputes start when parties did not record, or never discussed, an issue.

3 · Checklist and record

A written agreement reminds both sides what to agree on and records what they agreed. Modifications must also be in writing.

Verbal deals still happen (commodity trading by phone, orders taken at trade shows or meetings). Online selling has made formal agreements less common, which raises the risk of misunderstanding. The seller should also check for conflicting terms between its own form and the buyer's form.

Email ordersAn email order and acceptance can satisfy the "in writing" rule, but it usually contains only quantity, price, and maybe a delivery date. That leaves the seller unprotected on everything else. Fix: the seller's acceptance must state expressly that the sale is subject to the seller's standard terms and conditions, and attach them, with the buyer's acknowledgment, so the buyer cannot say it never saw them.

Agent asks for a special discountan unusual allowancean extra commissionpayment to someone other than the agent→exporter is charged with knowledge
The logicExtra money that has no business reason has to go somewhere. The law treats the exporter as knowing because a careful exporter would have asked where. That is why the agreement should include an FCPA compliance clause.

Terms of purchase: who carries cost, risk and legal duties

Four things must be agreed in every import purchase: passage of title, risk of loss, price, and payment. The buyer should set its purchasing policy in advance rather than accept whatever each seller offers. Abbreviations in this figure: FOB Free On Board · CIF Cost, Insurance and Freight

Bade's advice: to make the seller responsible for its own country's export laws, the buyer should usually not buy ex-factory, but FOB port of export, CIF, or landed. If the seller fails to obtain an export permit, the buyer suffers anyway: lawsuits, failed delivery, lost supply.

Translation note: slides 20–21 of deck 07 say "used goods". In Bade's original text this is ex-factory (ex-works), the term where the seller's responsibility and risk are minimal. Read "used goods" on those slides as "ex-factory".
FOB and CIF in one line eachFOB (Free On Board, named port of shipment): seller delivers once goods are on board the ship; cost and risk pass to the buyer there. CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight, named port of destination): same delivery and risk point, but the seller also pays freight and minimum insurance (ICC (C), the Institute Cargo Clauses minimum cover, at 110% of the value) to the destination port. Both are sea-only terms; "FOB plant" on slide 19 is an older American usage, not Incoterms (International Commercial Terms) FOB. When the buyer may prefer to do more itself (slide 23): the seller is inexperienced in international shipping; competitors buy ex-factory, so the buyer must match their cost; the buyer is buying from an affiliate; the buyer already has warehouse-to-warehouse marine insurance under a blanket policy; or the buyer can get cheaper ocean freight or insurance than the seller. Three levels: “Treaty, Law, Land”

Quiz: what each regulation does, its exact title, and how it changed

This quiz drills the three things exam questions on regulations usually test: what a regulation covers, its exact official title, and its amendment history (which later rule changed it, and what it changed). Questions are drawn at random from the 44 regulations in Part K. Every answer shows the full card, so a wrong answer still teaches you the rule.

How Indonesian titles are built Learn these six patterns and most titles become guessable.
  • Base rule: type + Nomor + Tahun + tentang + subject: Undang-Undang Nomor 7 Tahun 2014 tentang Perdagangan.
  • Amendments: Perubahan atas (1st), Perubahan Kedua atas (2nd), Ketiga (3rd), Keempat (4th), Kelima (5th), followed by the full title of the rule being changed. Example: Permendag 9/2025 is Perubahan Ketiga atas Peraturan Menteri Perdagangan Nomor 23 Tahun 2023 tentang Kebijakan dan Pengaturan Ekspor.
  • jo. (juncto, “read together with”) joins a rule and its amendment: UU 10/1995 jo. UU 17/2006.
  • Replacement (mencabut, revoke): the new rule often keeps the same subject in its title, so only the number changes. PP 5/2021 → PP 28/2025 (risk-based licensing); Permendag 36/2023 → 16/2025 (import policy).
  • Treaties: Pengesahan (ratification) + the treaty’s name: UU 24/2022 tentang Pengesahan Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement.
  • Emergency law made permanent: Penetapan Peraturan Pemerintah Pengganti Undang-Undang … menjadi Undang-Undang (UU 6/2023). PMK numbers carry the unit: PMK.04 customs, PMK.010 fiscal policy (tariffs and duties).

PART I · SYNTHESIS ACROSS THE COURSE

Exam-ready reasoning: the chains to remember

1 · Compliance → profit

Specialized staff prevent fines, delays and seizures; that is why a compliance-oriented program is called profitable.

2 · Volume → specialization

One person in a small firm; separate export and import departments as volume and insourcing grow.

3 · Export orders differ

Delivery, payment security, insurance, packing all change, so many departments must give input, so someone must coordinate.

4 · Hub pattern

In quotation, order entry, shipment and collection, the export department collects input, consolidates and passes it on.

5 · Design decides duty

Classification, duty, FTA eligibility, valuation and origin follow from how a product is made, so import compliance starts at design.

6 · Intent decides the fine

Missing records: intentional = lesser of $100k or 75%; negligent = lesser of $10k or 40%.

7 · Federal border, state contract

Crossing the border is national law; the sale contract is state (private) law. Add international and foreign law on top.

8 · Risk decides the permit

Export = release from the customs area. Free → regulated → supervised → prohibited; regulated goods need ET (who), PE (how much), LS (verify the cargo).

9 · Product + volume decide procedure

Component, spare, finished, set, modified, used: these decide documents and foreign customs treatment. Bigger volume → formal agreement, secure payment, agents.

10 · Four tests of export control

What, Who, Why, Where. Fail one and the deal is blocked. Red flags are things that "do not fit" a normal buyer.

11 · Writing protects the seller

UCC (Uniform Commercial Code): sales over US$500 enforceable by the seller only in writing. Email orders need the seller's T&Cs (Terms and Conditions) attached.

12 · Control when the contract forms

Price list "not an offer"; check RFQ (Request for Quotation) fine print; cost sheet before quoting; no agreement until the seller accepts the PO. Conduct accepts all the offer's terms.

13 · Ownership splits distributor and agent

Distributor buys, owns, bears resale risk, sets its own price. Agent only solicits orders. FCPA: the exporter is liable if it knew of bribes.

14 · Delegate work, not liability

Exporter is responsible for the forwarder's errors. Certificate of origin ≠ origin label. DG (Dangerous Goods), precursor and health documents protect carriers and destination countries.

15 · EAR in order

Scope → other agency → de minimis (10%/25%) → ECCN + Country Chart → 10 prohibitions → exception → licence. NLR is declared under penalty of perjury.

16 · Import mirrors export

Sourcing, supplier vetting, labelling, terms of purchase. Buy FOB/CIF/landed to make the seller handle its own export law. Buying commission not dutiable.

FOB and CIF

Two of the most common trade terms in sea freight, both defined in Incoterms (International Commercial Terms), the rulebook of trade terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC). Both answer the same three questions: where the seller has delivered, who pays for each leg of the journey, and who bears the loss if the goods are damaged on the way. The diagrams below follow one shipment from the seller's factory to the buyer's warehouse.

FOB
Free On Board · named port of shipment

The seller delivers when the goods are loaded on board the vessel nominated by the buyer at the named port of shipment, cleared for export. From that moment the buyer bears all risk and pays all further costs, including ocean freight and insurance.

CIF
Cost, Insurance and Freight · named port of destination

The seller delivers the same way, on board at the port of shipment, but must also pay the freight and buy minimum insurance to the named port of destination. Risk still passes to the buyer at loading, even though the seller has paid for the voyage.

Test the logic: four what-ifs

Crane drops the container while loading

If it falls before the goods are on board: seller's loss under both FOB and CIF. Delivery is not yet complete.

Ship sinks mid-ocean

Buyer's loss under both. Under FOB the buyer recovers only if it bought its own insurance. Under CIF it claims on the policy the seller bought.

Freight rates double after the contract

FOB: the buyer pays the higher rate. CIF: the seller absorbs it, because the CIF price was fixed including freight.

Seller cannot get an export permit

Seller's problem under both, because export clearance is the seller's task. This is why Week 7 advises buying FOB port of export or CIF rather than ex-factory.

Assignment · Export law and the GATT

Permendag No. 11 of 2024 and the GATT ban on quantitative export restrictions

The questions
  1. What is discussed in the Decree of the Minister of Trade No. 11 of 2024 concerning Export Policies and Regulations? Provide a case example.
  2. Discuss how the provisions of the Minister of Trade Decree No. 11 of 2024 concerning Export Policies and Regulations are viewed from the perspective of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT-WTO), particularly the article prohibiting quantitative export restrictions. Provide an example.

Answer

When national rice stocks dwindled due to adverse weather conditions, the Indonesian government temporarily suspended the issuance of Export Approvals (PE) for organic rice to partner countries. Although importing nations argued that this violated Article XI:1 of the GATT, Indonesia could legally justify the policy under WTO rules, specifically Article XI:2(a) of the GATT, as a measure to secure domestic food supplies.

  • Ministry of Trade Regulation provisions: rice exports (encompassing general rice, organic rice, and specialty varieties such as Basmati or Hom Mali) must align with the national commodity balance and meet established criteria regarding broken grain content.
  • GATT-WTO perspective: restrictions or suspensions of rice export quotas could potentially be deemed a violation of Article XI:1 of the GATT (quantitative restrictions). However, Indonesia can justify such measures under Article XI:2(a) of the GATT, which provides an exception for preventing or alleviating critical shortages of essential foodstuffs in the interest of national food security.
Assignment · Export documents (study case)

Bridging the e-SKA and the Dangerous Goods Declaration so a shipment avoids the Red Channel

The case question

How can exporters bridge the naming difference between the Ministry of Trade's e-SKA (based on HS Code) and the DGD (based on transport safety rules) so it does not trigger a Red Channel at Customs, and what solution do Indonesian regulations support?

1. The problem

  • e-SKA: a trade document proving Indonesian origin for tariff preferences. Goods must be described according to the invoice and HS Code.
  • DGD: a transport safety document. Goods must be described by Proper Shipping Name (PSN) and UN Number.
  • Conflict: the same product has two official names, for example "Solvent-based wall paint, HS 3208" on the e-SKA and "PAINT, UN1263, Class 3" on the DGD. When data is synchronized in INSW, this looks like a mismatch and the documents are held.

2. Legal basis

  • Certificate of Origin rules: Minister of Trade Regulation No. 24 of 2018, as last amended by No. 39 of 2020, governs SKA issuance. Goods descriptions follow the invoice and HS Code. Note: Permendag No. 24 of 2021 regulates distributor and agent agreements, not e-SKA or HS Codes.
  • Law No. 17 of 2008 on Shipping, Articles 44 to 48: dangerous goods must be transported according to regulations, classified by class (e.g. Class 3 flammable liquids), packed and labelled according to the IMDG Code, and reported to the Harbor Master.
  • Minister of Transportation Regulation PM 90 of 2013 (air): packages must be marked with the PSN and UN Number (point 6.3), and the shipper's declaration must be signed by licensed personnel and written in English and Indonesian (points 7.1 and 7.2).
Update since submission

The course study pack (Part J of the walkthrough) records two later changes to the rules cited above:

  • Permendag 24/2018 is still the base rule for issuing the SKA, but it was amended again after 39/2020: Permendag 34/2023 is its fourth amendment. See The Certificate of Origin: Permendag 24/2018 and 39/2020.
  • PM 90/2013 was revoked and replaced by PM 32/2022 on 12 October 2022. The shipper's duties continue under the new number. See UU 17/2008 on Shipping and PM 90/2013 on air cargo.

Both naming standards are legally required, so neither can be replaced by the other.

3. Recommended solution

  1. Keep both names. The e-SKA uses the trade description and HS Code. The DGD uses the PSN and UN Number.
  2. Use the invoice and packing list as the bridge. Write both identities in one line, e.g. "Solvent-based wall paint, HS 3208.20, PAINT, UN1263, Class 3, PG II".
  3. Keep the HS Code consistent across the invoice, export declaration (PEB), and e-SKA, along with matching quantities and weights.
  4. Cross-reference documents by listing the same invoice and B/L or AWB numbers on the DGD.
  5. Attach the Safety Data Sheet (SDS) showing both the trade name and the PSN/UN Number.

4. Supporting case: risk of non-compliance

The Saintara article (Fakhrurrozi & Widodo, 2022) shows how strictly dangerous goods documentation is enforced.

  • Permit procedure: the shipping agent applies to the Head of KSOP with a B/L copy, origin-of-goods letter, manifest, stowage plan, and crew list. The LALA section checks and approves it, and KPLP officers supervise the operation. Each permit covers one activity.
  • Law: Article 216(1) of Law 17/2008 requires Harbor Master approval for handling dangerous goods. Under Article 322, a ship's captain who violates this faces up to 6 months in prison or a fine of up to Rp100 million.
  • Court ruling No. 159/Pid.Sus/2020/PN Tgg: the captain was fined Rp10,000,000 (or 3 months' confinement if unpaid) plus Rp2,000 in court costs.

Errors in dangerous goods documents can lead to held shipments and legal sanctions, so each document must be correct for its own purpose.

Conclusion

The best solution is not to force the e-SKA and DGD to use the same product name. The e-SKA keeps the trade description and HS Code under the Ministry of Trade's origin rules, while the DGD keeps the PSN and UN Number under Law No. 17 of 2008 and PM 90 of 2013. The two are linked through an invoice and packing list showing both identities, consistent HS Codes, cross-referenced document numbers, and an SDS.

This shows Customs that the difference comes from each document's function, not from any inconsistency in the goods, which reduces the risk of document holds and further inspection as long as all information is accurate and verifiable.

Assignment · Export controls (case study)

The extraterritorial reach of the U.S. Export Administration Regulations (EAR) and its implications for Indonesia

Question 1

The EAR is a domestic law of the United States, but it can apply extraterritorially. Explain what is meant by extraterritorial and why the EAR can reach Indonesian jurisdiction.

The Export Administration Regulations (EAR) are U.S. regulations issued and administered by the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) of the U.S. Department of Commerce. Their legal basis is the Export Control Reform Act of 2018 (ECRA), and they are codified in Title 15 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Parts 730 to 774 (Export Administration Regulations, 2026; Export Control Reform Act of 2018, 2018). The EAR control the export, re-export, and in-country transfer of commercial and dual-use goods, software, and technology.

1.1 Meaning of "extraterritorial"

A law is extraterritorial when a country applies it to persons, property, or activities located outside its own national territory. Normally, U.S. domestic law applies only within the United States. The EAR are different because they also apply to certain transactions that take place entirely in other countries, as long as the item involved is "subject to the EAR."

1.2 Why the EAR can reach Indonesia

The key principle is that the EAR follow the item, not the nationality or location of the company. According to the course material, the first step in any export analysis is to determine whether an item is "subject to" the EAR, and that scope is very broad (Bade, 2015; Karim, 2025). Under 15 C.F.R. § 734.3, items subject to the EAR include:

  • Items located in the United States, including items in U.S. foreign trade zones or moving in transit through the United States.
  • U.S.-origin items wherever located. A U.S.-made server or machine remains subject to the EAR after it arrives in Indonesia, so selling it on to a third country is a "re-export" that may require authorization.
  • Foreign-made items with U.S. content above the de minimis level. A product made in Indonesia becomes subject to the EAR if its controlled U.S.-origin content exceeds the permitted threshold. The threshold is 25 percent for most countries, including Indonesia, and 10 percent for embargoed destinations (Karim, 2025).
  • Foreign-produced direct products of certain U.S. technology or software. Some items made outside the United States are covered because they were produced with specified U.S. technology, software, or equipment (Export Administration Regulations, 2026, § 734.9).

The Ten General Prohibitions confirm this reach. They prohibit, among other things, re-exporting controlled items without a license, re-exporting foreign-made items that contain more than de minimis controlled U.S. content, dealing with parties under a denial order, and exporting to prohibited end users or end uses (Karim, 2025; Export Administration Regulations, 2026, § 736.2). These prohibitions apply to "any person," including an Indonesian company.

1.3 How the EAR are enforced against parties in Indonesia

The EAR do not give U.S. authorities the power to act inside Indonesian territory, and Indonesian courts do not enforce U.S. export regulations. The reach of the EAR is therefore practical rather than territorial. It works through three channels:

  1. Access to U.S.-origin items. BIS can add a foreign company to the Entity List or issue a denial order. Every other party in the world is then prohibited from supplying that company with items subject to the EAR, which can cut it off from U.S. technology (BIS, n.d.-b).
  2. Penalties linked to U.S. exposure. Fines and other sanctions can be enforced when a company has U.S. assets, U.S. business partners, or uses the U.S. financial system.
  3. Contracts. U.S. suppliers usually require Indonesian buyers to sign end-user statements and re-export clauses, so EAR obligations become contractual obligations that are enforceable under Indonesian contract law.

1.4 Example involving Indonesia

An Indonesian company buys specialized equipment manufactured in the United States. Later, it wants to resell the equipment to a buyer in another country. Even though the equipment is physically in Indonesia, the resale is a re-export under the EAR. Whether a license is needed depends on the item's Export Control Classification Number (ECCN), the destination, the end user, the end use, and whether a license exception applies.

1.5 Relationship with Indonesian law

The EAR do not replace or override Indonesian law. The two systems apply side by side. Indonesian exports are governed by Law No. 7 of 2014 on Trade and by Minister of Trade Regulation No. 23 of 2023 on Export Policy and Regulation, as amended by Regulation No. 11 of 2024. These rules require registered exporter status (Eksportir Terdaftar), export approval (Persetujuan Ekspor), and surveyor reports (Laporan Surveyor) for specific goods such as non-pharmaceutical precursors, mining products, and coal (Kementerian Perdagangan Republik Indonesia [Kemendag], 2024). A single shipment can therefore be subject to Indonesian export requirements and, if the item is subject to the EAR, to U.S. requirements at the same time.

Conclusion

The EAR are extraterritorial because their application is determined by the item, not by where the item is located. The rules follow U.S.-origin goods overseas and also cover certain foreign-made products that contain controlled U.S. content or were made with U.S. technology. The United States cannot enforce the EAR inside Indonesia directly, but it can effectively enforce them through access to U.S. technology, penalties, and supplier contracts. Indonesian companies that handle such items must therefore comply with U.S. export controls in addition to Indonesian law.

Question 2

What is the real impact for Indonesia in the IT, manufacturing and electronics sectors, and in the commodity and logistics sectors?

The real impact of the EAR is that Indonesian businesses may face U.S. export control requirements even though they operate outside the United States. This happens when a product contains controlled U.S.-origin components, uses certain U.S. technology, or is intended for a restricted destination, end user, or end use. The EAR are not a ban on Indonesian exports, and many products traded by Indonesian companies are not subject to the EAR at all.

2.1 IT, manufacturing, and electronics

Main impact: technology access, production costs, and market access.

  • IT and data centers. Advanced computing chips, high-performance servers, and some networking equipment and encryption software are controlled under the EAR. In January 2025, BIS issued the AI Diffusion Rule, which would have placed limits on how many advanced AI chips could be shipped to most countries, including Indonesia. BIS rescinded that rule in May 2025, but it issued guidance at the same time. That guidance warns that using certain Chinese-made advanced chips anywhere in the world may violate the EAR and lists red flags for chips being diverted to restricted users (Bureau of Industry and Security [BIS], 2025). In practice, Indonesian data center and cloud operators face closer due diligence from U.S. chip suppliers and must provide end-use and end-user information.
  • Manufacturing. Indonesian factories using U.S.-origin machinery, industrial software, or technical data must check the EAR before they transfer, resell, or send that equipment abroad. Servicing and replacement parts may be covered by License Exception RPL, but only if its conditions are met (Karim, 2025).
  • Electronics. Companies assembling computers, circuit boards, or other electronic products must check whether the de minimis rule or a foreign direct product rule brings their finished products under the EAR. If it does, exports to certain destinations or customers may need a U.S. license.
  • Business consequences. Classification, screening, and licensing take time and money. This raises administrative costs and can delay equipment purchases, production upgrades, and shipments.

Example: an Indonesian electronics manufacturer imports a controlled U.S. component and builds it into a product for export. Before shipping, the company must check the component's ECCN, calculate the U.S. content against the de minimis threshold, and screen the destination and end user.

2.2 Commodity sector

Main impact: mostly indirect, through equipment, software, and company ownership.

Indonesia's main commodities, such as nickel products, coal, copper, and palm oil, are Indonesian-origin goods with no U.S. content, so they are generally not subject to the EAR. For these goods, the more important export controls are Indonesian. For example, Regulation No. 11 of 2024 allowed copper, iron, lead, and zinc concentrates to be exported only until 31 December 2024. After that date, these concentrates may be exported only for research and development, re-export, or certain industrial purposes (Kemendag, 2024, Art. 49). The same regulation lists nickel ore and its concentrates among mining goods that may be exported only for those limited purposes (Kemendag, 2024, Lampiran I, Section XVI).

The EAR still affect the commodity sector in the following ways:

  • Mining and smelting equipment. Specialized U.S.-origin processing equipment, sensors, and industrial control software remain subject to the EAR after they arrive in Indonesia.
  • Company ownership. Chinese companies control roughly three quarters of Indonesia's nickel smelting capacity (Nangoy, 2025). In September 2025, BIS issued the "Affiliates Rule," which extends Entity List restrictions to any company that is 50 percent or more owned by a listed party. The rule was suspended for one year as part of a U.S.-China trade arrangement, and the suspension runs until 9 November 2026 unless BIS extends it (Egan et al., 2025). If the rule comes back into force, an Indonesian smelter that is majority-owned by a listed Chinese parent could lose access to U.S.-origin equipment, software, and spare parts.
  • Investment and operating costs. Additional compliance checks can delay the purchase of specialized machinery or the expansion of processing plants.

Example: an Indonesian mineral processing company buys U.S.-origin equipment. It must check the EAR if it later moves that equipment to another country or sells it to a different user. The nickel or copper product it produces is not controlled by the EAR just because U.S. equipment was used.

2.3 Logistics, shipping, and supply chains

Main impact: more compliance checks, possible shipment delays, and greater documentation responsibility.

  • Freight forwarders and exporters. They must establish whether goods are subject to the EAR and whether a license is needed. When "NLR" (no license required) is declared in export documentation, the declaration is made under penalty of perjury, and a wrong determination can be penalized (Karim, 2025).
  • Re-exports and transshipment. Shipping an EAR-controlled item from Indonesia to another country is a re-export. General Prohibition 8 also restricts moving controlled items through or unloading them in certain countries (Karim, 2025).
  • Changes in destination or buyer. Changing the buyer, destination, or end user while goods are in a warehouse can change whether the transaction is permitted.
  • Knowledge of violations. General Prohibition 10 prohibits proceeding with a transaction when a party knows a violation has occurred or is about to occur, so a forwarder that ignores obvious red flags can also be liable (Export Administration Regulations, 2026, § 736.2).
  • Supply chain transparency. Businesses need clearer records of product origin, U.S. content, classification, destination, and end user.

Conclusion

The EAR do not impose a blanket restriction on Indonesian exports. They create compliance obligations for specific goods, technologies, and transactions that have a link to U.S. export controls. The IT, manufacturing, and electronics sectors feel the impact directly through components and technology. The commodity sector is affected mainly indirectly, through U.S.-origin equipment and, potentially, through the ownership of companies. The logistics sector faces more screening and documentation duties. The practical challenge for Indonesia is to keep its access to international technology and markets while complying with both U.S. export controls and Indonesian law.

Question 3

Is there a risk of violations if Indonesia ignores the EAR?

Yes. The Indonesian state itself is not bound by U.S. regulations and cannot "violate" the EAR, but Indonesian companies and individuals can. The risk arises when they export, re-export, or transfer items that are subject to the EAR without the required authorization, or when they deal with restricted parties. The risk does not apply to every Indonesian company, only to those whose goods, software, technology, or business partners have a link to U.S. export controls.

3.1 Potential consequences

  1. Civil penalties. BIS can impose fines of up to USD 374,474 per violation or twice the value of the transaction, whichever is greater. This amount applied from 15 January 2025 and is adjusted every year for inflation (BIS, n.d.-b).
  2. Criminal penalties. Willful violations can lead to fines of up to USD 1 million per violation, imprisonment of up to 20 years, or both (BIS, n.d.-b; Export Control Reform Act of 2018, 2018).
  3. Loss of export privileges. BIS can issue a denial order, or a Temporary Denial Order lasting up to 180 days, and can add a company to the Entity List. Under General Prohibition 4, other companies anywhere in the world are then prohibited from dealing with that company in transactions involving EAR items (BIS, n.d.-b; Karim, 2025).
  4. Supply chain disruption. Shipments of controlled goods can be delayed or stopped if authorization is missing, and suppliers may stop selling to a company they consider high risk.
  5. Reputational and national risk. Companies can lose partners and access to U.S. technology. If Indonesia is seen as a route for diverting controlled items, the United States may apply stricter licensing to all Indonesian buyers.

3.2 Example involving Indonesia

An Indonesian electronics distributor imports controlled U.S.-origin components and re-exports them to a buyer in a third country that is on the Entity List, without a license. Even though the transaction happens outside the United States, the distributor could face BIS enforcement, civil fines, and placement on the Entity List. The forwarder that handled the shipment could also be liable if it ignored clear warning signs.

Conclusion

Ignoring the EAR creates legal, financial, and operational risks for Indonesian businesses, and these risks can also harm Indonesia's reputation as a trading partner. The practical solution is to determine whether a transaction is subject to the EAR, check whether a license or license exception is needed, and comply with both U.S. export controls and Indonesian law.

Question 4

What mitigations does Indonesia need to take to be compliant with the EAR?

Mitigation is needed at two levels: the government, which sets the national framework, and companies, which carry out individual transactions.

4.1 Government level

  1. Complete a national strategic trade management framework. At the Southeast Asia Forum on Export Controls (SEAFEC) in Jakarta in September 2026, the Minister of Trade stated that Indonesia is building a risk-based strategic trade management system based on UN Security Council Resolution 1540, to be implemented in phases (Kemendag, 2026). Finishing this framework, including a clear list of controlled dual-use goods, would give exporters legal certainty and increase trading partners' confidence in Indonesian products.
  2. Build on existing legal tools. Law No. 7 of 2014 on Trade allows the government to prohibit exports to protect national security (Undang-Undang Nomor 7 Tahun 2014, 2014, Art. 50). The current export regulation already controls sensitive goods through registered exporter status, export approval, and surveyor reports. For example, non-pharmaceutical precursors require a recommendation from the Ministry of Industry (Kemendag, 2024, Lampiran I, Section XI). The same mechanism could be extended to strategic goods.
  3. Strengthen customs and transshipment monitoring. Customs authorities can add risk profiles for dual-use items to their systems so that suspicious re-exports and transshipments are detected early.
  4. Provide guidance and training. The government and industry associations should offer guidance in Bahasa Indonesia, training, and help desks, especially for micro, small, and medium enterprises that rely on U.S. technology.
  5. Maintain dialogue with the United States. Regular government-to-government communication helps Indonesia anticipate rule changes, such as the possible return of the Affiliates Rule in November 2026, and lowers the risk of Indonesia being treated as a diversion route.

4.2 Company level

BIS identifies eight elements of an effective export compliance program: management commitment, risk assessment, export authorization, recordkeeping, training, audits, handling of violations and corrective actions, and building and maintaining the program (BIS, n.d.-a). Indonesian companies can apply them as follows:

  1. Establish an export compliance program. Management should assign clear responsibilities, provide resources, and conduct a risk assessment at least once a year (BIS, n.d.-a).
  2. Determine jurisdiction and classification. Following the steps in the course material, companies should first check whether another U.S. agency controls the item and whether it is subject to the EAR, then identify its ECCN on the Commerce Control List and check the Country Chart. If they are unsure, they can request an advisory opinion from BIS, which is normally given within 30 days (Bade, 2015; Karim, 2025).
  3. Screen destinations, end users, and end uses. Customers and other parties should be checked against U.S. restricted party lists, such as the Entity List and the Denied Persons List, and the final destination and intended use should be verified.
  4. Use license exceptions correctly or apply for a license. If a license is required, companies should first check whether a license exception such as LVS, GBS, CIV, TMP, RPL, GOV, GFT, TSU, or BAG applies, keep documents that prove the exception is valid, and otherwise apply to BIS for a license (Karim, 2025). They should not assume that every U.S.-origin product needs a license, or that every product made outside the United States is exempt.
  5. Train employees and keep records. Purchasing staff, engineers, sales teams, and freight forwarders should receive regular training. Records of classifications, screening results, licensing decisions, and shipping documents must generally be kept for five years (Export Administration Regulations, 2026, Part 762).
  6. Include export control clauses in contracts. Contracts with suppliers and customers should include end-use statements and re-export restrictions so that obligations pass along the supply chain.
  7. Audit regularly and correct violations. Companies should review their procedures periodically, investigate possible violations, take corrective action, and consider voluntary self-disclosure to BIS, which can reduce penalties (BIS, n.d.-a; Export Administration Regulations, 2026, § 764.5).

Conclusion

Indonesia can reduce EAR-related risks if the government completes its strategic trade management framework and supports businesses, while companies build compliance programs that cover classification, screening, licensing, training, recordkeeping, and audits. These measures help Indonesian companies keep their access to international technology, avoid penalties and shipment delays, and protect their global supply chain relationships. Compliance with the EAR does not replace compliance with Indonesian law, so companies must meet their obligations under both systems.

Assignment · Import labelling and payment

Why import labels are applied at the factory, and why the letter of credit suits document-based trade

Question 1

Why must import labelling regulations be fulfilled at the factory?

Import labeling regulations include requirements such as local-language text, ingredient lists, safety warnings, and energy efficiency ratings. These requirements must be fulfilled at the factory in the country of origin for the following reasons:

  • Compliance is checked at the point of entry. Many countries require goods to be correctly labeled at the moment they enter the country. In Indonesia, for example, Ministry of Trade regulations require imported goods to already carry an Indonesian-language label when they enter the Indonesian customs area. Since the legal checkpoint is the border, the label must be applied before the goods are shipped.
  • Consumer protection. Labels allow consumers to read safety instructions, warnings, and usage guidelines in their own language. Labeling at the factory ensures that every unit is compliant before it reaches the market.
  • Cost and efficiency. Applying labels on the production line is much cheaper and faster than unpacking, relabeling, and repacking goods after they arrive.
  • Quality control and verification. Labels applied under controlled factory conditions are more accurate and consistent. They can also be checked by a third-party inspector before shipment, giving the importer proof that the goods are compliant.
  • Avoiding rejection at customs. Shipments with missing or incorrect labels can be refused entry, held, re-exported, or destroyed, which creates heavy losses for the importer.

Why labels are generally not applied at the port of destination

Ports are designed for cargo handling, inspection, and customs clearance. They are not packaging or manufacturing facilities. Relabeling at the port is generally not allowed as a way to fix non-compliant goods because of:

  • Port congestion. Large-scale unpacking and relabeling would slow down port operations and delay other cargo. It would also cost the importer storage and demurrage charges while the goods wait.
  • Risk of tampering and evasion. If importers could bring in unlabeled goods and "fix" them later, it would create a loophole for smuggling, counterfeit products, and avoiding technical standards.

Some countries do allow limited relabeling in bonded warehouses or free trade zones under customs supervision. However, this is an exception and should not be relied on as a normal business practice.

Question 2

Why is the Letter of Credit (L/C) well-aligned with document handling?

International trade is largely document-based, and the Letter of Credit fits this system well. Under UCP 600 (Article 5), the international rules for L/Cs, banks deal only with documents and not with the actual goods. The L/C supports document handling in the following ways:

  • Payment depends on correct documents. The issuing bank promises to pay the seller only when the seller presents the documents required in the L/C. These usually include the commercial invoice, bill of lading, packing list, and certificate of origin.
  • Strict compliance. The documents must match the terms of the L/C exactly. If there is a discrepancy, the bank can refuse payment. This pushes the seller to prepare accurate documents.
  • Control over the goods. The bill of lading is a document of title. Whoever holds it can claim the goods, so the buyer only gains control of the goods after the bank has checked the documents.
  • Protection against non-compliance. The importer can require extra documents in the L/C, such as an inspection certificate or labeling compliance certificate from an independent surveyor. This links back to Question 1. If the seller cannot prove that labeling was done correctly at the factory, the bank will not release payment.
  • Reducing the need for trust. The buyer and seller may not know or trust each other. The L/C solves this by replacing the buyer's credit with the bank's credit. The seller is assured of payment, and the buyer is protected from paying for goods that were not shipped as agreed.

Limitation: because banks only check documents, an L/C cannot guarantee that the goods themselves are compliant. It only confirms that the documents say so. This is why requiring a certificate from an independent inspector is important. L/Cs are also more expensive and slower than other payment methods, so they are most useful in new trade relationships or high-risk shipments.
