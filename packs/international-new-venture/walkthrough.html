In plain words

International New Venture is organized as fourteen sessions taught in pairs, one pair per week. The first five weeks serve the first course intended learning outcome (CILO 1): identifying international market opportunities and reading the international business environment. Weeks 1 and 2 look at the entrepreneur, Week 3 at where opportunities come from, Week 4 at the industry and the market an opportunity must survive in, and Week 5 at the business model that turns an opportunity into money. The last two weeks serve CILO 2, analyzing a business model and strategic plan suitable for international expansion: Week 6 covers designing, prototyping and validating the product, and Week 7 covers legal protection of the venture's knowledge at home and abroad.

The order matters because each week supplies what the next one needs. A business model (Week 5) cannot be sketched until the entrepreneur knows the first customer and the competitive forces (Week 4), and those cannot be studied until there is a framed problem worth solving (Week 3). Likewise, intellectual property (IP) protection (Week 7) only makes sense once there is a validated product worth protecting (Week 6).

The three cases test the frameworks in practice. EuroDisneyland, set beside Week 1, shows how a model that worked in the United States and Japan lost money near Paris. LocalBite, set beside Week 2, is a student founder who believes several of the myths the week dismantles. FreshMart, set beside Week 5, asks students to separate a business model from a value proposition and to choose a profitable change on a limited budget.

Week 1 · Sessions 1–2 · CILO 1

What entrepreneurship is: the definition and the entrepreneurial process

Problem"Entrepreneur" is often used to mean anyone who opens a shop, which hides what makes entrepreneurial ventures different.
Big ideaEntrepreneurship is pursuing opportunity without regard to the resources currently controlled; it is a mindset and a set of behaviors, not only the act of starting a business.
How (tools)Stevenson's definition and the three-phase entrepreneurial process: discover, validate, then design and plan.
ResultA new venture that keeps testing and adapting to a dynamic environment after launch.
In plain words

Howard Stevenson defines entrepreneurship as "the process by which individuals, either on their own or inside organizations, pursue opportunities without regard to the resources they currently control." The last phrase is the key. An entrepreneur does not start by counting the money, staff and equipment already available; they start from an opportunity and then gather what they need. The definition also says "inside organizations", so entrepreneurship can happen within a large company as well as in a startup.

The slides stress that entrepreneurship is more than starting a business. It is a way of thinking that is opportunity-focused, risk-taking, innovative and growth-oriented, together with the behaviors that follow from that thinking. Someone who opens a copy of an existing café to earn a steady income is running a small business; someone who spots an unserved need and builds a venture designed to grow is acting entrepreneurially.

The entrepreneurial process is not a straight line. The textbook describes it as a dynamic system with moving parts, organized in three phases. In Phase 1 the entrepreneur finds a significant problem, researches the industry, the market and any intellectual property (IP) or regulatory issues, and turns what they learn into hypotheses. In Phase 2 those hypotheses about the customer, the solution and the business model are tested, which reveals the likely sources of revenue and drivers of cost. Only when Phases 1 and 2 give enough confidence does Phase 3 begin: designing the business, assembling the team and partners, and choosing when to launch. Testing does not stop at launch, because the new venture now lives in an environment that keeps changing.

Stevenson's definition, unpacked

  • Process: entrepreneurship unfolds over time; it is not a single decision.
  • Individuals, alone or inside organizations: startups and corporate ventures both count.
  • Pursue opportunities: the starting point is an unmet need or unsolved problem.
  • Without regard to resources currently controlled: resources are gathered after the opportunity is identified.

The entrepreneurial mindset

The way of thinking the slides attach to entrepreneurship has four traits: it is opportunity-focused, risk-taking, innovative and growth-oriented.

The three phases of the entrepreneurial process

  1. Phase 1 · Find the problem and research itFind a significant problem or need in a market or industry; do preliminary research on the industry, the potential market and any issues in intellectual property (IP), regulation or product development; develop hypotheses from what was learned.
  2. Phase 2 · Validate the hypothesesTest the hypotheses about the customer, the solution and the proposed business model. The results show potential sources of revenue and major drivers of cost.
  3. Phase 3 · Design and plan executionWhen Phases 1 and 2 give sufficient confidence that the business is feasible, design the business and plan its execution: put the right team and partners in place and choose the best time to launch.

The dynamic environment after launch

Successful execution of the process produces a new venture, but testing and validation continue because the business responds to variables outside its control:

  • The economic environment
  • Competition
  • New laws and regulations
  • Labor supply
  • Sources of capital
Exam tip: When asked to "define entrepreneurship", quote Stevenson and then add the second layer from the slides: it is also a mindset (opportunity-focused, risk-taking, innovative, growth-oriented) and a set of behaviors. Then show the process is iterative by naming the three phases and the loop back from failed hypotheses.

Why entrepreneurship matters: technological change, industries and jobs

ProblemEconomies need new jobs and new industries, and old industries do not last forever.
Big ideaEntrepreneurs create technological change by acting on shifts in customer needs; that change gives birth to industries and creates jobs.
How (tools)The entrepreneurship and technological change model, the industry life cycle, the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) economy types, and the three traits of high-impact ventures.
ResultA clear picture of the role entrepreneurship plays in economic growth, and of what distinguishes a high-impact venture.
In plain words

Technological change does not appear on its own. It happens when an entrepreneur notices something on the customer side: a new customer segment emerging, a new need, an existing need nobody has satisfied, or a new way to make and distribute products. The entrepreneur combines that insight with resources, builds a new venture, and the new product or process changes how things are done. Repeated across many ventures, this is what drives economic growth.

New industries are born the same way. An industry is simply the people and companies engaged in a category of business activity. When technological change opens a novel opportunity and entrepreneurs seize it, a new industry forms. Like people, industries have life cycles: they are born, grow, decline and die. That is why entrepreneurship is a permanent need in an economy rather than a passing trend.

Entrepreneurs also create jobs. The annual Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) groups countries into factor-driven, efficiency-driven and innovation-driven economies. Factor-driven economies rely on unskilled labor and natural resources, so many businesses start out of necessity and entrepreneurial activity rates are very high. Across all three groups, the 25–34 age group shows the most intention to start a business. Not every new business has the same impact, though. High-impact ventures are innovative, value-creating and growth-oriented, aiming at regional, national or global scale, which is the kind of venture an international new venture aspires to be.

Four signals that produce technological change

Technological change happens when an entrepreneur identifies:

  1. New customer segments that appear to be emerging.
  2. New customer needs.
  3. Existing customer needs that have not been satisfied.
  4. New ways of manufacturing and distributing products and services.

The industry life cycle (Week 1 view)

  • Industry: the people and companies that engage in a category of business activity.
  • Birth: new industries are born when technological change produces a novel opportunity that entrepreneurs seize.
  • Life cycle: industries are born, grow, decline and die. Week 4 breaks this into six named stages.

Three characteristics of high-impact ventures

  1. InnovativeThe venture brings something new to the marketplace.
  2. Value-creatingIt creates new jobs that do not draw from existing businesses and serves customer needs that are currently unserved.
  3. Growth-orientedThe entrepreneur sees the business growing to a regional, national or global level.

Week 1 · Sessions 1–2 · CILO 1

Risk, failure and the changing climate: the fuzzy front end and current trends

ProblemMost new venture risk is taken before launch, when information is thin, and many new businesses fail for avoidable reasons.
Big ideaRisk is concentrated in the fuzzy front end; gathering information and testing the business model reduces it.
How (tools)The fuzzy front end, the four reasons for new business failure, the decades of entrepreneurship, and the technology trends shaping new ventures.
ResultAn entrepreneur who knows where risk lives, avoids the classic failure patterns and reads the trends that create the next opportunities.
In plain words

Risk runs through the whole entrepreneurial process, but much of it sits early. The period before launch and startup is called the fuzzy front end: activities are unclear and change as information arrives. Nascent entrepreneurs, people in the process of starting a business, use this period to estimate three things: the probability of success, what success would mean as a return on their time, money and effort, and what failure would cost. Because these estimates are highly subjective, the remedy is to gather enough information and test the business model to push the risk down.

Failure has recognizable patterns. Entrepreneurs often start with a solution looking for a problem, with no real need and no customers willing to pay. Or the solution is a "me-too" product with nothing different from what is already on the market. Or the value proposition is not communicated well. Or the business model was never identified and tested. Each failure points to a step in the entrepreneurial process that was skipped.

The climate for entrepreneurs also changes. The word "entrepreneur" only came into popular use in the United States in the 1980s, the "decade of entrepreneurship", even though the country was founded on free enterprise. Since then, the information age and the knowledge economy have opened new fields, and the slides name the technology trends research firms such as Gartner, Forrester and Deloitte see as the foundation for the next generation of ventures: intelligent technology, the digital environment and disruption.

What nascent entrepreneurs calculate in the fuzzy front end

  1. Probability of success as an entrepreneur.
  2. Return: what success will mean for the investment of time, money and effort.
  3. Cost of failure: the risk if it does not work.

Four reasons new businesses fail

  1. Solution looking for a problemNo real need in the market, no customers willing to pay.
  2. Not unique or compellingA "me-too" solution offering nothing different from what exists.
  3. Value proposition poorly communicatedCustomers do not understand why they should buy.
  4. Untested business modelNo identified and tested business model that actually works.

The decades of entrepreneurship

The free enterprise principle encouraged entrepreneurs to take on the risk of developing businesses, but popular use of the word "entrepreneur" in the United States dates only from the 1980s.

Current entrepreneurial trends

Week 1 · Case · CILO 1

Case study: why EuroDisneyland lost money in its first year

ProblemA theme park model that succeeded in Anaheim, Orlando and Tokyo lost about half a billion dollars within a year of opening near Paris in April 1992.
Big ideaA business model proven in one country is only a hypothesis in another; costs, prices and culture all had to be retested and were not.
How (tools)The case exhibits (costs, admission rates, visitor profiles, Disney's payoff), the spend-to-admission ratio, and a ranked set of turnaround options.
ResultA diagnosis with four causes and a turnaround that stabilizes cash first, fixes the offer second and reinvests third.
In plain words

By 1983 Disney had three successful parks: Disneyland in Anaheim (1955), Walt Disney World in Orlando, and Tokyo Disneyland. Tokyo was a near-exact copy of Anaheim, with signs in English and American food, and it thrived because Japanese visitors wanted the "real thing." Disney took no equity in Tokyo; it licensed the park to the Oriental Land Company and collected 10% of admissions, 5% of food and merchandise sales and licensing fees. Encouraged, Disney chose Marne-la-Vallée, 20 miles from Paris, over Spain for a European park, citing some 300 million people within a day's drive or train ride.

The European deal was structured differently. Disney took a 49% stake for only $160 million of its own capital, kept management control, and collected the same royalties as in Japan plus management fees and 49% of profits. French authorities provided low-interest loans and infrastructure and cut the value added tax (VAT) on tickets from 18.6% to 7%. Disney promised in return to respect and use French culture in its themes. The park opened on 12 April 1992. By September 1992 it had reported a net loss of FFr188 million (French francs), cumulative losses approached $500 million by April 1993, the share price had fallen from FFr166 to about FFr65, and the chairman had been replaced by Philippe Bourguignon.

The puzzle is that attendance (about 10 million a year) exceeded the stated break-even of 7 to 8 million visitors, yet the park lost money. The class analysis resolves it by working through the exhibits. The break-even figure belonged to the original $1 billion plan, but construction estimates grew to $4.4 billion. Visitors paid the highest adult ticket of any Disney park but spent the least in total. French household leisure budgets and long holiday blocks did not fit an expensive day out. And Disney's fees were charged on revenue, so Disney earned while shareholders lost. Cultural choices, such as banning alcohol in a wine-drinking country and imposing a strict United States appearance code, made the fit worse.

Cause 1: costs grew but the break-even was never updated

Construction cost estimates rose from $1.0 billion in 1985 to $4.4 billion in 1991. French construction costs averaged 20% above similar United States jobs; facades received six coats of paint against one in the United States parks; Le Visionarium ran $8–10 million over; and there were delays and modifications. The break-even of 7 to 8 million visitors was set for the $1 billion version and never recalculated, so the project became unprofitable during construction.

Cause 2: a high ticket left little to spend inside

Supporting causes: copying instead of adapting

  • American formula copied: no alcohol in a wine country, mainly American food, and a United States appearance code challenged under French labor law, which bans restrictions on individual liberties unless they are required by the job.
  • Tokyo's lesson misread: Japan wanted American culture; French intellectuals called the park a "cultural Chernobyl."
  • Weather: Paris averages 32–42°F in January against Orlando's 49–70°F; covered walkways and 35 fireplaces were added, but the season stayed short.
  • Local relations: work-bid adverts first placed only in English; farmers protested land expropriation; the communist-led union federation, the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT), opposed flexible working hours.

The class analysis recommends Option E, drawing on retrenchment-then-recovery turnaround theory: a firm must stop the bleeding before it can grow, and reversing the order is a common reason turnarounds fail. Its action list: ask Disney to waive royalties and fees for a period; use the fact that Disney risked only $160 million while lenders risked far more as leverage to reschedule debt; freeze Phase Two without cancelling it; cut ticket and hotel prices and add winter pricing; serve wine and beer and add cheaper menus; rename the park to stress its Paris location; and, once cash is stable, build a major new attraction.

Lessons for international new ventures

  1. Product-market fit comes first, especially in a foreign country.
  2. Past success does not travel automatically: a strategy that works in one region may not work in another.
  3. Recalculate break-even on the latest cost estimate.
  4. Set price to the customer segment, not only to cover costs.
  5. Heavy debt plus high operating costs puts a venture at constant risk.

Who becomes an entrepreneur: stereotypes and six myths

ProblemWould-be founders hold back because they believe they lack the right personality, enough money or a brilliant idea.
Big ideaResearch finds no stereotypical entrepreneur, and six common beliefs about entrepreneurship are myths.
How (tools)The six myths, each answered with the research finding or quotation from the slides.
ResultEntrepreneurship is seen as a learnable discipline open to people of any age and starting capital.
In plain words

Entrepreneurship begins in the mind of an individual, and from the motivation to start through every decision about growth, customers, facilities, employees and exit, it comes down to people and their needs. That makes it tempting to look for a "type" of person who becomes an entrepreneur. Research has failed to find one: no psychological or sociological characteristic predicts with any certainty who will become an entrepreneur or who will succeed.

Without a stereotype to rely on, beliefs fill the gap, and many are wrong. The slides list six myths. Each one, if believed, either stops a person from starting or pushes them into a costly mistake, such as taking on unnecessary risk because "bigger risk means bigger reward," or spending months on a formal plan before speaking to a customer.

The corrections share one theme: success comes from disciplined behavior that can be learned, not from luck, genes or a single big idea. Great businesses have more often started with a great team than a great idea, investors expect founders to reduce risk, and Peter Drucker called entrepreneurship "a discipline" that, "like any discipline, can be learned."

Saying goodbye to stereotypes

Research has failed to identify a stereotypical entrepreneur. There are no psychological or sociological characteristics that can predict with any certainty who will become an entrepreneur or who will succeed as one.

The six myths and the reality

MythReality from the slides
1 · It takes a lot of money to start a businessA false assumption: "Nothing could be further from the truth."
2 · It takes a great ideaMost great businesses successful for at least 50 years started with a great team that wanted to build an enduring company. Usually the execution plan, not the idea, makes the business a success.
3 · The bigger the risk, the bigger the rewardRisk is relative, and the goal of entrepreneurs and investors is to reduce it. Investors expect founders to test the market, acquire the first customer and invest some of their own capital.
4 · A business plan is required for successMany successful businesses started without a formal plan. Current thinking: (1) get in front of customers to test the idea and (2) have a plan to grow the business.

6 · Entrepreneurship cannot be taughtDrucker: "It's not magic, it's not mysterious, and it has nothing to do with the genes. It is a discipline. And, like any discipline, it can be learned." Specific skills and behaviors can be taught and applied.

Paths to entrepreneurship: five routes and the corporate entrepreneur

ProblemEntrepreneurship takes many forms, from a home-based freelancer to a venture team inside a large firm, and each faces different conditions.
Big ideaPeople start out of necessity or opportunity, and they take one of several recognizable paths, each with its own rules.
How (tools)The conditions that favor startups, five paths (home-based, serial or portfolio, nonprofit, corporate), the four corporate venturing models, and the challenges and opportunities of the career.
ResultA founder who can place themselves on a path and weigh its challenges against its opportunities.
In plain words

People start businesses either out of necessity, because they need income, or because they are driven by an opportunity. The startup route becomes more attractive when entry barriers in the industry are low, the job market is uncertain, investment capital is plentiful, or the opportunity rests on a breakthrough technology that will make older technology obsolete.

The paths differ widely. Home-based businesses include hobby and freelance work but also ventures that compete with brand-name firms, made possible by technology; the Small Business Administration (SBA) reported that 52 percent of small businesses were home-based as of 2014. Serial entrepreneurs start one business after another, building on experience, while portfolio entrepreneurs hold stakes in several ventures at once. Nonprofit entrepreneurs pursue educational, religious or charitable goals and seek tax-exempt status; contrary to popular belief they can and should make a profit, but it stays in the organization. Corporate entrepreneurs innovate inside existing organizations, where projects are risky and uncertain enough to be managed apart from the core business.

Large companies use four models to simulate an entrepreneurial environment, ranging from waiting for champions to building a dedicated, autonomous unit. Whatever the path, the career brings real challenges (uncertainty, long unpaid hours, isolation) alongside real opportunities (independence, wealth, doing good). Because opportunity is rarely found in stable, predictable settings, would-be entrepreneurs must learn to embrace change and uncertainty.

When people choose the startup route

  • Low industry entry barriers
  • Uncertainty in the job market
  • Plentiful investment capital
  • A breakthrough or disruptive technology that will make previous technology obsolete

Four models of corporate entrepreneurship

  1. Opportunistic modelWaits for project champions to emerge and suggest opportunities, then decides whether to support them.
  2. Enabler modelSprinkles resources throughout the organization to encourage innovation at all levels, with clear criteria for selecting opportunities.
  3. Advocate modelActs like an evangelist: assigns ownership of a project or new business and provides modest seed funding to test it.
  4. Producer modelEstablishes formal organizations with dedicated funds and significant autonomy; this approach used to be called a skunkworks.

Those on the corporate path need to align with key decision makers, work with only the best, most motivated people, and do any job needed to make the project work.

Challenges

  • Working long hours, often without pay
  • Facing uncertainty and high risk
  • Making major decisions that affect other people's lives
  • Relying on others for expertise and resources

Getting ready: six steps to prepare for entrepreneurship

ProblemStartups expose founders to extra risk because they are typically undercapitalized, poorly managed and undifferentiated from competitors.
Big ideaSpecific preparation steps raise the odds of success, and a venture with growth potential repays the effort better than one that yields a single job.
How (tools)Six steps: find a mentor, build a professional network, learn about entrepreneurs, understand personality and preferences, acquire critical skills, study an industry.
ResultA founder with guidance, contacts, self-knowledge, skills and the industry knowledge where the best opportunities are found.
In plain words

Starting any business takes a tremendous amount of time, effort and resources, so it usually makes sense to start one with the potential to grow large and give a good return, rather than spend the same effort on a business that yields only one job. Research also shows that starting a business is risky because new firms are typically undercapitalized, poorly managed and undifferentiated. Preparation is how a founder works on all three weaknesses before launch.

The six steps begin with people. A mentor is someone already living the life the founder envisions, who acts as guide, sounding board, champion and gateway to people the founder could not otherwise meet. A professional network adds more reach. Here the course draws a useful distinction: strong ties (close friends and family) and weak ties (acquaintances and business contacts). Weak ties matter more than one might expect, because they are not biased by a shared history and so give objective advice that moves the founder forward faster. To make a network large and meaningful, the founder connects with brokers, opinion leaders who act as gateways to other networks.

The remaining steps build knowledge. Learning about entrepreneurs means reading and, most importantly, talking to them. Understanding personality and preferences means facing the six barriers that keep people from self-employment. Acquiring skills means building analysis, critical thinking and communication. Studying an industry, perhaps by working in it, is where the best opportunities come from, because opportunities can appear anywhere along an industry's value chain.

Step 1: Find a mentor

A mentor is someone leading the type of life the founder envisions, who can act as guide and sounding board as well as champion and gateway to people the founder could not otherwise meet.

Step 2: Build a professional network

  • Networking: the exchange of information and resources among individuals, groups or organizations whose common goals are to mutually benefit and create value for the members.
  • Strong ties: close friends and family members.
  • Weak ties: acquaintances and business contacts; not biased by prior history, so entrepreneurs rely on them for objective advice and move faster with their help.
  • Network brokers: opinion leaders who exert influence between groups and serve as gateways to other networks.

Step 3: Learn about entrepreneurs

Read magazine articles, books, blogs and newspapers, and above all talk to entrepreneurs. Good places to meet them are university entrepreneurship programs, local chambers of commerce, small business development corporations and industry events.

Step 4: Understand personality and business preferences

Research has found six barriers that keep people from becoming self-employed:

  1. Lack of confidence
  2. Financial needs
  3. Startup logistics
  4. Personal or family issues
  5. Time constraints
  6. Lack of skills

Step 5: Improve or acquire critical skills

Entrepreneurs operate in uncertainty, so they must analyze a situation, extract the important and ignore the superfluous, compare outcomes and extrapolate from other experiences, often weighing options in complex situations.

Analysis and critical thinking

  • Calculated risk taking
  • Embracing uncertainty and ambiguity
  • Opportunity creation
  • Organizational and time management (details)
  • Resource gathering

Written and oral communication

  • Storytelling
  • Vision
  • Persuasion and negotiation
  • Decision making
  • Leadership and people management

Step 6: Study an industry

An industry is a group of companies engaged in a similar or related field. The computer industry, for example, includes every business providing parts, assembly, manufacturing and distribution for computers, that is, the whole value chain. The best opportunities come from experience and knowledge of an industry, a market or a type of business, and because opportunities are not limited to products and services, studying an industry reveals openings anywhere along its value chain.

Explaining why weak ties beat strong ties for advice is a frequent discriminator between good and excellent answers.

From idea to opportunity: design thinking

ProblemIdeas are a commodity; everyone has them, but few turn them into something with commercial potential.
Big ideaDesign thinking looks at the world from the customer's perspective and moves an idea through inspiration, ideation and implementation until it becomes an opportunity.
How (tools)The three groups of design thinking activity, its four core uses, empathy and four further traits of design thinkers.
ResultAn opportunity grounded in a real customer need, ready to become a business.
In plain words

The week opens with a sharp distinction. An idea is just a thought, and ideas are everywhere. What distinguishes entrepreneurs is that they know how to extract value from ideas and turn them into opportunities with commercial potential. An opportunity, unlike an idea, can turn into a business. Later in the week the course gives the most compact definition: opportunity is the intersection of an idea and a customer.

Design thinking is the process the course uses to make that move. It is an approach that looks at the world from the customer's perspective, and it can be thought of as three groups of related activities: inspiration (creativity), ideation (opportunity development) and implementation (innovation and commercialization). In its simplest form it defines a problem, creates and considers several options, refines them through iteration, and settles on a winning solution.

At the heart of design thinking is empathy: a deep understanding of what customers want. That requires the entrepreneur to embed themselves in the customer's world and see needs as the customer sees them. A founder designing a meal service for busy workers, for example, would spend time in offices at lunchtime rather than guess from a desk. Design thinkers also show integrative thinking, optimism, a taste for experimentation and a habit of collaboration.

Creativity and inspiration: the creative process and its tools

ProblemCreativity is the basis of differentiation, but it is hard to study and easily blocked by distractions and self-doubt.
Big ideaCreativity follows recognizable stages and can be strengthened with habits and structured tools.
How (tools)Wallas's four stages, Seeff's seven stages, the two kinds of roadblock, the five creative skills, idea-generation rules, brainwriting, visualization and journey mapping.
ResultA steady flow of original ideas from which opportunities can be drawn.
In plain words

Creativity is the ability to use the imagination to come up with original ideas. It lets entrepreneurs differentiate their businesses so customers notice them. It is also the root of invention, which is discovering something that did not exist before, and of innovation, which is finding a new way to do something or improving an existing product or service. And it is fundamental to problem-solving.

The creative process is hard to study because it happens inside a person's head, often unnoticed even by that person. Graham Wallas offered one of the earliest models with four stages: preparation, incubation, illumination and verification. A more recent view from Norman Seeff traces seven stages that individuals and teams go through, from "hearing the call" through "fear and resistance" to "fulfillment." Seeff's model is useful because it names the emotional dip that makes many people abandon ideas.

Creativity tends to occur naturally, but entrepreneurs often put up roadblocks without meaning to. These are environmental (multitasking is one of the biggest drains on productivity) or personal (needing ideas to seem acceptable or rational to others). Confidence grows with small wins, deep learning about the idea, and knowing when to stop polishing and start acting. Structured tools then help a person or group generate ideas: start individually, aim for quantity, capture everything, and combine ideas only after individual generation.

Creativity, invention and innovation

  • Creativity: using imagination to come up with original ideas.
  • Invention: discovering something that did not exist previously.
  • Innovation: finding a new way to do something or improving an existing product or service.

Wallas's four stages

  1. PreparationLooking at a problem from a variety of perspectives.
  2. IncubationLetting the problem lie in the subconscious for a time.
  3. IlluminationThe discovery of a solution.
  4. VerificationBringing the idea to an outcome.

1 Hearing the call · 2 Fear and resistance · 3 Resolution · 4 Turning point · 5 Creation · 6 Manifestation · 7 Fulfillment (adapted from Norman Seeff Productions).

  1. Individual first: in a group, generate ideas individually before sharing.
  2. Quantity over quality at the start.

Recognizing opportunity and framing the right problem

ProblemTeams rush to kill "crazy" ideas and jump to the obvious problem, so the real problem gets lost and money is spent on the wrong solution.
Big ideaOpportunities are either discovered in market shifts or created by the entrepreneur's own actions; either way, the problem must be found and framed before solutions are developed.
How (tools)Discovery and creation theories, affirmative judgment, problem restatement with "how" and "why," attribute identification, force fitting, and explicit and implicit criteria.
ResultA well-framed problem statement and a shortlist of solutions judged against clear criteria.
In plain words

Research offers two theories of how opportunities happen. Discovery theory says opportunities arise from shifts in external factors, such as regulation, technology or customer preferences, and entrepreneurs are better at spotting them because of alertness: their risk preference, their cognitive differences and the information asymmetries that let them know what others do not. Creation theory says opportunities are created by the entrepreneur's actions, reactions and experiments with products, services and business models. There are no "seeds" in the market; if seeds exist, they lie within the entrepreneur.

Whichever theory applies, the hardest part of problem-solving is identifying the right problem, because time and money spent on the wrong one are wasted. After generating ideas, the temptation is to kill the strange ones and rush to judge the best. The course recommends affirmative judgment (look for the strengths of a problem first), predefined criteria for choosing the best problem definition, and patience before concluding, especially from personal experience alone.

Restating the problem is the main technique. Asking "how" and "why" breaks open a statement that is too narrow or too broad. In the textbook example, a firm asking "How can we sell more mobile ultrasound devices?" asks why five times and ends at the real issue: "How can we speed up our product development process?" Attribute identification and force fitting then generate fresh angles, and solutions are judged against explicit criteria (budgets, deadlines) and implicit ones (intuition, team culture, preferences).

Two theories of opportunity

Discovery theory

Opportunity arises from shifts in external factors: regulation, technological change, changing customer preferences. Entrepreneurs recognize it through alertness, made up of:

  • Risk preference: how much risk you can tolerate
  • Cognitive differences: how you learn versus how others learn
  • Information asymmetries: what the entrepreneur knows that others do not

Creation theory

Entrepreneurs create opportunities through their actions, reactions and experiments around new products, services and business models. There are no "seeds" in the industry or market; any seeds lie within the entrepreneur, so these opportunities do not exist outside the entrepreneur's mind.

Finding and framing the problem

  • Affirmative judgment: look for the strengths or positive aspects of a problem first.
  • Predefined criteria: set standards for selecting the best problem definition.
  • No quick conclusions: especially when based only on personal experience.

Four components of an effective problem statement

  1. A "how" questionThe statement opens with "How can…"
  2. Who is responsibleThe party that will deal with the problem ("we").
  3. An action verbThe positive course of action anticipated ("speed up").
  4. The targetThe desired outcome ("our product development process").

Two more problem techniques

  • Attribute identification: break a problem into its elements and generate new approaches or modifications for each.
  • Force fitting: take a random object, think about its characteristics, and force them onto the problem statement; good ideas often connect things that normally do not go together.

Developing solutions

The same techniques that generate and focus problems also generate and focus solutions. Criteria matter even more here because of constraints on cost, skills and time.

Explicit criteria

Formal and stated: time limits, budget constraints and the like.

Implicit criteria

Unstated but known to matter: intuition, team culture, preferences, prejudices, and perspectives based on previous experience.

Exam tip: To show problem framing, write a weak statement, ask "why" until a root cause appears, and finish with a statement that has all four components. Label each component; examiners look for them by name.

Innovation and commercialization: turning solutions into value

ProblemA good idea earns nothing until someone will pay for it.
Big ideaInnovation extracts value that someone will pay for, and commercialization moves the innovation from the laboratory to the market by executing a business strategy.
How (tools)Schumpeter's five categories of innovation, the research, outcomes and execution stages of commercialization, incremental versus disruptive innovation, and eight sources of innovation.
ResultAn innovation with a research-backed strategy, the right team and the right market timing.
In plain words

The process of innovation extracts value from an idea, value that someone will pay for. In the 1930s the economist Joseph Schumpeter named five categories of innovation that still hold: a new product or a substantial change to an existing one, a new process, a new market, new sources of supply, and changes in industrial organization. The list is a reminder that innovation is not only about new gadgets; a new way to reach a market counts too.

Commercialization is the process that moves an innovation from the laboratory to the market by executing a business strategy. It starts with research on the industry, the target market, intellectual property (IP) protection and regulation, the customer, the business model and the business design. That research produces outcomes, decisions that set the startup capital requirements, the revenue sources and the cost drivers. It also guides two critical execution decisions: who is on the management team and when to enter the market.

Innovations fall into two broad groups. Incremental innovations improve on an existing technology or product base, often to differentiate in the market; the Apple iPad is the course example. Disruptive or radical innovations make previous technology or ways of doing things obsolete; the Internet, the birth control pill and artificial intelligence (AI) are the examples. Ideas for either kind come from many sources, from customers and observation to demographic shifts and new laws.

Schumpeter's five categories of innovation

  1. New product or substantial change in an existing product
  2. New process
  3. New market
  4. New sources of supply
  5. Changes in industrial organization

  2. OutcomesResearch informs decisions that drive startup capital requirements, revenue sources and cost drivers.
  3. ExecutionResearch guides two critical decisions: the makeup of the management team and market timing.

Reading the industry: sources, life cycle and value chain

ProblemNo business operates independently of its external environment, and some industries leave little room to make a profit.
Big ideaIndustry analysis tells the entrepreneur whether a worthwhile profit is possible and where in the industry to stand.
How (tools)Ten key questions, gross margin, secondary and primary sources, the six-stage industry life cycle and the value chain.

In plain words

An industry is a grouping of businesses that interact in a common environment as part of a value chain or distribution channel delivering a particular good. A market is different: it is defined by customers, the people who pay for the benefits of the product or service. Industry analysis looks at the first; market research (later in the week) looks at the second. Within the market, the most important early task in a feasibility study is identifying the primary market, the customers with the most need for the offer, and the first customer.

There are two reasons to study the industry. It is the business's external environment, and no business is independent of it. And the entrepreneur needs to know whether a profit can be made and whether it will be large enough to justify the effort. Gross margin is a quick test: gross profit divided by sales shows how much is left to pay overhead and make a profit, and therefore how much room there is for mistakes.

Research begins with secondary sources (published material) and moves to primary field data, because secondary sources are rarely the most current. Two frameworks then organize the findings. The industry life cycle shows the stage the industry has reached, from emerging to decline, and each stage brings different activity. The value chain shows every company that contributes to developing and distributing the industry's products; once the venture is located on it, it is easier to see who pays whom and to work out costs and prices. A venture's position on the chain usually reflects the entrepreneur's capabilities and appetite for risk.

  • Market: defined by customers, those who pay for the benefits of the product or service.

Why some industries pay better: Porter's five forces

ProblemTwo ventures with equally good products can earn very different returns depending on the industry they enter.
Big ideaFive forces in any industry set its long-run profit potential; sustained performance needs a strategy based on how the industry works.
How (tools)Barriers to entry, threat of substitutes, buyer power, supplier power and rivalry, plus the attractive versus unattractive industry profile.
ResultA judgment of industry attractiveness and an entry route, usually a niche, that avoids head-on battles.
In plain words

Michael Porter's work gives a way to look at an industry's structure and at a company's competitive strength and position within it. His premise is that high performance over time needs a well-thought-out strategy and implementation plan built on knowledge of how the industry works and how attractive its markets are. He identifies five forces that affect a venture's profit potential in terms of long-run return on investment.

Barriers to entry decide how easily new firms can come in; high barriers protect incumbents but can also block the new venture. Substitutes are products from other industries that meet the same need. Buyers with bargaining power make it hard for a newcomer to gain a foothold. Suppliers with power can raise prices, limit quality or quantity, or integrate forward by buying the outlets for their goods. Rivalry among existing firms shows up as price wars and advertising battles.

For a new venture the lesson is practical. Most new ventures cannot win a price war or afford costly advertising. In a highly competitive market they must find a niche that serves an unmet need, entering quietly to gain a foothold. An attractive industry has high entry barriers, weak supplier and buyer power, few substitutes and mild competition; an unattractive one is the opposite.

The five forces in detail

1. Barriers to entry (threat of new entrants)

Where barriers are high they discourage competitors from entering. Seven barriers:

  1. Economies of scale
  2. Brand loyalty
  3. Capital requirements
  4. Switching costs for the buyer
  5. Access to distribution channels
  6. Proprietary factors
  7. Government regulations

2. Threat from substitutes

A new venture competes not only with its own industry but with logical substitutes other industries bring to market.

3. Threat from buyers' bargaining power

Where buyers (customers) have bargaining power, it is harder for a new entrant to gain a foothold and grow.

4. Threat from suppliers' bargaining power

Suppliers exert power by threatening to raise prices, limit the quality or quantity of goods, or change the quality of what they supply. They may also integrate forward, buying the outlets for their goods and so controlling final prices.

5. Competitive rivalry among existing firms

Firms resort to price wars and advertising skirmishes. New ventures usually cannot compete on price or afford image battles, so they should identify a niche that serves an unmet need and enter quietly.

Finding the first customer: market research and customer discovery

ProblemFirst contact with customers usually overturns the entrepreneur's assumptions about who has the problem and what they need.
Big ideaCustomer discovery forms hypotheses about problems and customers and tests them with real people, cycle after cycle, until the right problem is matched to the right customer.
How (tools)Research goals and key questions, secondary customer data, the four-step market data process, the customer discovery hierarchy, ethnography, interviews, focus groups and surveys.
ResultA profiled first customer, an estimate of their demand, and the next segments to grow into.
In plain words

Entrepreneurs experiment. They form hypotheses about problems and needs and about the customers who have them, then test those hypotheses with real customers. More often than not, first contact forces a rethink and another round of tests. This iterative process may run several cycles before the entrepreneur is satisfied that the problem is the right one and is matched to the right customer.

For a new company, market research has three goals: identify and profile the first customer, estimate that customer's potential demand, and identify later segments for growth. The first customer is the one with a significant problem the venture can solve. It is not necessarily the biggest market; often it is an unserved niche that lets the venture enter without direct competition for a while. The work starts with secondary data (demographics and census figures that size and segment a market) and then moves to primary research, which is where the most valuable insight lies.

In practice, customer discovery means engaging a hierarchy of people with different roles. In a hospital buying a medical device, for example, a purchasing office may be the gatekeeper, senior doctors the influencers, an administrator the decider holding the budget, procurement the purchaser, and nurses the users. Each needs a different conversation. The tools are observation using ethnographic techniques, interviews, informal focus groups and surveys, each with its own rules to avoid bias.

Goals of market research for a new company

  1. Identify and profile the first customer.
  2. Estimate potential demand from that customer.
  3. Identify subsequent segments to tap later to grow.

Key questions when moving from market to segment

  • What are the potential markets? Do they differ in customer needs? How big and how dynamic is each? Is it growing?
  • Which customers are most likely to buy at market introduction?
  • How much do they buy, how do they buy, and how do they hear about the product?
  • How often do they buy, and what is their buying pattern through the year?
  • How can the new venture meet their needs?

Secondary customer data

Demographic data (age, income, race, occupation, education) indicate the likelihood that a person will buy and allow the market to be segmented into distinct subgroups. Census data estimate how many target customers live within the target market's geographic boundaries; those meeting the demographic requirements can then be segmented out. Data are only as good as the research methods behind them.

A process for gathering market data

  1. Assess information needsHow will the data be used, what must be collected, which analysis methods, which business designs are under consideration?
  2. Research secondary sources firstDemographics and psychographics (buying habits), market size and growth, geography, how to reach the market, how competitors reach it, which strategies have worked.
  3. Measure the target market with primary researchWho is the first customer, would they buy, why, how much, when, where would they like to find it, what do they like about competitors?
  4. Forecast demandWhat substitutes reveal, what customers, end users and intermediaries predict, whether a limited production run or test market is possible.

InfluencersPeople who want to affect the purchase decision and whose approval is often required
DecidersPeople who make the final purchase decision, usually for a company and based on a budget
PurchasersThose with the actual authority to buy
UsersThe ultimate beneficiaries of the purchase

Four ways to collect primary data

1. Ethnographic observation

Ethnography explores the social culture of groups: it studies customers in their everyday environment, collects data from many sources (the most insightful being observation and informal discussion), and analyzes through descriptions and explanations. The entrepreneur's version has five steps: learn from observation, explain what was observed, develop a hypothesis, test the theory, draw conclusions. The textbook's version phrases the activities as observe (look for confusion, skipped steps, frustration and workarounds), question (avoid leading questions and "Will you buy?"), listen (stay objective; be ready to scrap the plan), and demonstrate (let customers experience the solution through storyboards, videos or prototypes).

2. Structured interviews

More costly and slower than surveys, but they allow clarification and discussion, let the interviewer read nonverbal cues to judge truthfulness, have a high response rate, permit open-ended questions for deeper information, and build the entrepreneur's industry network.

3. Informal focus groups

A representative sample of potential customers is brought together for a presentation and discussion led by a qualified facilitator.

4. Surveys

Online, in person or by phone, drawn from a representative and, to avoid bias, random sample. Questionnaire rules:

  • Short: plenty of white space so respondents are not intimidated.
  • Neutral: no leading or biased questions.
  • Easy first: simple questions before complex ones.
  • Demographics last: when attention may have waned.
Exam tip: Asked how to validate a first customer, combine the hierarchy (who to talk to) with a method (how). Never propose asking customers "Would you buy this?" as the test; the course warns against it. Propose observation or a demonstration instead.

Segments, profiles and competitors: the customer matrix and competitive intelligence

ProblemEntrepreneurs define segments too broadly and underestimate or ignore competitors, the weakest part of most feasibility studies.
Big ideaA customer matrix makes segments specific, and systematic competitive intelligence reveals direct, indirect and emerging rivals before they surprise the venture.
How (tools)The customer matrix, the customer profile, outcome-based demand questions, three competitor types, hidden-threat checks, good versus bad competition, and sources of competitor information.
ResultA conclusion on market feasibility backed by a clear segment, a detailed profile and an honest view of the competition.
In plain words

Customer segmentation matters because it puts the right benefits in front of the right customer. Many entrepreneurs define segments too broadly and then wonder why they capture only a sliver. A customer matrix lays out, for each candidate segment, the pain, the benefit, the distribution channel and the competition. The textbook example for a portable ultrasound device compares hospital administrators (who pay and want productivity and lower litigation risk), catheter specialists (who use it and want easier guidance) and rural practitioners in underdeveloped regions (who lack imaging and are reached through government agencies or charities). Each segment needs a different offer and route.

From primary research comes the customer profile, a detailed description of the primary customer, consumer or business, that feeds everything from product design to distribution and the strategy to attract them. Demand data come next. Customers can describe their problems and the outcomes they want, but they are poor at describing solutions, so questions should seek outcomes ("How do you want to use a communication device?") rather than features. Finally, raw data must be interpreted and turned into business intelligence and a conclusion on market feasibility.

The competitive analysis is usually the weakest part of a feasibility analysis, because competitors hide their strategies, entrepreneurs underestimate the resources needed to establish a presence, they miss roadblocks, and they do not know what they do not know. The cure is to name all three competitor types, to check whether firms outside the industry have transferable competencies, and to distinguish good competition (firms making customers unhappy, an opening) from bad competition (firms doing everything right, a wall).

The customer profile

Whether consumer or business, the profile records: age, income level, education, buying habits (when, where, how much), where they typically find such products and services, and how they would like to purchase them.

Gathering demand data

Customers describe problems and desired outcomes well but solutions poorly. Asking outcome-seeking questions leads to better solution design and prepares the entrepreneur to gather demand data. Data must then be analyzed, synthesized and turned into business intelligence and a conclusion on market feasibility.

Why competitive analysis is weak

  1. Incomplete information: competitors do not reveal their most proprietary strategies and tactics.
  2. Underestimated effort: the resources and skills needed to establish a market presence.
  3. Missed roadblocks along the way.
  4. Unknown unknowns: they do not know what they do not know.

Three types of competitor

  • Direct: serve the same customer needs with the same types of resources.
  • Indirect or substitute: serve the same needs with different resources, such as substitute products, services or distribution channels.
  • Emerging or potential: not serving the same needs yet, but with the resources to move in quickly.

Spotting threats from outside the industry

Determine (1) what the competitor must do to succeed in its own core business, (2) whether it has a competency in the same area as the venture, and (3) which of its core competencies transfer to the venture's business.

Good versus bad competition

Good competition

Companies doing what they do very badly: customers are unhappy. An opening for the new venture.

Bad competition

Companies doing everything right: customers are happy, they add value and they prosper. Hard to displace.

Where to find competitor information

  • Visit competitors' websites or the outlets where their products are sold.
  • Buy their products to compare features and benefits and see how customers are treated.
  • Use Internet search engines to read what customers say.
  • For public companies, use Hoover's Online, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and OneSource as industry benchmarks.

What a business model is: Drucker's questions and the canvas

ProblemA product that meets a real need can still fail if nobody has worked out how the business creates and captures value.
Big ideaThe business model reflects the strategic decisions and trade-offs an entrepreneur makes to earn a profit; it is the blueprint for running the business.
How (tools)Drucker's five questions, the entrepreneur's business model canvas, and its front-end components: value proposition, customer segments, distribution channels, strategic partners, and activities and resources.
ResultA one-page model whose boxes can each be tested as hypotheses.
In plain words

Before a product reaches the market, a lot of thought must go into two things: a business concept that meets a real market need, and a business model that can create and capture value. Peter Drucker, often called the greatest management thinker of the last century, pushed entrepreneurs and chief executive officers (CEOs) alike to answer five questions about their business model: What is our mission? Who is our customer? What does our customer value? What are our results? What is our plan?

The term business model has been used in different ways: as the activities a business undertakes, "an analytical device for evaluation and action," or as a "blueprint" for running a business. At its essence it reflects the strategic decisions and trade-offs entrepreneurs make to earn a profit. The course's tool for laying it out is the entrepreneur's business model canvas, based on Alex Osterwalder's canvas and Ash Maurya's Lean Canvas, which places problem, solution, unique value proposition, unfair advantage and customer segments above the cost structure and revenue streams.

The first set of components describes how value is created and delivered. The value proposition is the reason the customer will buy, and the classic mistake is to describe features (design, physical attributes, functions) instead of benefits (better health, time or money saved, reliability). Customer segments divide the market into meaningful, measurable parts. The distribution channel answers how the benefit reaches the customer, and choosing direct or indirect sales is a real trade-off between control and risk. Strategic partners supply expertise and resources the startup lacks, and the business activities and resources depend on where the venture sits on the value chain.

Drucker's five questions

  1. What is our mission?
  2. Who is our customer?
  3. What does our customer value?
  4. What are our results?
  5. What is our plan?

Definitions of the business model

  • Activities view: the activities or tasks a business undertakes, an "analytical device for evaluation and action."
  • Blueprint view: a "blueprint" for running a business.
  • Essence: the strategic decisions and trade-offs entrepreneurs make to earn a profit.

Value proposition

The benefit the customer derives from the offer, the reason they will buy. It is the unique mix of product, price, service, relationship and image offered to targeted clients, and it must explain what the company can do for them better or differently from competitors.

Benefits

Intangibles: better health, saving time and money, reliability.

Features

Attributes of the product: design, physical characteristics, functions.

Customer segments

Segmenting divides a market into meaningful and measurable parts based on needs, buyer behaviors and demographics (age, education, income, ethnicity). A segmentation matrix lists the comparison variables down the left side and the segments across the top.

Distribution channels

The distribution channel answers "How do you deliver the benefit to the customer?" The best option fulfills the customer's expectations about where and how they want to buy. Most services go direct; products often pass through intermediaries such as distributors and retailers. With an intermediary, the intermediary is the direct customer and the end user is the beneficiary.

Strategic partners

Startups commonly partner with existing companies to tap expertise and resources they lack. The most common type is the buyer–supplier relationship, used to cut costs or share facilities such as manufacturing or warehousing. In industries such as electronics, competitors may share resources in one area while competing in another.

Business activities and resource requirements

Depending on the value proposition and the venture's value chain position, activities may involve manufacturing, distribution, research and development (R&D), or a network or technology platform. Each has its own functions and so unique resource requirements critical to delivering the value proposition.

Exam tip: Examiners often ask you to distinguish the business model from the value proposition. The value proposition is the promise to the customer; the business model is the whole machine (partners, activities, channels, revenue and costs) that delivers the promise at a profit. The FreshMart analysis puts it in one line: the promise works, the machine leaks.

Making money: revenue models, cost drivers and changing models

ProblemA venture with one customer type and one revenue stream is fragile, and one whose costs run ahead of revenue cannot last.
Big ideaHealthy models draw revenue from several customer types and products and control the key cost drivers; models must also evolve as conditions change.
How (tools)Five revenue models, three kinds of cost, five cost structures, the cost-driven versus value-driven choice, and five ways business models change.
ResultA capture side of the model that is diversified, cost-aware and ready to adapt.
In plain words

One of the most important parts of a business model is identifying the revenue streams that flow from the products and services offered. A healthy model supports revenue from multiple types of customers and multiple products and services. The revenue model describes the ways the venture plans to make money, and the course names five common categories: subscription or membership, volume or unit-based, licensing and syndication, transaction fees, and advertising. EuroDisneyland's deal shows the power of the choice: Disney earned licensing-style royalties and fees on revenue while the park itself made losses.

On the cost side, the business that keeps costs low enjoys a significant advantage. Every business has key cost drivers, the costs with the biggest impact on total cost. They can be fixed (rent), variable (manufacturing) or nonrecurring (a one-time expense). Depending on the type of business, the cost structure is dominated by marketing or advertising, inventory, office or retail space, a support center, or direct costs.

A strategic choice follows: a cost-driven model keeps costs as low as possible because margins are narrow, while a value-driven model creates premium experiences with personal service and luxury facilities. FreshMart's analysis argues for moving from cost-driven toward a hybrid: still lean on cost, but paid for saving customers time. Finally, business models are never fixed. They evolve, sometimes radically, through circumstances often beyond the entrepreneur's control.

Testing the model: feasibility analysis and the macro environment

ProblemEarly business models are filled with guesses, and most guesses are wrong when tested on real customers.
Big ideaFeasibility analysis tests, evaluates and validates the proposed business model to assess and reduce risk at startup.
How (tools)The four things to understand, the feasibility analysis areas, hypothesis testing in six steps, three critical success questions, macro environmental factors and Taleb's risk barbell.
ResultA go, rethink or stop decision backed by evidence, and a model built to survive change.
In plain words

Feasibility analysis is the testing, evaluation and validation of a proposed business model for a new venture, a tool for assessing and reducing risk at startup. To judge an opportunity's future, the entrepreneur must understand four things: their own capabilities, the capabilities and intentions of competitors, the needs and desires of customers, and the bargaining power they hold in the value chain. The more information acquired, the more likely predictions are close to the mark, risk is reduced and uncertainty is managed.

The method is hypothesis testing. At first the canvas boxes are filled in from current knowledge, so they are hypotheses. The textbook's waiting-in-line app shows an unvalidated model: save time and avoid lines for students, sold direct, with advertising and partner subscriptions as revenue. Each hypothesis needs an experiment, a field test with customers, and a look at how the result changes the rest of the model. The cycle repeats until the full model is validated. Alongside the canvas, three critical success questions must be answered: Is there a big enough market to be viable and grow? Do the capital requirements to reach positive cash flow make sense? Can the right founding team be assembled?

Factors outside the entrepreneur's control also bear on the model: industry factors (supply chain, substitutes, barriers, incumbents), market factors (demand, segments, switching costs, competition) and global economic factors (resources, global and capital markets, regulation, infrastructure). No business model survives first contact with the market unchanged, so the advice is to rethink the model before change forces it. Nassim Taleb's barbell offers a way to do that safely: guard the roughly 90 percent of the business you cannot afford to lose, and take risks with about 10 percent, so the business is antifragile and gains from positive surprises without being destroyed by negative ones.

Four things the entrepreneur must understand

  4. Your bargaining power in the value chain

Developing and testing hypotheses

Hypotheses are what the entrepreneur believes to be true about each component. Most are proven wrong when tested on real customers, so for each one the entrepreneur must decide how it will be tested.

  1. Develop hypothesesabout each component of the business model.
  2. Design an experimentfor each hypothesis.
  3. Testhypotheses with customers in the field.
  4. Consider the impactof field results on all components.
  5. Change the modelbased on results.
  6. Retestnew hypotheses until the full model is validated.

  3. Team: can an appropriate founding team be assembled to execute the concept?

If conditions are not favorable, the tested areas (industry, market, product, pricing and so on) are reviewed to see whether another approach could make the business viable.

Taleb's risk barbell

A business is antifragile when it guards what it cannot afford to lose (perhaps 90 percent of the business) and takes risks with 10 percent, benefiting from the positive side of uncertainty without suffering the negative side. Every business must take risk to grow: sometimes by changing the model to stay competitive, sometimes in response to macro threats, sometimes to seize opportunity. Deciding how much risk the business can afford is part of designing the model.

Drawing conclusions from feasibility analysis

Feasibility analysis converts an uncertain opportunity by acquiring information that answers the questions behind the uncertainty. At each point the entrepreneur judges whether to proceed; each step reveals conditions needed for feasibility, and together these give a level of confidence about the risk and the ability to execute.

Innovating with business models: blue oceans, free users and dominant players

ProblemSome markets are crowded, some customers refuse to pay, and some industries are dominated by giants with strong negotiating power.
Big ideaUncertainty is an opportunity to find new places to compete, and certain model designs suit difficult contexts.
How (tools)Kim and Mauborgne's blue ocean strategy and its four actions, two-sided platforms with advertising, freemium or sponsorship, and value chain positioning against dominant players.
ResultA model that competes where others are not, or that captures value despite free users and powerful incumbents.
In plain words

Entrepreneurs should treat the uncertain future as a chance to find new places to compete. W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne call this "blue ocean strategy": seeking the white spaces where no one is playing, where opportunity lies at the intersection of value and cost. They suggest four actions: eliminate something the industry has always done, reduce something below the industry standard, raise something above it, and create something the industry has never done.

The range of possible business models is endless, but two contexts are especially challenging. The first is where customers do not want to pay. Social networking sites are two-sided platforms: the platform is the intermediary and users provide the content that gives it value. Because users contribute freely, they do not expect to pay, so the entrepreneur must monetize some other way. Advertising has been the traditional answer, but it is under attack from both advertisers and users. Alternatives are a freemium model (basic use free, a subscription for enhanced features) and sponsorship (an entity funds the network so it can stay free to users).

The second context is facing dominant players. Startups competing for value against large firms with strong negotiating power often struggle to find the right position on the value chain, and each position has long-term consequences. In such industries, startups often focus on acquiring intellectual property (IP) and know-how they control and can develop until it is attractive to one of the dominant firms, as an acquisition or licensing target.

Blue ocean strategy: the four actions

  1. Eliminatesomething the industry has traditionally done.
  2. Reducesomething below the industry standard.
  3. Raisesomething above the industry standard.
  4. Createsomething the industry has never done.

Facing dominant players

The sample value chain for devices runs: research and development → parts suppliers → engineering and design → manufacture and assembly → distribution. In plain words

FreshMart is a fictional chain of 25 stores near residential areas and university campuses. It sells fresh food, household products and ready-to-eat meals to university students and young working adults who want convenient shopping at affordable prices. It buys from suppliers and resells; main revenue is product sales, so profit depends on selling prices and purchasing costs. Customers shop in store or order through an app for pickup, or for delivery at an extra fee, and free membership gives points and personalized offers. The challenges are high delivery costs on small orders, unsold fresh food and customers switching for discounts. Management is weighing paid membership with delivery benefits and a minimum order, weekly meal bundles ordered in advance, or discounted food nearing expiry.

Designing new products and why they fail

ProblemBetween 70 and 90 percent of new consumer products fail, and startups develop products with limited budgets, people and time.
Big ideaDesign decides features, reliability, price, appeal and up to 80 percent of final cost; most failures trace back to weak market and industry analysis and to resource shortfalls.
How (tools)The definition of product design and its seven elements, the product life cycle, the innovation pipeline, the main reasons for failure and the five consequences of resource shortfalls.
ResultA product strategy that strengthens its weakest link before launch.
In plain words

Every business, large or small, product or service, is involved in design and development at every stage of its life. The goal of product development is to bring new products to market at the right time and cost, so customers pay a price that reflects the value they perceive. That is hard for startups, which are limited by budgets, people and time.

Product design transforms a set of requirements into an integrated whole: form, texture, color, fit, user interfaces, production and assembly processes, and the methods for joining the parts. Design determines features and performance, reliability, price and appeal, and above all cost, as much as 80 percent of the final cost. Technology has shortened product life cycles, so entrepreneurs must manage an ongoing innovation pipeline rather than a single product, and must find the weakest link in their new product strategy.

Failure rates for new consumer products sit between 70 and 90 percent, varying by industry and product. The main reason is a lack of good market and industry analysis. Innovators may create value for customers but fail to capture enough of it, so imitators and makers of complementary products take the returns. Other reasons are the industry's architecture (a firm offering only part of the solution, like an independent film studio acting as a system integrator, may see the film make money while the studio does not), technical problems from a poorly defined problem, and insufficient resources for research and development (R&D). Shortfalls in new product development (NPD) resources produce five common problems.

Product design and its elements

Product design: the activity that transforms a set of requirements into a format that brings all the elements into an integrated whole or system. The system consists of:

  1. Form
  2. Texture
  3. Color
  4. Fit
  5. User interfaces
  6. Production and assembly processes to make the parts
  7. Methods for joining the parts together

Why design matters

  • Determines features and performance, reliability, price and customer appeal.
  • Determines cost: as much as 80 percent of the final cost.
  • Product life cycle: like an industry life cycle, with new product development, market introduction, growth, maturity and decline; technology has shortened it.
  • Innovation pipeline: staying competitive means a continuing flow of products, not one product.

From idea to market: the product development cycle

ProblemProducts built before the customer and solution are validated waste scarce money and time.
Big ideaProduct development runs eight tasks in three groups (customer discovery, customer validation and design, business creation), often in parallel or out of order.
How (tools)The eight tasks, six solution checks, the new product checklist, prototypes, four ways to commercialize, and the product development trade-offs.
ResultA tested product and business model ready for a launch strategy.
In plain words

The product development cycle is a series of tasks leading to the product's introduction. All the tasks happen, but often in parallel or out of order. The eight tasks fall into three categories, each answering one question. Customer identification or discovery: who is the primary customer and what do they need? Customer validation and design: how can we design, develop and test the product, market and business model? Business creation: what should the launch strategy be?

Discovery starts with a problem, which may point to an unserved niche, an improvement on an existing product, or a breakthrough. Validation checks whether the solution already exists, whether it really solves the problem, whether it can be produced and protected, what it will cost and how long it will take. Preliminary drawings support cost estimates and, for technology, patent applications. The prototype that follows usually works like the final product even if it does not look like it. For service, retail or wholesale businesses the prototype is a design, storyboard or flowchart of how the service reaches the customer. A prototype can be field-tested with users where the product would normally be used; for software, a beta version goes to a limited group of early adopters.

The final phase is the go-to-market strategy. The entrepreneur chooses how to commercialize: license the technology, start a business to make and distribute it, sell the technology, or form a joint venture with a company that has the needed resources. The operations, marketing and funding plans built along the way come together in a complete, tested business model. Throughout, trade-offs must be made among features, cost, capital expenditure, development cost and timing: something must be sacrificed to gain something else.

Building a minimum viable product and outsourcing development

ProblemBuilding a complete product before learning what customers want is slow and costly, but an overly simple test product can mislead.
Big ideaA minimum viable product (MVP) carries just enough functionality to attract early adopters whose feedback shapes the final product; how minimal it can be depends on where the idea sits on the innovation spectrum.
How (tools)The MVP definition and goals, the innovation spectrum, rules for outsourcing product development, and its risks.
ResultFaster, cheaper validation with the right partners, without losing control of the customer or the intellectual property.
In plain words

A minimum viable product (MVP) is the version of a product with just enough functionality and value proposition to attract early adopters, who give feedback and help shape the final product for the mass market. The MVP aims to attract initial customers who can guide the product; the final product aims to attract passionate users who refer it to others. If early adopters dislike the MVP, that does not necessarily mean the product is not viable; different customer groups may have different needs.

The MVP approach does not suit every product. Near the incremental end of the innovation spectrum, customers have real problems, quickly judge whether a solution works, and have alternatives from major competitors, so a simple MVP will not get useful feedback; going out with a complete product may make more sense. Near the disruptive end, there is no direct competition at first and customers do not expect perfection from something novel, so building the MVP in stages from feedback is more valuable. Either way, the number of iterations does not create a viable product; recognizing when customers say it is viable does.

Many companies outsource all or part of product development to keep pace with fast innovation and short windows of opportunity. Outsourcing can reduce risk, lower costs and cut cycle times by 60 to 90 percent, and it gives a young firm a network of expertise it could not afford to hire. But the course adds clear rules and warnings: do not hand both design and development to one firm unless it specializes in both, never choose on price alone, keep core research and development close to the customer, and be careful about intellectual property in regions where piracy is rampant.

The MVP

  • Definition: the version of a product with just enough functionality and value proposition to attract early adopters who give feedback and help develop the final mass-market product.
  • Goal of the MVP: attract initial customers who can guide the product's direction.
  • Goal of the final product: attract passionate users and customers who refer it to others.
  • Mixed feedback: early adopters disliking the MVP does not prove it is not viable; needs may differ by customer group.
  • Finishing: once the MVP fits the target market, complete the product so it reflects what testing taught.

Outsourcing product development

Outsourcing can reduce risk, lower costs and decrease cycle times by 60 to 90 percent, and it provides a network of expertise. Offshore outsourcing generally aims to cut costs or, for medical products, speed up clinical trials.

Areas suitable for outsourcing

  • Component design
  • Materials specifications
  • Machinery to process
  • Ergonomic design
  • Packaging design
  • Assembly drawings and specifications
  • Parts and material sourcing (suppliers)
  • Operator's and owner's manuals

Rules of thumb for using development service firms

  1. Split design and development between firms unless one firm specializes in both.
  2. Spend enough time on design before building or coding.
  3. Break development into meaningful parts so early tests are cheap.

Is outsourcing right for the business?

  • Never choose on price alone: the lowest prices often carry the highest risk.
  • Keep core research and development close to the customer: development is fueled by customer needs and insights.
  • Distance is costly: managing teams at a distance, or scattered around the globe, is harder and more expensive than onsite.
  • Protect intellectual property: patents, trade secrets and copyrights are hard to protect where piracy is rampant.

What a venture knows and owns: knowledge, intellectual property and trade secrets

ProblemA venture's most valuable assets are often intangible, and intangible assets are easy to lose or copy.
Big ideaKnowledge rises from tacit intellectual capital to documented intellectual assets to intellectual property (IP), which carries legal rights; every business has some IP.
How (tools)The three categories of knowledge, the nature of IP rights, the IP pyramid, and trade secrets with their protection and remedies.
ResultAn inventory of what the venture knows and which parts deserve which level of protection.
In plain words

The course distinguishes three categories of knowledge by level of protection and value. Intellectual capital is the most common: tacit knowledge acquired in the daily activity of doing business, such as how an experienced cook judges a batch. Intellectual assets are explicit knowledge, documented in a physical form that is easy to communicate and share, such as a written recipe or procedures manual. Intellectual property (IP) is the highest form of protection and the most valuable, because it carries exclusive legal rights with remedies through the courts for protecting intangible assets.

Every business, however small, has IP rights: a trademark on its name or brand, copyright on its advertising design, perhaps a patent on a device, and trade secrets such as its customer list. IP resembles personal property: it can be bought, sold, donated, licensed or exchanged, and its owner has the right and the duty to defend it against infringement or theft. The striking difference is that IP is intangible; it is a right under the law. To exercise the right, the owner must put the IP into some physical form.

The IP pyramid ranks the four forms by degree of protection: trade secrets at the base, then copyrights, trademarks, and patents at the top. Trade secrets are the least protected because no patent or trademark law covers them; the only protection is confidentiality agreements or contracts. If someone who signed such an agreement misappropriates the secret, the owner can sue and, if successful, obtain an injunction, damages and attorney's fees, within a statute of limitations that varies by state.

Three categories of knowledge

  1. Intellectual capitalThe most common form; essentially tacit knowledge acquired in the daily activity of doing business.
  2. Intellectual assetsExplicit knowledge, documented in physical form; easy to communicate and share.
  3. Intellectual propertyThe highest form of protection and most valuable; exclusive legal rights with court remedies for protecting intangible assets.

The nature of intellectual property rights

  • Who qualifies: a unique device, novel process or service, or other proprietary item may qualify for IP rights.
  • Examples in every business: a trademark on the business or brand name; copyrights on advertising design; patents on an invented device; trade secrets such as the customer list.
  • Like personal property: can be bought, sold, donated, licensed or exchanged; the owner has the right and duty to defend it.
  • Unlike physical property: intangible, a right under the law, but must be put into some physical form to be exercised.

The IP pyramid (degree of protection, low to high)

Trade secrets → copyrights → trademarks → patents.

Trade secrets

Under the Uniform Trade Secrets Act, a trade secret is a formula, device, idea, process, pattern or compilation of information that (1) gives the owner a competitive advantage in the marketplace, (2) is novel in the sense of not being common knowledge, and (3) is kept in a reasonably confidential state.

Examples

  • Survey methods used by professional pollsters
  • Customer lists
  • Source codes for computer chips
  • Customer discounts
  • Inventions for which no patent will be applied

Protecting trade secrets

  • No legal means under patent and trademark law; the only way is confidentiality agreements or contracts.
  • People who regularly work with entrepreneurs and inventors generally will not sign confidentiality agreements, because doing so puts them in legal jeopardy.
  • Remedies after misappropriation by a signatory: injunctive relief, damages and attorney's fees.
  • A statute of limitations applies to claims and varies by state.
Update: Since the Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016, owners in the United States can also bring a federal civil claim for trade secret misappropriation, with a three-year limitation period, in addition to state law. The course's point stands: there is no registration system, so confidentiality agreements remain the main protection.
Exam tip: When advising a small food or service venture, start at the base of the pyramid: the recipe or process is a trade secret protected by confidentiality agreements, the brand is a trademark, the packaging and content are copyright, and only a genuinely new invention justifies a patent.

Protecting the brand and the content: trademarks and copyrights

ProblemA brand or creative work that others can copy loses its value, and protection rules differ by country and language.
Big ideaTrademarks protect the signs that identify a business in commerce for as long as they are used; copyrights protect the tangible form of original works for a fixed term.
How (tools)Trademark definition, registration steps and limits, infringement and dilution; copyright rights, terms, fair use, the Digital Millennium Copyright Act and the Berne Convention.
ResultA registered, distinctive brand tested in each target language, and creative work marked and registered for full protection.
In plain words

A trademark is a symbol, logo, word, sound, color, design or other device used to identify a business or product in commerce: the FedEx logo, L'Oréal's slogan "Because you're worth it," Coca-Cola's bottle shape. The term also covers service marks, which identify services. A color can be a trademark if it has secondary meaning, as pink has for one brand of insulation, but a logo's background color alone cannot (the green of Starbucks' logo cannot be trademarked; the whole logo can). A trademark lasts as long as it is used and renewal fees are paid, unless it becomes generic, as "aspirin" and "thermos" did.

In the United States a trademark cannot be registered until it is in use, though an intent-to-use application can be filed with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO); until registration the owner places the trademark symbol (TM), or the service mark symbol (SM) for services, after the name, and after registration the ® symbol. Registration is not required but gives enhanced rights: national scope, a place in the USPTO database, the ability to record the mark with United States Customs and Border Protection to block counterfeit imports, and a basis for registering abroad. For an international venture the most practical step is to test the mark's translation in the languages of target countries.

A copyright protects original works of authors, composers, screenwriters and programmers. It protects not the idea but its tangible form, which cannot be copied without permission. The course lists six rights of the copyright owner, a term of life plus 70 years, and limits such as fair use. Digital content brings new problems, addressed in the United States by the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) of 1998. Across borders, copyright is fairly consistent thanks to treaties, above all the Berne Convention.

Trademarks

  • Definition: a symbol, logo, word, sound, color, design or other device that identifies a business or product in commerce. Examples: FedEx logo; L'Oréal slogan; Coca-Cola bottle shape.
  • Service mark: identifies services, activities performed for the benefit of others, for pay or otherwise.
  • Color: protectable with secondary meaning (people associate the color with a product, such as pink insulation); a logo's background color alone is not.
  • Life: exclusive right while actively used and renewal fees are paid; lost if the mark becomes generic (aspirin, thermos).
  • Use requirement: cannot be registered in the United States until in use; cannot be stockpiled and sold; file an intent-to-use application with the USPTO and use TM or SM until registered; ® means registered.

Marks that cannot be trademarked

  • Anything immoral or deceptive
  • Official symbols of the United States or any state or municipality, such as the flag
  • A person's name or likeness without permission

Infringement, dilution and counterfeiting

  • Infringement: a mark likely to cause confusion with an existing mark; identical marks can coexist where goods or services are unrelated. A strong mark is "inherently distinctive" and "identifies the source of goods."
  • Dilution: the mark's value is substantially reduced through competition or likely confusion with another mark.
  • Counterfeiting: deliberate copying of a mark, subject to civil and criminal penalties.

Why register, and the four steps

Registration gives national scope for enforcement, a USPTO database entry that warns others, recording with United States Customs and Border Protection against counterfeit imports, and help when registering abroad.

  1. Searchexisting marks at the USPTO site and in Internet and business name databases.
  2. Pick a distinctive marknot common or closely descriptive; descriptive marks are weak and costly to protect.
  3. Test the translationin the languages of every country where the mark will be protected.
  4. Choose the categoriesof products and services to protect.

Copyrights

Copyright protects original works of authors, composers, screenwriters and computer programmers; it protects the tangible form, not the idea.

The six rights (taught as the First Sale Doctrine, Section 106 of the 1976 Copyright Act)

  1. Reproduction
  2. Preparation of derivative works
  3. Distribution
  4. Public performance
  5. Public display
  6. Digital transmission performance
Update: In the Copyright Act itself, Section 106 lists these six exclusive rights, while the first-sale doctrine is a separate provision (Section 109) that limits the distribution right once a lawful copy has been sold. Obtaining protection

  • The work must be in a fixed and tangible form: someone must be able to see or hear it.
  • Notice is not legally required, but registering at the Copyright Office of the Library of Congress in Washington, District of Columbia (DC) gives full protection by creating a document trail.
  • Notice format: the word "copyright" or ©, the year of first publication, and the owner's full name, for example "© 2018 Kathleen Allen."
  • Berne Convention: members (more than 100 nations, per the slides) must protect authors from any member country for at least the life of the author plus 50 years.
  • On discovering infringement, put the party on notice with a cease and desist letter, or take stronger action if the infringement will damage the company.
Update: The Berne Convention now has more than 180 contracting parties, so "more than 100 nations" understates its reach today.

Protecting inventions: patents, from criteria to infringement

ProblemBringing an invention to market takes time and money, and without protection imitators can copy it as soon as it appears.
Big ideaA patent gives a temporary, exclusionary right (usually 20 years from application) to stop others making, using or selling the invention, in exchange for full public disclosure.
How (tools)When to patent, economic life, the four criteria, three patent types, provisional and nonprovisional applications, the America Invents Act (AIA), the utility patent process, maintenance fees and infringement.
ResultA patent decision timed to the invention's economic life, with claims broad enough to resist design-arounds.
In plain words

A patent is the primary means of protecting an original invention. It lets the holder defend the invention against anyone who would make, use or sell it during the patent period, for most patents 20 years from the date of application. The right is exclusionary: the holder can "exclude others from making, using, offering for sale, or selling" the invention in the United States. When the patent ends, the invention enters the public domain.

Not every invention deserves a patent. The course gives four signals: the invention solves a significant problem and changes how things are done; it falls under United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulation; it will earn more than the potential cost of enforcing the patent; and there is a plan to license it. The deciding idea is economic life, the period in which the invention can generate revenue. In a fast-changing field, a patent may outlive the invention's economic life and waste money; for inventions needing years of development and regulatory approval, patents are essential.

To qualify, an invention must meet four United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) criteria: it fits a statutory class, has utility, is novel (no prior art) and is not obvious. There are three patent types (utility, plant and design). Inventors often file a cheap provisional application first, which allows "patent pending" for 12 months, and then the nonprovisional application that can actually issue. Since the AIA, the United States is a first-inventor-to-file country, so the filing date decides disputes. A patent protects only within the country that grants it, which is why international filing under the Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) matters for international ventures.

Signals that a patent may be needed

  1. Does the invention solve a significant problem and change the way things are done?
  2. Does it fall under FDA regulations?
  3. Will revenues exceed the potential cost of patent enforcement?
  4. Is there a plan to license it to a third party?

Economic life (the ability to generate revenue) is critical: in rapidly changing fields the patent may outlive it; for long, regulated development, filing is essential.

Four criteria for patentability

  1. Statutory classFits one of four classes (or is a new use of one): machine (moving parts or circuitry: fax, rocket, photocopier, laser, electronic circuit); process or method (chemical reaction, method for producing products); article of manufacture (furniture, transistor, diskette, toy); composition of matter (gasoline, food additive, drug, genetically altered life form).
  2. UtilityIt must be useful, and the utility must be real, not speculative, and described in the application.
  3. Novelty (no prior art)Prior art is knowledge publicly available or published before the filing date. Novelty means physical differences, new combinations of components or new uses.
  4. NonobviousnessNot obvious to someone of ordinary skill in the field; it must show "new and unexpected results," not the next logical step.

Provisional or nonprovisional?

Provisional application

  • A first filing at lower cost than a formal application
  • Allows the term patent pending; protects small inventors while they talk to manufacturers
  • Lasts 12 months from filing; cannot be extended
  • The 12 months do not count toward the 20-year term, so it effectively extends protection by one year
  • If no nonprovisional is filed in time, it is abandoned and the earlier date is lost

Nonprovisional application

  • Required for any patent to issue; lasts 20 years from filing
  • A complete description: what the invention does, how it differs from prior art, its scope
  • Detailed drawings and specifications so a person of ordinary skill could build it
  • Claims: the novel parts to be patented, with at least one claim showing novelty, utility and nonobviousness; specific enough to show uniqueness, broad enough to stop slight-modification copies
  • The invention must be reduced to practice: described so someone could build it and prove it works

The America Invents Act

The America Invents Act (AIA) of 2013 made the United States a first-inventor-to-file country instead of first-to-invent. The focus moved from the date of conception to the filing date: barring prior art, whichever of two inventors files first wins.

Update: The AIA was signed into law in September 2011; its first-inventor-to-file provisions took effect on 16 March 2013, which is the date the slides refer to.

The patent process

  • Publication: most applications are published 18 months after filing. Until then the inventor may market and sell the product labeled "patent pending." Once issued, the application and patent become public record.
  • Appeals: if the USPTO rejects a modified claim, the inventor can appeal to a Board of Patent Appeals within the USPTO, then to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit; this may take years.
  • Maintenance fees: due at 3½, 7½ and 11½ years after grant; failure to pay can end the patent.
Update: Since September 2012 the USPTO's appeal board is the Patent Trial and Appeal Board (PTAB), which replaced the Board of Patent Appeals and Interferences.

Patent infringement

  • Infringement: someone other than the holder or a licensee makes and sells a product containing every element of a claim.
  • Doctrine of equivalents: protects inventors against infringers who make small, insignificant changes to the claims.
  • Infringement actions are costly and difficult; courts tend to favor the inventor and may issue injunctions before trial; willful infringement can bring triple damages.

Intellectual property strategy and protecting assets abroad

ProblemRights granted in one country stop at its border, and waiting too long to file can destroy novelty or priority.
Big ideaAn intellectual property (IP) strategy decides what to protect, with which right, and when, and extends protection to target markets through international routes.
How (tools)Timing under first-to-file, intent-to-use trademark filings, three patent strategies, and international filing under the Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT), plus copyright treaties and trademark translation checks.
ResultA protection plan matched to the business model and to every country the venture plans to enter.
In plain words

Understanding what to protect and with which type of protection is half the battle; knowing when to protect and enforce IP is critical to an effective long-term strategy. Patents are the most time-sensitive form, especially now that the United States uses first-to-file: the longer an entrepreneur waits, the greater the chance the invention is no longer novel. Trademarks work similarly. The first to file holds the presumptive right to stop confusing marks, and because intent-to-use applications can be filed before a mark is in use, the earliest filing date usually wins a dispute.

Research finds three patent strategies that align with particular business models. A proprietary strategy acquires the patents needed to cover the opportunity and defends the market position. A defensive strategy gives freedom to design around existing patents or builds a portfolio to use as bargaining chips in cross-licensing agreements. A leveraging strategy suits firms that do not seek freedom to operate or for which proprietary protection is not critical.

For an international venture, geography is the final issue. A United States patent has no effect in any foreign country, so someone could make and sell the product abroad without consequence. The PCT allows one international application covering all member countries; filed within 12 months of the first filing, it keeps that first date, and the applicant then has 30 months to choose countries for the national phase, paying fees and following each country's rules. Copyright is more uniform thanks to the Berne Convention, and trademarks need registration in each market, with the mark's translation tested in each language. Week 6's warning applies too: outsourcing to regions where piracy is rampant makes protection much harder.

Timing

  • Patents: the most time-sensitive form under first-to-file; delay risks loss of novelty.
  • Trademarks: the first to file holds the presumptive right to stop confusing marks; intent-to-use applications allow filing before use; the earliest filing date usually wins.

In plain words

All three cases in the course end the same way: a decision is needed and the evidence is incomplete. EuroDisneyland must decide how to turn around, LocalBite whether and how to start, FreshMart which model change to make. A good answer moves through the course in order. It names the opportunity or problem (Weeks 1 to 3), tests it against the industry and the market (Week 4), lays out the business model and its numbers (Week 5), judges feasibility and states the riskiest assumption (Week 5), proposes how to validate it with a prototype, minimum viable product (MVP) or pilot (Week 6), and protects what makes the venture hard to copy (Week 7). It ends with a clear recommendation.

Two habits lift an answer from adequate to excellent. The first is quantifying: the spend-to-admission ratio at EuroDisney, the break-even basket at FreshMart, the market sizing in the LocalBite blueprint. The second is sequencing: EuroDisney's turnaround must fix cash before product before reinvestment; FreshMart must test in two stores before 25; LocalBite must test with 30 to 50 customers before writing a plan. The recurring message of the course is to test before you scale.

Finally, the international angle should be explicit. A model that works at home is a hypothesis abroad: Tokyo welcomed an American copy, France resisted one. Culture, climate, income, holiday patterns, language and law all change the business model, which is why the course places validation "across cultures" and asset protection "abroad" at its end.

Worked outline: "Should this venture expand to a new country, and how?"

  1. Restate the opportunityName the unmet need in the new country and whether it was discovered (market shift) or created.
  2. Test the environmentIndustry life-cycle stage, five forces, macro factors (economy, regulation, infrastructure), and the culture and climate differences that matter.
  3. Re-draw the modelValue proposition, segment and first customer, channel (direct or through a partner), revenue model and cost drivers in the new market.
  4. Name the riskiest assumptionWrite it as a hypothesis with a measure and a pass condition.
  5. Validate smallPrototype, MVP or pilot with local users; keep about 90 percent of the business safe (barbell).
  6. ProtectRegister the trademark locally after a translation check; file under the PCT within 12 months if there is an invention; use confidentiality agreements for trade secrets.
  7. RecommendA clear decision, the sequence of steps and the reason for the order.
