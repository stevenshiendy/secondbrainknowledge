Session 1–2 · CILO 1: fundamental principles

What a supply chain is and what flows in it

ProblemFirms no longer compete alone; products pass through many organisations before reaching the consumer.
Big ideaManage the whole network, not just your own company.
How (tools)3 flows, 6 foundational premises, logistics as the material link.
ResultValue created, efficiency raised, customers satisfied.
In plain words

Think of a bottle of Aqua water. It involves a plastic maker, a bottling plant, a distributor (often a third-party logistics provider, or 3PL), a minimarket and you. That whole network is the supply chain. Logistics is the part that physically moves and stores the bottles, aiming to deliver the "7 Rights": the right product, in the right way, quantity and quality, at the right place and time, to the right customer, at the right cost.

Supply chain management is bigger. It manages the relationships between all those firms and three flows: materials (moving downstream to the customer), information (orders, forecasts and point-of-sale (POS) checkout data, moving both ways) and resources such as money, people and equipment (payments move upstream). So logistics is one component inside SCM, a point the slides stress.

Logistics and supply chain management (LSCM) grew important because of six developments: freight became less transport-intensive, product prices fell (squeezing margins), transport was deregulated, productivity rose, firms focused on cutting inventory, and company structures changed (more outsourcing). The six foundational premises (FP) describe how a chain behaves: it is a network that no single firm fully controls, and each firm sees only part of it.

Must-know definitions

  • Supply chain: the network of organisations linked upstream and downstream that produce value for the ultimate consumer.
  • SCM purpose: create value, enhance efficiency, satisfy customers.
  • Value chain vs supply chain: the value chain (activities and competencies that add value) creates the flows; the supply chain operationalises them.
  • Sector names: farm to fork (food), sketch to store (clothing), dust to rust (cars and machinery), teeth to tail (military).
  • Applications: cost savings plus service add-ons (after-sales, delivery). IKEA flat-pack; hospital triage that matches patients to the right care stream early.

Six developments behind LSCM

  • Reduced transport intensity of freight
  • Falling product prices
  • Deregulation of transport
  • Productivity improvements
  • Emphasis on inventory reduction
  • Changes in company structure

Six foundational premises: read them as one argument

  1. NetworkFP1: nodes (firms) and links (flows).
  2. ComplexFP2: a complex adaptive system; tension between control and emergence.
  3. RelativeFP3: a chain is defined from one product and one agent.
  4. Two layersFP4: physical chain + support chain (banks, IT, logistics providers).
  5. Limited viewFP5: bounded by what the focal firm can see.
  6. Fading viewFP6: the view fades with physical distance, cultural distance and closeness centrality.
Exam tip: For "distinguish logistics and SCM", describe the diagram: logistics = the material links (7 Rights); SCM = the whole box, including relationships and all three flows.

Session 3–4 · CILO 1

Making things without waste: lean

ProblemMass production gave low cost but huge stocks, defects and slow change.
Big ideaProduce only what the customer pulls; anything that adds no value is waste.
How (tools)5 lean principles, 7+1 wastes, just-in-time, level production, quality tools.
ResultLower cost and inventory, better quality, faster response.
In plain words

Production evolved from craft (custom, one by one) to mass production (Henry Ford: standard products, economies of scale), to flexible manufacturing, to lean (Toyota), and now to Industry 4.0 (automation, digital, sustainability). Each step tried to fix the weakness of the one before.

Mass production is a push system: you build to a forecast and hold the result as stock, called make-to-stock (MTS). If the forecast is wrong, you get piles of unsold goods. Lean flips this to pull: production starts only when a real order arrives, called make-to-order (MTO), with materials arriving just-in-time (JIT) and production kept level (even, with no idle time and no surges).

Lean sees anything the customer would not pay for as waste (Japanese muda). Removing waste lowers cost and speeds flow. Because quality and price are now expected by every buyer, the slides say "order winners have become order qualifiers": firms now compete through their whole supply chain. Quality approaches here are total quality management (TQM), kaizen (Japanese for continuous small improvement) and Lean Six Sigma.

Session 5–6 · CILO 1

Running the chain: processes, IT and analytics

ProblemMany partners, lots of data, but poor visibility and slow decisions (e.g. aerospace before AirSupply).
Big ideaManage processes systematically and decide with data.
How (tools)Process management, information systems, problem-solving cycles, analytics, simulation, optimisation.
ResultVisibility, collaboration, lower inventory and cost, faster response.
In plain words

AirSupply is the opening case: one online platform where aerospace makers (Airbus and its suppliers) share forecasts, track orders and run vendor-managed inventory (VMI), where the supplier watches and refills the customer’s stock. It works as early warning, and everyone sees problems before they become shortages.

Three ideas make this work. Business process management (BPM) treats each process (procurement, order fulfilment) as something to model, analyse, redesign, execute and monitor in a loop. Management information systems (MIS) collect the data: enterprise resource planning (ERP) software, supplier portals, internet of things (IoT) sensors, cloud platforms. Business analytics turns that data into decisions, measured with key performance indicators (KPIs).

When something goes wrong (bottlenecks, stockouts, delays), you use problem-solving cycles. Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) runs quick small experiments. Six Sigma’s Define-Measure-Analyse-Improve-Control (DMAIC) is more data-heavy. Root-cause tools include the 5 Whys and the fishbone diagram. For big design questions, simulation tests "what if" safely, and optimisation (linear or integer programming) finds the best answer under constraints.

Systems and models

  • MIS types: ERP, SCM execution systems, supplier portals → real-time visibility and exception management.
  • Technologies: IoT sensors for inventory tracking, cloud platforms, artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) for forecasting.
  • Simulation (discrete-event, agent-based; software AnyLogic, Arena, Simul8): capacity planning, risk evaluation, layout tests.
  • Optimisation: network design (where to put warehouses), inventory (holding vs stockout cost), scheduling.
  • Emerging: digital twins (live virtual copy of the chain), blockchain (secure shared records), AI, sustainability analytics.
  • Research methods: quantitative (statistics, optimisation), qualitative (interviews, case studies), mixed methods.

4 challenges

  • Data quality & integration: incomplete or inconsistent data; partners' IT systems don't talk → silos.
  • Modelling complexity: global, dynamic, uncertain chains are hard to model; needs computing power and expertise.
  • Resistance to change: fear of job loss, habit, low trust, little training.
  • Robustness vs agility: robustness needs spare capacity/stock (cost); agility needs flexible processes (complex). Models must balance cost, service, risk and flexibility.
Exam tip: Name the analytics type and give one supply chain example each. Link predictive to forecasting and prescriptive to optimisation.

Session 7–8 · CILO 1

Choosing a strategy: outsourcing, lean vs agile, leagile

ProblemOne chain cannot be both cheapest and fastest for every product.
Big ideaMatch the chain to the product and decide what to do yourself vs buy in.
How (tools)Outsourcing + service level agreements, supplier selection, offshoring, postponement, leagile.
ResultCost efficiency where demand is stable, agility where it is not.
In plain words

Strategy flows top-down: corporate strategy → business strategy → functional strategies such as logistics. The first big decision is make or buy. Outsourcing transfers a process to another company, often a third-party logistics provider (3PL) that runs transport or warehousing. A firm that outsources almost everything is a virtual organisation. To keep control, the buyer writes a service level agreement (SLA): a contract that sets measurable standards (on-time delivery %, inventory accuracy, response speed) and what happens if they are missed.

Relationships with suppliers mature over four stages, and suppliers are organised in tiers: tier 1 supplies you directly, tier 2 supplies tier 1. A module is a complete sub-assembly, such as a car dashboard, supplied by a lower tier. Offshoring is different from outsourcing: it moves work to another country, usually for lower cost, and brings culture, time-zone and regulation challenges.

The core trade-off is lean vs agile. Lean is cheapest when demand is stable; agile (responsive, flexible) is needed when demand is unpredictable. Leagile combines them: be lean upstream, where you make a generic base product, and agile downstream, where you customise it to real orders. The switch point is the decoupling point. Postponement deliberately delays customisation as far downstream as possible. Examples: paint mixed in-store to the customer’s colour, or a laptop configured after the order. Postponement is what enables mass customisation.

What an SLA contains

  • Roll-out and duration of the service
  • Scope of services
  • Areas of responsibility and legal requirements
  • Performance metrics
  • Termination / exit arrangements

Outsourcer–supplier relationship stages

  1. Master-servanttransactional; buyer dictates, one-way
  2. Consultativesupplier input asked; buyer still leads
  3. Peer-to-peerequals; trust, joint decisions, shared risk & reward
  4. Competitiveseveral suppliers compete to drive improvement

Supplier selection (order qualifiers): capability, delivery reliability, quality certifications, conformance to specifications, delivery lead time, financial capability, performance track record, price or cost reduction, senior management attitude, responsiveness to demand uncertainty, ethical requirements.

  • Responsiveness = meeting customer demand quickly. Agility = adapting to market changes and disruptions. Modern strategy balances both against cost.
  • Integration & collaboration: real-time information sharing, joint planning, operational coordination → better visibility, less inefficiency, faster response.
Exam tip: In any leagile or postponement answer, draw the triangle: lean on the left, agile on the right, decoupling point = generic stock, postponement pushes it right. Fisher's matrix (Strategy toolbox) explains why.

Session 9–10 · CILO 2: analyse and evaluate

Sustainability: cutting the environmental cost

ProblemMoving freight creates emissions and waste; regulators, investors and customers now demand action.
Big ideaMeasure the footprint across the whole chain, then redesign to reduce it and close the loop.
How (tools)ESG + CSR, Scope 1/2/3, carbon footprint formula, reverse logistics, 3Rs.
ResultLower emissions and risk, better reputation, compliance.
In plain words

Sustainable logistics reduces the environmental and other "disbenefits" of moving freight, so today’s decisions don’t harm future generations. Two frameworks guide companies. Corporate social responsibility (CSR) is the voluntary ethical commitment: the values and vision. Environmental, social and governance (ESG) criteria are the measurable targets that put CSR into practice and let investors check it. In short, CSR is the "why" and ESG the "how much".

To manage emissions you first measure them. Greenhouse gases (GHG) are reported as carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂eq), which converts methane and nitrous oxide into the same warming effect as CO₂. They are split into three scopes by asking "whose activity is it?" Scope 1 is your own fuel, Scope 2 is electricity you buy, and Scope 3 is everyone else in your chain. Scope 3 is the hardest to measure but often the largest share.

Finally, reverse logistics moves products back upstream for return, reuse, repair, remanufacture or recycling. It is the backbone of the circular economy and its 3Rs: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle.

ESG in detail

  • Environmental: carbon emissions, resource use, waste, biodiversity.
  • Social: fair labour, community engagement, human rights.
  • Governance: ethical leadership, legal compliance, stakeholder accountability.
Carbon footprint = Fuel used × Emission factor 1,000 L diesel × 2.68 kg CO₂eq/L ≈ 2,680 kg CO₂eq

About 95% of GHG emissions are CO₂, mostly from burning fossil fuels.

How to improve: redesign supply chains (remove unnecessary freight movements), use less harmful energy, use scale (move freight in larger single loads, which cuts cost and emissions per unit), improve efficiency, and develop more fuel-efficient engines and alternative fuels.

Exam tip: The same truck can be Scope 1 for the carrier and Scope 3 for the retailer that hired it. Always say whose report you mean.

Session 11–12 · CILO 2

Complexity and risk: what breaks the chain

ProblemLong, complex global chains amplify small problems and break under shocks.
Big ideaReduce complexity, see further, build in flexibility and back-ups.
How (tools)Bullwhip fixes, risk management tools, security standards, diversification, VUCA mindset.
ResultA robust and resilient chain.
In plain words

A supply chain sits inside bigger systems (geopolitics, ecosystems, politics, regulation), so shocks from outside hit it. The bullwhip effect shows how fragile a chain is even without shocks. Consumers buy steadily, but each level reacts to the orders of the level below. It adds a safety margin, orders in batches, stocks up on promotions and over-orders when it fears a shortage. So small consumer changes grow into wild swings at the factory.

Complexity makes this worse: too many nodes, steps, products, components, customer options, suppliers, organisational layers or data streams. Three terms describe how a chain copes. Vulnerability is how likely it is to be damaged or disrupted. Robustness is handling normal demand fluctuations. Resilience is returning to the original or desired state after a disturbance.

Supply chain risk management (SCRM) uses visibility, flexibility, postponement, multiple sourcing, buffers and joint planning. The world is also VUCA: volatile, uncertain, complex and ambiguous. The suggested response is vision, understanding, clarity and agility.

Session 13–14 · CILO 2–3: apply to improve efficiency

Buying globally: international purchasing

ProblemBuying across borders adds distance, currencies, laws, documents and payment risk.
Big ideaTreat purchasing as a planned process integrated with logistics and finance.
How (tools)8-step process, logistics providers, trade terms, payment methods, duty-free storage.
ResultReliable, lower-cost supply with controlled risk.
In plain words

International purchasing is obtaining goods or services from a market that requires crossing international borders. It needs many skills at once: logistics, negotiation, language and culture, technical specifications, supplier audits, standards and trade finance (paying in the buyer’s, seller’s or a third currency).

The buyer follows a planned process (below). Key partners are 3PLs and fourth-party logistics providers (4PL), which integrate and manage the whole chain, often coordinating several 3PLs. Contracts specify Incoterms (International Commercial Terms: standard rules on who pays for and carries the risk of transport at each point). Letter-of-credit documents follow the UCP 600 rules (Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, publication no. 600).

Goods are cleared through customs at seaports or inland container depots (ICD), inland "dry ports". Importers can store goods in a free trade zone (FTZ) or bonded warehouse, paying import duty only when goods are withdrawn. The last piece is money: choosing a payment method, managing foreign exchange (FX) risk, and using trade credit and insurance.

The 8-step process

  1. Identify needsspecification, volume, quality, standards
  2. Find suppliersdirectories, associations, exhibitions; choose 3PL/4PL
  3. Plan negotiationprice, Incoterms, payment, insurance, documents, delivery
  4. Activate contractdates, quantities, issuing bank, UCP 600 documents
  5. Manage logisticscollection, customs at ICD/seaport, transshipment, FTZ
  6. Track cargoonline tracking by 3PL/4PL
  7. Receive & evaluateinspect quality, pay, clear customs
  8. After-carebenchmark performance, continuous improvement

10 strategic areas

(1) Positioning the firm: in-house vs outsourced; (2) product design strategy; (3) supply chain environment in developed vs less-developed countries; (4) marketing–logistics collaboration; (5) trade-offs: near the market vs distant sourcing with longer lead times but lower cost (Switzerland precision engineering, Italy design, Germany heavy engineering, China low-cost mass production); (6) project management and value engineering; (7) structural planning (containerisation, warehousing, FTZ/ICD, finance, insurance); (8) planning and control; (9) human resource management (HRM); (10) location of factories and distribution centres (DC).

Financing the chain

  • Letter of credit (L/C): the buyer's bank guarantees payment once correct documents are presented. The most common instrument.
  • Documentary collection: banks release shipping documents (needed to claim the goods) only against payment or acceptance.
  • Open account: the buyer pays after receiving goods; highest risk for the supplier.
  • Plus FX management, trade credit and supplier financing, insurance and guarantees, and banks' oversight.

Contract negotiation covers: commercial, technical, legal and transport aspects; standards; sourcing; tendering; supplier audit; costing; quality and dispute procedures; security.

Lecture notes · used across all sessions

Strategy toolbox (Mr. Syamil's notes)

ProblemHow does a firm decide where and how to compete?
Big ideaLook outside (industry), look inside (resources), then position operations to win orders.
How (tools)Porter, resource-based view, order winners/qualifiers, competitive priorities, Fisher, balanced scorecard.
ResultA strategy that operations can actually deliver.
In plain words

Operations management (OM) is the design, operation and improvement of the systems that transform inputs into outputs of greater value. The transformation can be physical (factory), locational (transport), exchange (retail), physiological (hospital), psychological (entertainment) or informational (telecom). Productivity measures how well you transform: output divided by input.

Strategy starts with two views. The outside view is Michael Porter’s Five Forces, which shows how attractive an industry is. The inside view is the resource-based view (RBV): your advantage comes from resources that pass the VRIO test (Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Organised to capture value). Next, decide what customers need just to consider you (order qualifiers) and what makes them choose you (order winners). Then position on cost, speed, quality or flexibility.

Marshall Fisher adds the supply chain link. Products with stable demand (functional) need an efficient chain; products with unpredictable demand (innovative) need a responsive chain. Strategy is turned into action through Hoshin Kanri (Japanese for "policy management", also called policy deployment) and tracked with the balanced scorecard (BSC) and its key performance indicators.

Strategy formulation (Russell & Taylor)

  1. Primary taskwhat business are we in?
  2. Core competencewhat do we do better than anyone? (RBV, VRIO)
  3. Winners & qualifierswhat gets us considered, what wins?
  4. Positioncompete on cost, speed, quality or flexibility

Inside view and Porter

  • RBV (Birger Wernerfelt, Jay Barney); core competence (Gary Hamel & C.K. Prahalad); VRIO example: Starbucks.
  • Focal firm: the dominant player that shapes the chain's design and controls resource flows.
  • Porter also gave: value chain analysis, generic strategies (cost leadership, differentiation, focus), activity mapping (Southwest Airlines), Diamond Model of national competitive advantage.

Competitive priorities with class examples

  • Cost: waste elimination, lean; low-cost carriers.
  • Speed: Zara's 9-day design-to-rack.
  • Quality: Ritz-Carlton's empowered staff.
  • Flexibility: mass customisation; National Bicycle offers 11,231,862 variations within two weeks.
  • Today's winner often becomes tomorrow's qualifier.

Measuring

  • Productivity = output ÷ input. Single-factor: 1,000 units ÷ 40 labour hours = 25 units/hour. Multifactor = output ÷ (labour + materials + overhead), all in money.
  • Competitiveness: how well a nation's goods and services pass the test of international markets.
  • BSC (Robert Kaplan & David Norton): financial, customer, internal process, learning & growth.

Service and industry cases

  • Airlines: hub-and-spoke vs point-to-point; full-service carrier (FSC) vs low-cost carrier (LCC: lower fares, paid extras, secondary airports); yield/revenue management (different prices for the same seat); overbooking (sell 105 tickets for 100 seats because some passengers don't show).
  • Service design: complexity (number of steps) vs divergence (how much each step varies per customer).
  • Focused operations (Shouldice hernia hospital, KFC) vs flexible ones (general hospital).
  • Little's Law: L = λW (average number in system = arrival rate × time in system).

Other names to recognise: Walmart cross-docking (goods move straight from inbound to outbound trucks without storage); VMI (Hytrol case); Eliyahu Goldratt's theory of constraints (TOC); Clayton Christensen's disruptive innovation; quality function deployment (QFD), which turns customer needs into design specs; program evaluation and review technique (PERT), developed for the US Navy Polaris project; the Halal supply chain (Marko Tieman); Paragon/Wardah's founder values. History: craft → division of labour (Adam Smith 1776) → interchangeable parts (Eli Whitney) → scientific management (Frederick Taylor 1911) → moving assembly line (Ford 1913) → quality revolution → lean → ERP and e-commerce → globalisation → sustainability → digital. Keith Oliver of Booz Allen Hamilton coined "supply chain management". Why go global: favourable cost, international markets, responding to demand, reliable supply, latest technology.

Lecture notes · improvement & quality

Improving operations

ProblemProcesses drift, defects appear, customers complain.
Big ideaFind the vital few causes, then improve either radically or continuously.
How (tools)Four improvement approaches, Pareto, 5 Whys, fishbone, PDCA vs DMAIC.
ResultFewer defects, lower cost, happier customers.
In plain words

There are four broad approaches. Business process reengineering (BPR) is radical: throw out the old functional process and rebuild it around the customer (Procter & Gamble’s supply chain, Taco Bell’s kitchens). TQM puts quality at the heart of everything. Lean, from the Toyota Production System (TPS), creates smooth flow synchronised with demand. Six Sigma is a disciplined, data-driven method to cut defects. They overlap and are often combined.

Improvement can be a big leap (innovation/breakthrough) or many small steps (kaizen). To know where to improve, use a Pareto chart: usually about 20% of causes create 80% of problems. Then dig to the root cause with the 5 Whys or a fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram, which groups causes into categories such as people, method, machine and material.

Forum 11 · Excel forecasting

Forecasting: pick the method, then judge it

ProblemEvery plan (production, stock, staff) starts from a demand estimate.
Big ideaLet the pattern in the data choose the method; let the error measures judge it.
How (tools)Moving averages, simple and multiple regression, seasonal indices, error measures.
ResultA forecast you can defend with numbers.
In plain words

Forecasting is the predictive step in analytics. The method depends on the pattern in your past data. With no clear trend, average the recent past using a moving average (MA), or a weighted moving average (WMA) that gives newer months more weight. With a steady trend, fit a straight line through time using simple linear regression. If demand repeats every year, use seasonal indices. If demand depends on several drivers (price, advertising), use multiple regression, which Excel runs through its Analysis ToolPak add-in.

Every forecast is wrong by some amount, called the error (actual − forecast). You judge methods with three measures. Mean absolute deviation (MAD) is the average error size. Mean squared error (MSE) squares errors, so big misses count more. Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) gives the error as a percentage, which is easy to compare across products. The method with the lowest values wins.

1 · Simple linear regression

Ŷ = a + bX b = (Σxy − n·x̄·ȳ) ÷ (Σx² − n·x̄²) a = ȳ − b·x̄

Ŷ ("Y-hat") is the forecast, a is the intercept (value when X = 0), b is the slope (change per period), x̄ and ȳ are the averages.

Linear Programming (LP): define it and give real business cases

What the question really asks

Explain what Linear Programming is, why managers use it, how a problem is built, and give real business cases. A small worked example shows you truly understand it.

Keywords to hit: optimisation, decision variables, objective function, constraints, limited resources, feasible region, corner point, Excel Solver.

Thinking it through, step by step

  1. 1Step 1: What problem do managers face?

    Every firm has limited resources: machine hours, labour, money, truck space, raw material. At the same time it has many possible choices: which products to make, how many, from which factory to ship. Guessing wastes money. The manager needs the single best combination.

  2. 2Step 2: So what is LP?

    Linear Programming (LP) is a mathematical method that finds the best (optimal) decision, either the maximum profit or the minimum cost, while respecting all the limits (constraints). It is called linear because the objective and every constraint are straight-line equations (no squares, no multiplying two variables together). It sits in prescriptive analytics: it does not just describe or predict, it tells you what to do.

  3. 3Step 3: What are the building blocks?

    Every LP model has four parts:

    1. Decision variables: what you control (e.g. T = tables per week, C = chairs per week).
    2. Objective function: what you maximise or minimise (e.g. maximise profit Z = 70T + 50C).
    3. Constraints: the limits (e.g. carpentry hours ≤ 240).
    4. Non-negativity: you cannot make a negative number of products (T, C ≥ 0).

  4. 4Step 4: What must be true for LP to work? (assumptions)

    • Certainty: the numbers (profit per unit, hours per unit) are known and fixed.
    • Proportionality: double the output, double the resource used and the profit earned.
    • Additivity: the total is the sum of the parts (no interaction effects).
    • Divisibility: answers may be fractions (if you need whole numbers you use integer programming).

  5. 5Step 5: How is it solved?

    With two variables you can graph it. Each constraint is a line; the area that satisfies all of them is the feasible region. The optimum always lies on a corner point of that region, so you only need to test the corners. With many variables, managers use Excel Solver (set objective cell, variable cells, constraints, choose "Simplex LP").

  6. 6Step 6: Worked example (classic furniture problem).

    A workshop makes tables (profit $70) and chairs (profit $50).

    • Carpentry: table 4 h, chair 3 h, 240 h available → 4T + 3C ≤ 240
    • Painting: table 2 h, chair 1 h, 100 h available → 2T + C ≤ 100

    Test the corner points:

    Corner (T, C)Profit 70T + 50C
    (0, 0)$0
    (0, 80)$4,000
    (30, 40)$4,100 ← optimum
    (50, 0)$3,500

    How (30, 40) was found: solve both constraints together. From painting, C = 100 − 2T. Put it into carpentry: 4T + 3(100 − 2T) = 240 → 4T + 300 − 6T = 240 → T = 30, so C = 40.

    Decision: make 30 tables and 40 chairs per week for a maximum profit of $4,100. Both resources are fully used (binding constraints).

  7. 7Step 7: Where is LP used in business and supply chains?

    Business problemVariablesObjectiveConstraints
    Product mix (factory deciding what to make)units of each productmax profitmachine hours, labour, materials
    Transportation problem (ship from factories to warehouses)units shipped on each routemin shipping costfactory capacity, warehouse demand
    Blending / diet (animal feed, fuel blending, food recipes)kg of each ingredientmin ingredient costnutrition or quality specs
    Workforce scheduling (call centre, hospital, airline crew)staff per shiftmin labour coststaff needed each hour

Exam-ready answer

Linear Programming (LP) is a mathematical optimisation technique that helps managers allocate limited resources to reach the best outcome, maximum profit or minimum cost, when the objective and all constraints are linear. An LP model has four parts: decision variables (what we choose), an objective function (what we maximise or minimise), constraints (limits such as hours, budget or capacity) and non-negativity. It assumes certainty, proportionality, additivity and divisibility. The set of solutions meeting all constraints is the feasible region, and the optimum always lies on one of its corner points. Small problems can be solved graphically; real ones use Excel Solver.

Example: a workshop earns $70 per table and $50 per chair, with 240 carpentry hours (4 h and 3 h each) and 100 painting hours (2 h and 1 h each). Testing the corners (0,80) = $4,000, (30,40) = $4,100 and (50,0) = $3,500 shows the best plan is 30 tables and 40 chairs, for $4,100.

Business cases: product mix in a factory; the transportation problem (cheapest way to ship from plants to warehouses); blending (animal feed or fuel at minimum cost while meeting quality specs); staff scheduling in hospitals, call centres and airlines; and distribution network design. In supply chains, LP turns data into a concrete decision, which is why it is called prescriptive analytics.

Common mistakes

  • Defining LP as "a forecasting method". It is an optimisation method; forecasting only provides its input numbers.
  • Forgetting non-negativity or the units of each constraint.
  • Choosing the corner by eye. Always compute the profit at every corner.
  • Listing examples without saying what is maximised or minimised and what the constraints are.

Thinking it through, step by step

  1. 1Step 1: Why do forum problems matter?

    Every supply chain plan (how much to produce, how much stock to hold, how many staff to roster) starts from an estimate of the future. If the estimate is wrong, the whole chain is wrong: too much stock (cost) or too little (lost sales). That is exactly what Forum 11 practised.

    • Several drivers (price, advertising, weather) → multiple regression

Exam-ready answer

In BINUS Forum 11 we worked on forecasting problems, because every supply chain decision (production, inventory, staffing) depends on an estimate of future demand or cost.

(1) Rising unit cost. A firm wanted to budget its future cost per unit. Because the data showed a steady upward trend, we used simple linear regression (Y = a + bX).   • Naming a method without saying why it fits the data pattern.
  • Using a moving average on trending data (it lags behind).
  • Reading R² as "the model is significant". Significance comes from Significance F and the P-values.
  • Stopping at the number. Always end with the managerial decision.

Define Operations Management (OM)

What the question really asks

Give a precise definition of Operations Management, explain the idea behind it (transformation and value added), and show its place in the firm.

Keywords to hit: design, operation, improvement, productive system, inputs → transformation → outputs, value added, efficiency and effectiveness.

Thinking it through, step by step

  1. 1Step 1: Why does a firm need OM?

    Every organisation exists to deliver something customers value: a product, a service or both. Somebody must decide how that thing is made and delivered: the process, the capacity, the quality, the schedule, the stock. That "how" is operations.

  2. 2Step 2: The definition.

    Russell & Taylor: Operations Management (OM) is the design, operation and improvement of productive systems. A productive system is any system that turns inputs into goods or services.

    • Design: choose the product, process, capacity and location (long-term decisions).
    • Operation: plan, schedule, control quality and inventory day to day.
    • Improvement: keep making it better (lean, Six Sigma, kaizen).

  3. 3Step 3: The core idea: transformation creates value.

    OM transforms inputs (materials, machines, labour, management, capital) into outputs (goods and services) of greater value.

    Value added = value of outputs − cost of inputs. If the output is not worth more than the inputs, the operation destroys value.

  4. 4Step 4: How do we know OM is doing well?

    • Efficiency: doing things right, measured as productivity = output ÷ input.
    • Effectiveness: doing the right things, i.e. what customers actually want (cost, quality, speed, flexibility).

  5. 5Step 5: Where does OM sit in the company?

    OM is one of the three core functions, beside marketing (creates demand) and finance (provides money). OM fulfils the demand. In a modern firm, OM extends beyond the factory walls into the supply chain (suppliers and distributors), which is why this course joins OM and supply chain management.

Illustration

OM is illustrated in Q4 (figures/q04-om-transformation.svg). For Q3, a one-line sketch is enough: Inputs → Transformation → Outputs (greater value), with a feedback arrow underneath.

Exam-ready answer

Operations Management (OM) is the design, operation and improvement of the productive systems that create a firm's goods and services. Its core is the transformation process: inputs such as materials, machines, labour, management and capital are transformed into outputs (goods and services) of greater value than the inputs. The difference is the value added. OM makes decisions on design (product, process, capacity, location), on daily operation (scheduling, quality, inventory) and on continuous improvement. It is judged by efficiency (productivity = output ÷ input) and effectiveness (meeting customer needs on cost, quality, speed and flexibility). OM is one of the three core business functions, together with marketing and finance, and today it extends across the whole supply chain.

Common mistakes

  • "OM is about factories." Services (hospitals, banks, universities, airlines) are productive systems too.
  • Leaving out "value added", which is the reason OM exists.
  • Confusing efficiency with effectiveness.

The bullwhip effect: explain it, with an example and an illustration (Hau Lee)

What the question really asks

Explain what the bullwhip effect is, why it happens (Hau Lee's four causes), why it is harmful, how to reduce it, and draw it. This was specially flagged by the lecturer, so expect high marks on it.

Keywords to hit: order variability amplifies upstream, demand forecast updating, order batching, price fluctuation, rationing and shortage gaming, information sharing, POS, VMI, EDLP.

Thinking it through, step by step

  1. 1Step 1: Notice the strange fact.

    Consumers buy fairly steadily: people use about the same number of diapers or bottles of water every week. Yet factories see wild swings in the orders they receive. Where does the swing come from, if not from the consumer?

  2. 2Step 2: Name it.

    The bullwhip effect is the amplification of order variability as you move upstream in the supply chain: from the retailer to the wholesaler to the distributor to the manufacturer and its suppliers. Like cracking a whip, a small flick of the wrist (consumer) becomes a big swing at the tip (factory). The term comes from Hau Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang (1997), "The Bullwhip Effect in Supply Chains."

  3. 3Step 3: Make it concrete with numbers (the lecturer's sketch).

    A small change in consumer demand of 5 units becomes 10 at the retailer's order, 15 at the wholesaler's order and 20 at the factory. Each player adds a bit of "just in case".

  4. 4Step 4: Ask why each player amplifies: Hau Lee's four causes.

    1. Demand forecast updating. Each level forecasts from the orders it receives, not from real consumer sales, and adds its own safety stock. Forecasts stacked on forecasts magnify the swing.
    2. Order batching. Firms order in big weekly or monthly lots (to save ordering or transport cost), so smooth demand looks lumpy upstream.
    3. Price fluctuation. Promotions and discounts make buyers forward-buy (stock up while it is cheap, then stop ordering).
    4. Rationing and shortage gaming. When supply is short and allocated, buyers inflate orders to get a bigger share, then cancel when supply recovers. The factory sees "phantom demand".

  5. 5Step 5: Why is this bad?

    The factory plans to the exaggerated orders, so the chain swings between excess inventory and stockouts. Result: wasted capacity, overtime followed by idle time, higher transport and storage cost, worse customer service and greater risk to the whole system.

  6. 6Step 6: So the cure follows from the cause (cause → countermeasure).

    CauseCountermeasure
    Demand forecast updatingShare POS (point-of-sale) data with every level; VMI (vendor-managed inventory); shorter lead times
    Order batchingSmaller, more frequent orders; electronic ordering; mixed truckloads through a 3PL (third-party logistics provider)
    Price fluctuationEDLP (everyday low price); fewer promotions
    Rationing and shortage gamingAllocate by past sales, not by orders; share capacity and stock information; penalise cancellations

    The common thread: share real information and reduce the incentives to over-order.

  7. 7Step 7: Real examples.

    • Procter & Gamble's Pampers diapers: babies use diapers at a steady rate, yet P&G saw big swings in distributor orders. This is the classic case behind Lee's article.
    • Toilet paper in early 2020 (COVID-19): panic buying plus retailers over-ordering created empty shelves, then excess stock months later.
    • Car chip shortage 2021: carmakers cancelled chip orders, then panic-ordered; suppliers saw huge swings.
    • The Beer Game (MIT simulation) shows the effect even when players are smart, because each sees only local orders.

Exam-ready answer

The bullwhip effect is the amplification of order variability as orders move upstream in the supply chain, from consumer to retailer, wholesaler and manufacturer (Hau Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang, 1997). A small change in consumer demand, say 5 units, becomes an order change of 10 at the retailer, 15 at the wholesaler and 20 at the factory, even though real consumption is stable.

Causes (Hau Lee): (1) demand forecast updating: each tier forecasts from the orders it receives and adds safety stock; (2) order batching: large periodic orders make demand look lumpy; (3) price fluctuation: promotions cause forward buying; (4) rationing and shortage gaming: during shortages buyers inflate orders and later cancel.

Why it is bad: excess inventory alternating with stockouts, wasted capacity, overtime then idle time, higher costs, poorer service and higher risk across the chain.

Countermeasures: share POS data and use VMI (vendor-managed inventory); order smaller and more often, using electronic ordering and mixed truckloads via a 3PL; use EDLP (everyday low price) instead of frequent promotions; allocate scarce supply by past sales and penalise cancellations.

Example: Procter & Gamble saw large swings in distributor orders for Pampers diapers although babies' consumption was steady; similar swings hit toilet paper during COVID-19 panic buying.

  • Saying the bullwhip effect is "high consumer demand". It is about variability growing upstream, not demand being high.
  • Drawing the waves getting smaller towards the factory (they grow).
  • Listing causes without the matching countermeasures. Pair them.
  • Forgetting to name Hau Lee, which the lecturer specifically mentioned.

Supply Chain Management (SCM): define it, illustrate it, and explain 1PL–5PL and the value chain

What the question really asks

Give the definition of SCM, draw the chain with its flows, and explain the related ideas: the levels of logistics outsourcing (1PL to 5PL), Porter's value chain, and the foundational premises.

Keywords to hit: network of organisations, upstream/downstream, three flows (materials, information, money), focal firm, 1PL–5PL, primary and support activities, margin.

Thinking it through, step by step

  1. 1Step 1: Why did SCM appear?

    Products today pass through many companies before reaching the consumer (raw material → components → assembly → distribution → retail). A firm can be perfect internally and still fail if a supplier is late or a distributor holds too much stock. So firms no longer compete alone; supply chains compete against supply chains.

  2. 2Step 2: Definition.

    Supply Chain Management (SCM) is the management of the flow of materials, information and money across the entire network of organisations, from raw-material suppliers through manufacturers and distributors to the final consumer, in order to deliver customer value at the lowest total cost.

    (Council of Supply Chain Management Professionals, CSCMP: SCM covers the planning and management of all activities in sourcing and procurement, conversion and logistics, including coordination and collaboration with channel partners.)

  3. 3Step 3: Picture the chain and its three flows.

    • Materials flow downstream (supplier → consumer); returns flow back (reverse logistics, Q14).
    • Information flows both ways (orders up; forecasts, POS data and shipping notices both directions).
    • Money flows upstream (consumer pays retailer, retailer pays distributor, and so on).

    The firm you look from is the focal firm. Everything before it is upstream (suppliers: tier 1, tier 2), everything after it is downstream (customers).

  4. 4Step 4: How does a chain behave? The six foundational premises (FP) from the slides.

    1. FP1 Network: a chain is nodes (firms) and links (flows).
    2. FP2 Complex: a complex adaptive system; there is tension between control and emergence.
    3. FP3 Relative: a chain is always defined from one product and one agent's point of view.
    4. FP4 Two layers: the physical chain plus a support chain (banks, IT, logistics providers).
    5. FP5 Limited view: bounded by what the focal firm can see.
    6. FP6 Fading view: visibility fades with physical distance, cultural distance and closeness.

    In one sentence: a supply chain is a network no single firm fully controls or sees.

  5. 5Step 5: Who moves the goods? The 1PL → 5PL ladder (logistics outsourcing).

    The logic: each step up, the firm hands more of the logistics to outsiders and gains more integration.

    LevelMeaningExample
    1PL First-Party LogisticsThe firm does it itself: own trucks, own warehouseA farmer delivering their own harvest; a factory with its own fleet
    2PL Second-Party LogisticsHires a carrier for one leg of transportTrucking company, shipping line, airline cargo
    3PL Third-Party LogisticsOutsources logistics operations: warehousing + transport + value-added services (packing, labelling)DHL Supply Chain, Kerry Logistics, JNE/SiCepat fulfilment
    4PL Fourth-Party LogisticsAn integrator that manages the whole chain and several 3PLs, as one point of contactLead logistics provider / consulting-style integrator
    5PL Fifth-Party LogisticsManages whole networks for many shippers, often through technology platforms (aggregating demand, e-commerce)E-commerce logistics platforms

  6. 6Step 6: Where does value come from? Porter's value chain (1985).

    Michael Porter split a firm into activities that create value:

    • Primary activities (the flow of the product): inbound logistics → operations → outbound logistics → marketing & sales → service.
    • Support activities (help all primary ones): firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, procurement.
    • Margin = the value customers pay for minus the cost of all activities.

    Link to SCM: inbound and outbound logistics are inside every firm's value chain, and the supply chain is simply many value chains linked together (sometimes called the value system).

  7. 7Step 7: So what?

    Good SCM raises customer value (right product, on time) and lowers total cost (less stock, less waste), and it reduces risks such as the bullwhip effect.

Lean operations: define Lean and explain the whole concept

What the question really asks

Explain where lean comes from, what it means, its principles, the wastes, its tools (JIT, jidoka, kanban, kaizen), and how it changes operations and supply chains.

Thinking it through, step by step

  1. 1Step 1: What was wrong before lean?

    Production evolved from craft (custom, one by one) to mass production (Henry Ford: standard products, long runs, low cost per unit). Mass production is a push system: produce to a forecast in big batches, store the result (make-to-stock). When the forecast is wrong, you get huge stocks, hidden defects and slow change.

  2. 2Step 2: Toyota's answer.

    After the Second World War, Toyota had little money or space for big stocks. Taiichi Ohno and Eiji Toyoda built the Toyota Production System (TPS): make only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed. The West later called this lean (it uses less of everything: inventory, human effort, space, tooling, engineering hours).

  3. 3Step 3: Definition.

    Lean operations is a way of managing that maximises customer value by systematically eliminating waste and continuously improving the flow of products and information, producing only on real demand (pull).

  4. 4Step 4: What counts as waste? Anything the customer would not pay for (Japanese: muda).

    The 8 wastes, mnemonic TIM WOODS:

    • Centre: waste elimination and respect for people, teamwork.
    • Foundation: heijunka (level production, no surges or idle time), standardised work, kaizen (small continuous improvement by everyone), and stability of machines, materials and people.

    Lower cost and inventory, better quality, faster response, and problems become visible quickly. Lean works best with stable, predictable demand and reliable suppliers (functional products in Fisher's model). Exam-ready answer

Lean operations is a management approach that maximises customer value by eliminating waste and producing only according to real demand. It comes from the Toyota Production System (TPS) developed by Taiichi Ohno, as an alternative to mass production, which pushed products to a forecast and created large inventories and hidden defects.

Waste (muda) is anything the customer would not pay for. The 8 wastes are transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, over-processing, defects and unused skills (TIM WOODS).

Lean follows five principles: define value from the customer's view, map the value stream, create flow, let the customer pull, and pursue perfection.

Instead of push (make-to-stock from a forecast), lean uses pull: production starts when real demand arrives, with materials arriving just-in-time (JIT), often signalled by kanban. The "lean house" has two pillars, JIT and jidoka (stop and fix defects immediately, build quality in), on a foundation of heijunka (level production), standardised work and kaizen (continuous small improvements by everyone), with respect for people at its centre.

The results are lower cost and inventory, higher quality and faster response. Lean fits stable demand best; because it holds little buffer, it must be paired with supply chain risk management. In supply chains it appears as VMI, cross-docking, small frequent deliveries and shared demand data.

Common mistakes

  • "Lean = cutting staff or cutting costs." Lean cuts waste, and respect for people is one of its pillars.
  • Listing the wastes without examples.
  • Forgetting push vs pull, which is the core shift.
  • Saying JIT means zero inventory. It means the minimum needed, delivered when needed.

    The key reasoning: the three are balanced, not traded off one by one. Profit is not the enemy: without profit the firm dies and helps nobody. But profit earned by hurting people or the planet is not sustainable, because it creates risks (boycotts, strikes, fines, lost licences, scarce resources) that eventually destroy the profit too. A sustainable business sits in the middle of the triangle, where all three are satisfied.

    How 3P connects to ESG and CSR (this is the logic to show in the exam):

    • CSR = WHY: the company's ethical foundation, values and vision ("we commit to treat people fairly and protect the planet while staying profitable").
    • 3P = WHAT: the three things the company commits to balance.
    • ESG = HOW it is measured: each P becomes measurable targets that are reported. People → S (Social), Planet → E (Environmental), and Profit → G (Governance), because good governance (ethical leadership, compliance, accountability) is what keeps profit honest and long-term, and investors read ESG reports to judge long-term risk to their returns.

  3. 3Step 3: ESG turns 3P into measurable areas: Environmental, Social and Governance.

    • Environmental: emissions, energy, water, waste, packaging.
    • Social: fair labour practice, community engagement and human rights, including in suppliers' factories. Example (lecturer): Apple and its Chinese supplier Foxconn: after worker suicides and harsh conditions at Foxconn around 2010, Apple was held responsible and had to audit and improve supplier working conditions. The lesson: you are judged by your suppliers' behaviour.
    • Governance: how the company is run: ethics, transparency, board oversight, anti-corruption, fair treatment of employees. Example (lecturer): when an employee performs badly, good governance means coaching first; if it must end, offer the choice to resign rather than dismissing abruptly.

  4. 4Step 4: Who is the firm responsible to? Stockholders vs stakeholders.

    • Stockholders (shareholders): the people who own shares. The traditional view (stockholder theory) says the firm's job is to maximise their return.
    • Stakeholders: everyone affected by the firm: employees, customers, suppliers, the community, government, the environment, and the stockholders too. Stakeholder theory says the firm must balance all of them.
    • The relationship: stockholders are a subset of stakeholders. ESG and 3P follow the stakeholder view.

  5. 5Step 5: CSR vs ESG (how they differ).

    • CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility): the firm's voluntary commitment to act responsibly. In the lecturer's words, CSR gives the vision and mission: the why and the intention (e.g. community programmes, donations, volunteering).
    • ESG: sets measurable targets that must be achieved and reported (e.g. cut emissions 30% by 2030, 100% of suppliers audited). Investors use ESG scores to judge risk.
    • In one line: CSR = commitment (what we believe); ESG = measurement (what we achieve).

  6. 6Step 6: Competition can also be cooperation (coopetition).

    Firms can compete and cooperate at the same time. Example (lecturer): Samsung competes with Apple in smartphones, yet Samsung Display supplies OLED screens for iPhones. Cooperation can also be sustainable: competitors sharing trucks or warehouses to promote scale, i.e. consolidating loads so fewer, fuller trucks drive, which cuts both cost and emissions.

  7. 7Step 7: 3R: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, and the recovery hierarchy.

    • Reduce: use less material and energy in the first place (best).
    • Reuse: use the product again as it is (returnable bottles, pallets).
    • Recycle: turn the material into new raw material (plastic, aluminium).

    The slide's recovery hierarchy (from most to least value kept): resource reduction → reuse → remanufacturing → recycling → disposal with energy recovery → landfill. Always choose the highest option possible. Collecting products back for these options is reverse logistics (Q14).

  8. 8Step 8: How it all connects.

    CSR states the commitment (why) → 3P names what to balance: people, planet, profit (what) → ESG turns each P into measurable targets: Social, Environmental, Governance (how it is measured) → all of it serves stakeholders, not only stockholders → coopetition and promoting scale make it efficient → 3R and reverse logistics deliver the planet part inside the supply chain.

Exam-ready answer

3P (People, Planet, Profit), the triple bottom line, judges a business on three results instead of profit alone. People means treating employees, supplier workers, customers and communities fairly; Planet means protecting the environment (emissions, resources, waste); Profit means earning a fair, lasting economic return that keeps the firm alive and shares value with suppliers and society. They must be balanced: profit earned by harming people or the planet is not sustainable, because it brings boycotts, fines and resource risks. 3P links CSR and ESG: CSR is the commitment (why), 3P is what to balance, and ESG measures it, with People reflected in Social, Planet in Environmental and Profit kept honest and long-term by Governance.

ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) turns this into measurable areas. Environmental covers emissions, energy and waste. Social covers fair labour practice, community engagement and human rights, including in the supply chain: Apple was held responsible for poor working conditions at its supplier Foxconn and had to audit and improve them. Governance is how the company is run: ethics, transparency and fair treatment, e.g. coaching an under-performing employee first and offering the option to resign rather than dismissing them abruptly.

Stockholders are the owners of shares; stakeholders are everyone affected by the firm (employees, customers, suppliers, community, government and stockholders). ESG follows the stakeholder view.

CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility) is a company's voluntary commitment to operate responsibly through ethical sourcing, fair labour and community engagement, across its suppliers and subcontractors. As the slides put it, CSR sets the ethical foundation and vision, while ESG provides the measurable targets and accountability needed to implement it.

Competition can include cooperation: Samsung competes with Apple in phones but supplies iPhone screens. Firms can also cooperate to promote scale, consolidating loads to cut cost and emissions.

3R (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle) reduces waste, following the recovery hierarchy: resource reduction, reuse, remanufacturing, recycling, disposal with energy recovery and, last, landfill. Returning products for these options is done by reverse logistics.

Common mistakes

  • Using "stockholder" and "stakeholder" as synonyms.
  • Saying CSR and ESG are the same. CSR = commitment; ESG = measurable targets.
  • Listing People, Planet, Profit without explaining each one or how they map to ESG (People → S, Planet → E, Profit → G).
  • Treating profit as "the bad P". All three are needed; the point is balance.
  • Writing 3R without the order of preference (reduce first, landfill last).
  • Forgetting the lecturer's examples (Foxconn, Samsung–Apple, coaching vs dismissing).
Question 14 · Kisi-kisi

Reverse logistics in detail

What the question really asks

Explain reverse logistics fully: definition, why it exists, what flows back, the process steps, the recovery options, the difficulties and real examples.

Keywords to hit: point of consumption → point of origin, recapture value or proper disposal, returns, end-of-life, gatekeeping, collection, sorting/inspection, disposition, recovery hierarchy, circular economy.

Thinking it through, step by step

  1. 1Step 1: Start from the forward chain.

    Normal (forward) logistics moves new products from supplier → manufacturer → distributor → retailer → consumer. But products also come back: wrong size, broken, unsold, recalled, or at the end of their life.

  2. 2Step 2: Definition.

    Reverse logistics is the process of planning, implementing and controlling the efficient, cost-effective flow of products, materials and related information from the point of consumption back to the point of origin, in order to recapture value or ensure proper disposal (Rogers and Tibben-Lembke).

  3. 3Step 3: What flows back, and why?

    • Customer returns: wrong size, changed mind (very high in e-commerce), damaged in delivery.
    • Product recalls: safety or quality defects.
    • Unsold stock: seasonal goods, expired items, overstock.
    • Repairs and warranty claims.
    • End-of-life products: old phones, batteries, electronics (e-waste).
    • Reusable packaging: pallets, crates, returnable bottles and gallons.

  4. 4Step 4: Why do firms bother? (drivers)

    • Economic: recover value from parts and materials; resell refurbished items.
    • Legal: take-back and extended producer responsibility (EPR) laws, e-waste rules.
    • Customer service: easy returns are an order winner online.
    • Environmental (ESG): less landfill, supports 3R and the circular economy (Q13).

  5. 5Step 5: How does it work? The process, step by step.

    1. Gatekeeping: decide which returns to accept at the entry point (screening out invalid or fraudulent returns saves cost).
    2. Collection: pick up from customers, stores or drop points.
    3. Sorting and inspection: test and grade the item's condition.
    4. Disposition: choose the best option for each item using the recovery hierarchy (most value kept first):
      • Resource reduction (design so less comes back)
      • Reuse / resell as is (return to stock, outlet, second-hand market)
      • Remanufacturing / refurbishing (repair and restore to "like new")
      • Recycling (recover materials)
      • Disposal with energy recovery (burn for energy)
      • Landfill (last resort)
    5. Redistribution: send recovered goods or materials back into the forward chain.

  6. 6Step 6: Why is it harder than forward logistics?

    • Uncertain timing, quantity and quality (you don't know what will come back, or when).
    • Many small, scattered pickups, so it is costly.
    • Each item needs individual inspection and a different route.
    • Products often lose value quickly while waiting (fashion, electronics).
    • Hence firms often outsource it to a 3PL or use centralised return centres.

  7. 7Step 7: Examples.

    • AQUA 19-litre gallons in Indonesia: empty gallons are collected when new ones are delivered, then cleaned and refilled (reuse).
    • Apple Trade In: old iPhones are refurbished and resold, or disassembled (Apple's "Daisy" robot) to recover materials (recycling).
    • E-commerce returns (Shopee, Tokopedia, Zalora): returned items are inspected and restocked or sent to outlets.
    • Product recalls (e.g. car airbag recalls): defective units are brought back to be fixed.
    • H&M garment collecting: used clothes collected in store for reuse or recycling.
    • Pallets and crates returned and reused between partners.

  8. 8Step 8: So what? Link to the big picture.

    Reverse logistics closes the loop: forward chain + reverse chain = a closed-loop supply chain, the operational heart of the circular economy. It turns returns from a cost into a source of value and supports the "planet" part of 3P and ESG.

Exam-ready answer

Reverse logistics is the planning, implementation and control of the efficient flow of products, materials and information from the point of consumption back to the point of origin, to recapture value or dispose of them properly. It handles customer returns, product recalls, unsold or expired stock, repairs and warranty claims, end-of-life products and reusable packaging.

Firms do it for economic reasons (recovering value), legal reasons (take-back and extended producer responsibility laws), customer service (easy returns) and environmental reasons (supporting 3R, ESG and the circular economy).

The process is: gatekeeping (screen which returns to accept), collection, sorting and inspection, and disposition, where each item takes the highest option on the recovery hierarchy: resource reduction, reuse or resale, remanufacturing or refurbishing, recycling, disposal with energy recovery and, last, landfill. Recovered goods are then redistributed into the forward chain.

Reverse logistics is harder than forward logistics because timing, quantity and condition are uncertain, pickups are small and scattered, and each item must be inspected individually, so firms often use 3PLs or central return centres.

Examples: AQUA's returnable 19-litre gallons are collected, cleaned and refilled; Apple's Trade In refurbishes old iPhones or recycles their materials; e-commerce returns are inspected and restocked. Together with the forward chain, reverse logistics creates a closed-loop supply chain.

Common mistakes

  • Defining it only as "returning goods". Add the purpose: recapture value or proper disposal.
  • Skipping the process steps (gatekeeping → collection → sorting → disposition).
  • Forgetting the recovery hierarchy, which the lecturer specifically pointed to.
