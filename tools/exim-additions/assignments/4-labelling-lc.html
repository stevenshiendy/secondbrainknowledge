Assignment · Import labelling and payment

Why import labels are applied at the factory, and why the letter of credit suits document-based trade

Question 1

Why must import labelling regulations be fulfilled at the factory?

Import labeling regulations include requirements such as local-language text, ingredient lists, safety warnings, and energy efficiency ratings. These requirements must be fulfilled at the factory in the country of origin for the following reasons:

  • Compliance is checked at the point of entry. Many countries require goods to be correctly labeled at the moment they enter the country. In Indonesia, for example, Ministry of Trade regulations require imported goods to already carry an Indonesian-language label when they enter the Indonesian customs area. Since the legal checkpoint is the border, the label must be applied before the goods are shipped.
  • Consumer protection. Labels allow consumers to read safety instructions, warnings, and usage guidelines in their own language. Labeling at the factory ensures that every unit is compliant before it reaches the market.
  • Cost and efficiency. Applying labels on the production line is much cheaper and faster than unpacking, relabeling, and repacking goods after they arrive.
  • Quality control and verification. Labels applied under controlled factory conditions are more accurate and consistent. They can also be checked by a third-party inspector before shipment, giving the importer proof that the goods are compliant.
  • Avoiding rejection at customs. Shipments with missing or incorrect labels can be refused entry, held, re-exported, or destroyed, which creates heavy losses for the importer.

Why labels are generally not applied at the port of destination

Ports are designed for cargo handling, inspection, and customs clearance. They are not packaging or manufacturing facilities. Relabeling at the port is generally not allowed as a way to fix non-compliant goods because of:

  • Port congestion. Large-scale unpacking and relabeling would slow down port operations and delay other cargo. It would also cost the importer storage and demurrage charges while the goods wait.
  • Risk of tampering and evasion. If importers could bring in unlabeled goods and "fix" them later, it would create a loophole for smuggling, counterfeit products, and avoiding technical standards.

Some countries do allow limited relabeling in bonded warehouses or free trade zones under customs supervision. However, this is an exception and should not be relied on as a normal business practice.

Question 2

Why is the Letter of Credit (L/C) well-aligned with document handling?

International trade is largely document-based, and the Letter of Credit fits this system well. Under UCP 600 (Article 5), the international rules for L/Cs, banks deal only with documents and not with the actual goods. The L/C supports document handling in the following ways:

  • Payment depends on correct documents. The issuing bank promises to pay the seller only when the seller presents the documents required in the L/C. These usually include the commercial invoice, bill of lading, packing list, and certificate of origin.
  • Strict compliance. The documents must match the terms of the L/C exactly. If there is a discrepancy, the bank can refuse payment. This pushes the seller to prepare accurate documents.
  • Control over the goods. The bill of lading is a document of title. Whoever holds it can claim the goods, so the buyer only gains control of the goods after the bank has checked the documents.
  • Protection against non-compliance. The importer can require extra documents in the L/C, such as an inspection certificate or labeling compliance certificate from an independent surveyor. This links back to Question 1. If the seller cannot prove that labeling was done correctly at the factory, the bank will not release payment.
  • Reducing the need for trust. The buyer and seller may not know or trust each other. The L/C solves this by replacing the buyer's credit with the bank's credit. The seller is assured of payment, and the buyer is protected from paying for goods that were not shipped as agreed.

Limitation: because banks only check documents, an L/C cannot guarantee that the goods themselves are compliant. It only confirms that the documents say so. This is why requiring a certificate from an independent inspector is important. L/Cs are also more expensive and slower than other payment methods, so they are most useful in new trade relationships or high-risk shipments.
