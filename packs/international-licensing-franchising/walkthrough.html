Course walkthrough

This book covers all seven session decks of International Licensing, Franchising & Strategic Alliances (effective period 2025), Sessions 1–14: the four ways of entering a foreign market, then licensing from its basics through its strategic use to its legal and ethical side, then franchising from its basics through international development to franchise agreements and the laws behind them. The cases taught in class (Disney, ARM Holdings, Apple versus Qualcomm, Domino's India, 7-Eleven Thailand and Burgerim) sit in the chapters they illustrate. Strategic alliances appear in the Sessions 1–2 overview only, because no deck in the course folder treats them on their own. Every chapter is organized the same way: a four-step chain (problem, big idea, tools, result), a plain-words explanation, at least one figure, and then the details to memorize under subheadings. Tags such as "Sessions 3–4" give the class sessions a chapter draws on, and "CILO" stands for course intended learning outcome; the three CILOs are set out in the first chapter. Every abbreviation is spelled out the first time it appears in each chapter and is listed in the glossary at the back.

In plain words

International Licensing, Franchising & Strategic Alliances (course code ENPR6305032) is taught in fourteen sessions, two per deck. Sessions 1–2 set the scene. They ask why a company would enter international markets at all, and they lay out four ways of doing it: exporting, licensing, franchising and strategic alliances. They then compare the four on investment, control and risk, and separate direct entry, through the company's own operations, from indirect entry, through agents, distributors or partners.

The rest of the course runs in two lanes. The licensing lane takes Sessions 3–8. Sessions 3–4 explain what licensing is, its five types, the clauses of a licensing agreement and two cases, Disney and ARM Holdings. Sessions 5–6 treat licensing as a deliberate strategy: its objectives, its timing over a product's life cycle, the choice of partner and the management of risk. Sessions 7–8 turn to the legal and ethical side: the types of intellectual property (IP), the laws that protect it in the United States, the European Union and worldwide, the legal clauses of a licensing contract, ethics, and the Apple versus Qualcomm patent dispute.

The franchising lane takes Sessions 9–14 and follows the same order. Sessions 9–10 explain what franchising is, its two types and the roles of franchisor and franchisee. Sessions 11–12 show how a franchise is developed abroad through direct franchising, master franchising, area development or a joint venture. Sessions 13–14 cover the franchise agreement, the franchise disclosure document (FDD), legal frameworks in several countries and the Burgerim case. Strategic alliances, the fourth route and part of the course title, are defined and compared in Sessions 1–2 but have no deck of their own in the course folder.

The three learning outcomes

  • CILO 1: Define the foundational principles of international licensing, franchising, and strategic alliances. Named in Sessions 1–2, 3–4 and 9–10.
  • CILO 2: Evaluate different market entry strategies and determine when licensing, franchising, or alliances are most appropriate. Named in Sessions 1–2, 3–4, 5–6, 9–10 and 11–12.
  • CILO 3: Identify legal, financial, and operational factors affecting international business collaborations. Named in Sessions 7–8 and 13–14.

PART ASESSIONS 1–2 · INTRODUCTION TO INTERNATIONAL MARKET ENTRY STRATEGIES
Sessions 1–2 · CILO 1

Why go abroad, and four ways to do it

ProblemA company that wants foreign customers must decide how it will reach them.
Big ideaThere are four main entry strategies: exporting, licensing, franchising and strategic alliances, each with its own advantages and challenges.
How (tools)A definition, the advantages and the challenges of each strategy.
ResultThe company can match a strategy to what it wants from the foreign market and what it is willing to give up.
In plain words

The course starts with the reasons for entering international markets. Sessions 1–2 give four: to access new customers, to increase revenue, to gain competitive advantage, and to spread risk across markets. The last reason is worth noticing, because it shows that going abroad is not only about growth: a company selling in several markets is less exposed to trouble in any one of them.

The slides then set out four entry strategies. Exporting means selling domestic products abroad, either directly, with the company handling it, or indirectly, through intermediaries. Licensing means permitting a foreign company to use your intellectual property (IP). Franchising means letting a foreign party run a business under your system and brand. A strategic alliance is a collaboration between two or more firms to pursue mutual goals while remaining independent.

Each strategy brings its own advantages and its own challenges, and the pattern is easy to follow once the definitions are clear. Exporting keeps the business at home, so it is low risk and easy, but the company has limited control abroad and faces trade barriers. Licensing hands over only the IP, so it is low cost and fast, but control is lost and the IP may be misused. Franchising hands over the whole system, so it is scalable and draws on local knowledge, but the franchisor must keep franchisee quality under control and comes to depend on its franchisees. An alliance shares the effort, so cost and risk are shared and the partner brings local market expertise, but coordination is complex and conflict is possible.

  2. Increase revenue
  3. Gain competitive advantage
  4. Spread risk across markets

Sessions 1–2 · CILO 2

Choosing a route: investment, control and risk

ProblemThe four entry strategies all lead abroad, but they ask for very different commitments.
Big ideaCompare them on three criteria (investment, control and risk) and on the use case each suits best.
How (tools)The strategy comparison table and a company example for each strategy.
ResultA clear picture of when each route is the natural choice, such as exporting to test demand or franchising to scale a service business.
In plain words

Once the four strategies are defined, Sessions 1–2 put them in one table. Investment is low for exporting and licensing, medium for franchising and medium to high for a strategic alliance. Control is low for exporting and licensing, medium for franchising and shared in an alliance. Risk is low for exporting and medium for the other three. The table then names a best use case for each: exporting for testing foreign demand, licensing for entering intellectual property (IP)-intensive industries, franchising for service businesses seeking global scale, and alliances for complex markets requiring local support.

Read this way, the table is a guide to fit. A company that is not yet sure foreign customers want its product can export first, because exporting asks for little investment and carries low risk. A company whose value lies in IP, such as characters or technology, can license it. A service business that wants to grow across many countries can franchise its system. A company facing a complex market where it needs a local partner's support can form an alliance and share control with that partner.

The slides give a well-known example of each. Indomie exports instant noodles from Indonesia to more than 80 countries without local factories. Disney licenses its characters to global partners for merchandise production. McDonald's operates globally through franchisees under standard systems. Starbucks formed an alliance with Tata to enter the Indian market, with Tata handling sourcing and operations.

Sessions 1–2 · CILO 2

Direct versus indirect entry

ProblemWhatever the strategy, a company must decide whether to run its foreign presence itself or work through others.
Big ideaDirect entry uses the company's own subsidiaries and branches; indirect entry uses agents, distributors or partners.
How (tools)A comparison on five criteria (control, cost, speed, market knowledge, risk) and a list of advantages and disadvantages.
ResultThe trade-off is plain: direct entry buys control and insight at a high price, indirect entry buys speed and low cost at the price of control.
In plain words

Direct entry means the company enters through its own subsidiaries or local branches. Indirect entry means it enters through agents, distributors or partners. The slides illustrate the difference with Starbucks opening its own stores (direct) and local distributors selling a domestic product overseas (indirect).

Each criterion in the comparison follows from who owns the operation. Because a company entering directly owns its operations and makes its own decisions, its control is high; because it has to set them up and run them, its cost is high and its entry is slower, since a physical presence must be established. It also gains high market knowledge, with direct access to consumers and their insights, but it bears higher risk, with full exposure to local challenges.

Indirect entry reverses each of these. Control is low because agents or partners do the work. Cost is low and entry is faster, because the company leverages networks that already exist. Market knowledge is low, because what the company learns is filtered through intermediaries. Risk is lower, because it is shared with or passed to partners. The advantages and disadvantages listed on the slides are the same trade-off seen from each side.

PART BSESSIONS 3–4 · FUNDAMENTALS OF LICENSING
Sessions 3–4 · CILO 1

What licensing is: definition and five types

ProblemA firm owns valuable intellectual property but does not want to invest capital in every foreign market itself.
Big ideaLicensing lets a foreign firm use that property under agreed conditions, governed by contract law and international intellectual property treaties.
How (tools)The definition, its legal form and strategic purpose, the industries that use it, and five types of licensing with examples.
ResultThe owner of the intellectual property expands with low risk and no capital investment, and the licensee gains the right to use a brand, a design, a technology or content.
In plain words

Sessions 3–4 define licensing as a business arrangement in which a licensor, the owner of the intellectual property (IP), permits a licensee, a foreign firm, to use its IP, such as a trademark, a brand or a technology, under agreed conditions. The licensor keeps ownership; what passes to the licensee is permission to use the IP within the conditions the two sides agree.

Three further points complete the definition. Its legal form: licensing is governed by contract law and by international IP treaties such as the TRIPS Agreement (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights). Its strategic purpose: it allows expansion with low risk and no capital investment by the licensor, because the licensee does the producing and selling. And who uses it: licensing is common in the pharmaceuticals, fashion, media, technology and sports industries.

The slides then separate five types of licensing, which differ in what is licensed. In product licensing the licensee produces and sells a product based on the licensor's technology or design. In brand or trademark licensing the licensee uses a brand name or logo in marketing or packaging. In technology licensing the licensee may use proprietary software, hardware or patents. Copyright licensing covers content, images, books and music. Merchandise licensing means selling goods under another company's branding. Each type comes with a familiar example, listed below.

The definition in four parts

  • Definition: a business arrangement where a licensor (IP owner) permits a licensee (foreign firm) to use its intellectual property (trademark, brand, technology) under agreed conditions.
  • Legal form: governed by contract law and international IP treaties (e.g., the TRIPS Agreement).
  • Strategic purpose: allows expansion with low risk and no capital investment by the licensor.
  • Who uses it: common in pharmaceuticals, fashion, media, tech and sports industries.

Sessions 3–4 · CILO 1–2

The licensing agreement: eight core clauses, benefits and limits

ProblemPermission to use intellectual property is only safe if both sides know exactly what is licensed, where, for how long and on what terms.
Big ideaA licensing agreement fixes these terms in eight core clauses, and each side weighs what it gains against what it gives up.
How (tools)The core clauses table and the benefits and limitations of licensing for the licensor and for the licensee.
ResultA deal whose scope, payments, standards and exit are clear, entered with an honest view of its costs to each party.
In plain words

Sessions 3–4 list eight core clauses in a licensing agreement. The first four fix the shape of the permission: the scope of the intellectual property (IP), the IP scope, defines what is being licensed, such as a trademark or a patent; the territory limits where it may be used, for example "license valid only in Southeast Asia"; exclusivity decides whether one or many licensees can use the IP in a region; and the duration sets the agreement period, for example three years, renewable.

The other four govern the relationship once it is running. Royalty terms settle the questions of payment the slides pose: a percentage of sales or a flat fee, monthly or annual reporting, and whether the licensor has audit rights. Quality control lets the licensor inspect operations to maintain its brand's reputation. Termination defines breach conditions, exit rights and dispute resolution. Renewal and transferability specify the renewal terms and whether the rights can be sold or transferred.

The benefits and limitations come from the same arrangement seen from each side. For the licensor, licensing gives quick market entry, low financial risk and revenue through royalties, but it limits control over operations and risks brand dilution. For the licensee, it gives access to a reputable brand or technology, a faster time to market and sometimes support from the licensor, but the licensee depends on the licensor's terms, and royalty costs may reduce its profit margin. The clauses are where these limits are managed: quality control answers the licensor's worry about its brand, and clear royalty terms answer the licensee's worry about cost.

Sessions 3–4 · Cases

Two licensing cases: Disney and ARM Holdings

ProblemProducing every product or chip in-house would tie up capital and limit how far a brand or a design can reach.
Big ideaLicense the intellectual property to others who make the products, and earn from what they sell.
How (tools)Disney's brand and merchandise licensing; ARM's technology licensing with upfront fees plus royalties per chip.
ResultDisney earns billions with minimal manufacturing risk; ARM became a global standard without owning factories.
In plain words

The slides call Disney one of the most successful companies in the world at brand licensing. Rather than producing all its merchandise in-house, Disney licenses its characters, such as Mickey Mouse, Marvel heroes and Star Wars, to manufacturers worldwide. The licensees are toy companies and apparel producers such as Hasbro and LEGO, the scope is global, and royalties are based on product sales. The result, in the slides' words, is that licensing enabled Disney to generate billions in revenue with minimal manufacturing risk and expanded its reach into local markets through themed toys, school supplies and clothing, while Disney maintains strict quality and branding controls to protect its intellectual property (IP).

ARM Holdings, a company from the United Kingdom (UK), designs processor architectures used in smartphones, tablets and Internet of Things (IoT) devices. Rather than manufacturing chips, ARM licenses its IP to companies such as Apple, Qualcomm and Samsung; the licensing details also name Broadcom and MediaTek among its licensees, which are global technology manufacturers. The license covers worldwide use in chip design, and the revenue model combines upfront licensing fees with royalties per chip sold. The slides record three outcomes: ARM has become a global standard without owning factories, its architecture powers over 90% of smartphones globally, and its business model needs little capital while scaling highly.

Set side by side, the two cases show the two sides of licensing from the earlier chapters. Both licensors gain quick, low-risk reach and royalty revenue without making the goods themselves. Disney's case also shows how a licensor answers the risk of brand dilution: by keeping strict quality and branding controls, which is the quality control clause in action.

PART CSESSIONS 5–6 · STRATEGIC USE OF LICENSING
Sessions 5–6 · CILO 2

Strategic licensing: objectives, competitive uses and timing

ProblemA license signed only as a legal formality may earn some royalties but serve no wider purpose.
Big ideaStrategic licensing uses licensing deliberately to reach long-term competitive or market objectives.
How (tools)Five strategic objectives, four competitive strategies, and the strategic use of licensing at each stage of the product life cycle.
ResultThe firm knows why it licenses, how licensing helps it against competitors, and when in a product's life licensing works best.
In plain words

Sessions 5–6 define strategic licensing as licensing used as a deliberate strategy to achieve long-term competitive or market objectives, not just as a legal agreement. It has three key characteristics: it is part of the market entry strategy, it focuses on leveraging the brand or intellectual property (IP), and it aims at long-term business alignment.

The slides give five strategic objectives. Market entry means entering new regions with minimal investment. Revenue generation means earning royalties from existing IP or assets. Brand extension means expanding global visibility through partners. Resource leverage means using a local partner's infrastructure and market knowledge. Regulatory navigation means entering markets that have trade or ownership restrictions. The first and last objectives both concern entry, but for different reasons: market entry is about keeping investment low, while regulatory navigation is about reaching markets that have trade or ownership restrictions.

Licensing can also be used as a competitive strategy in four ways: offensively, for first-mover advantage and brand dominance; defensively, to block competitors and saturate the market; through cross-licensing, to gain access to complementary technology or brands; and through co-branding, to enhance value perception and reach. Finally, the slides tie licensing to the product life cycle. At introduction, licensing gives fast market entry to build awareness; in growth, rapid scale-up through partners; at maturity, sustained revenue with low investment; and in decline, a way to maximize the remaining value through partners.

Key characteristics of strategic licensing

  • Leverage: focus on brand/IP leverage

Sessions 5–6 · CILO 2

Choosing a licensing partner and managing the risks

ProblemA licensor hands its intellectual property (IP) to a partner it does not control, so the wrong partner can misuse the IP or damage the brand.
Big ideaSelect partners against clear criteria, and meet each licensing risk with a matching mitigation.
How (tools)Four partner criteria, two evaluation tools and a table of five risks with their mitigations.
ResultA partner who can carry the brand well, and an agreement with the safeguards to correct course if something goes wrong.
In plain words

Sessions 5–6 give four strategic partner criteria for a licensing partner: market access and reputation; financial and operational capabilities; cultural and regulatory alignment; and a track record of compliance and performance. Each criterion answers a different worry. Market access and reputation tell the licensor whether the partner can reach customers in a way that suits the brand; capabilities tell it whether the partner can actually produce and sell; alignment tells it whether the partner fits the local culture and rules; and the track record shows whether the partner has kept its commitments before.

To apply these criteria the slides name two evaluation tools: a SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) or TOWS (threats, opportunities, weaknesses, strengths) analysis, and partner scorecards.

Even a well-chosen partner leaves risks, and the slides pair each with a strategic mitigation. IP misuse is met with strong legal agreements and monitoring. Reputation damage is met with partner vetting and brand guidelines. Quality control problems are met with clear standards and audits. Loss of market control is met with exclusivity terms and joint reviews. Legal and compliance risk is met with local legal counsel and jurisdiction clauses. Several of these mitigations are written into the licensing agreement itself, which links this chapter back to the core clauses of Sessions 3–4: quality control, exclusivity and termination with dispute resolution.

PART DSESSIONS 7–8 · LEGAL AND ETHICAL ISSUES IN LICENSING
Sessions 7–8 · CILO 3

Why the law matters in licensing: four types of intellectual property

ProblemA licensing deal crosses borders, but intellectual property laws differ from country to country.
Big ideaTreat the licensing agreement as a legally binding contract, and know which type of intellectual property it covers.
How (tools)Four reasons legal and ethical issues matter, and the four types of intellectual property relevant in licensing: trademarks, patents, copyrights and trade secrets.
ResultLegal oversight that protects the brand and keeps the deal ethical, avoiding lawsuits, theft of intellectual property and reputation damage.
In plain words

Sessions 7–8 open with four reasons why legal and ethical issues matter in licensing. Licensing agreements are legally binding contracts. Different countries have different intellectual property (IP) laws. Legal oversight ensures brand protection and ethical compliance. And failure to address these issues can result in lawsuits, IP theft or reputation damage. Taken in order, the four reasons form an argument: because the agreement binds both sides and the law changes at every border, legal oversight is what protects the brand, and its absence is what leads to the three harms in the last point.

Since a license is permission to use IP, the next step is to be clear about what kinds of IP can be licensed. The slides list four. Trademarks protect brand names, logos and slogans, such as the Nike logo or the Coca-Cola font. Patents protect inventions and technology, such as a smartphone chip design. Copyrights protect creative content, such as software code, books and music. Trade secrets are confidential business information, such as the Coca-Cola formula.

The examples show that one company can hold several types at once. Coca-Cola appears twice in the slides' table: its font is protected as a trademark, while its formula is kept as a trade secret, confidential business information. A licensing agreement therefore has to say exactly which of a company's IP it covers, which is the job of the IP scope or grant of rights clause.

Why legal and ethical issues matter

  1. Binding: licensing agreements are legally binding contracts.
  2. Different laws: different countries have different IP laws.
  3. Oversight: legal oversight ensures brand protection and ethical compliance.
  4. Consequences: failure to address these issues can result in lawsuits, IP theft or reputation damage.

Sessions 7–8 give a global view of intellectual property (IP) protection laws in three regions. Sessions 7–8 · Case

Case: Apple vs. Qualcomm, a patent licensing dispute

ProblemApple and Qualcomm disagreed over patent licensing fees for chips used in iPhones.
Big ideaLicensing terms on standard-essential patents must be clear and fair, or they invite litigation across many jurisdictions.
How (tools)The background, the legal actions, the April 2019 settlement and four lessons learned.
ResultAfter two years of legal battles the companies settled out of court, with a payment, a new licensing agreement and resumed chip supply.
In plain words

Qualcomm is a leading chipmaker that holds key standard-essential patents (SEPs) for mobile communication technologies such as fourth-generation (4G) Long-Term Evolution (LTE). Apple used Qualcomm's chips in iPhones but argued that Qualcomm's licensing model was unfair. Qualcomm charged royalties based on the full price of the device, not just on the value of the chip, which Apple claimed was excessive. Apple also accused Qualcomm of anti-competitive practices and of double-dipping: charging both for chips and for licenses.

The dispute spread across borders. Apple filed lawsuits in the United States, China, Germany and other countries challenging Qualcomm's practices. Qualcomm counter-sued for patent infringement and demanded product bans, and courts in China and Germany temporarily banned the sale of certain iPhone models because of patent violations. Antitrust regulators in several jurisdictions also investigated, including the United States Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the European Union (EU) and Korea.

In April 2019, after two years of legal battles, Apple and Qualcomm settled out of court. Apple agreed to pay Qualcomm an undisclosed sum, estimated at 4.5 to 6 billion United States (US) dollars; the two signed a new six-year licensing agreement with an optional two-year extension; and Qualcomm resumed supplying chips for iPhones. The slides draw four lessons, listed below, which tie the case back to the chapter's themes: clear licensing terms, careful valuation of intellectual property (IP), awareness that IP law varies by country, and the strategic value of settling.

In plain words

Sessions 9–10 define franchising as a business model in which one party, the franchisor, grants another, the franchisee, the rights to operate under its brand and to sell its products or services in exchange for a fee and royalties. In plain words

A franchise disclosure document (FDD) is a legal disclosure document, required in many countries such as the United States of America (USA), that provides prospective franchisees with key information about the franchisor and the franchise system. Its purpose is threefold: to protect prospective franchisees, to promote transparency and informed decision-making, and to comply with franchise laws and regulations.

Sessions 13–14 · CILO 3 · Case

Legal risks in franchising and the Burgerim case

ProblemFranchise agreements can fail through unclear territories, false disclosures, weak dispute mechanisms or poor enforcement abroad.
Big ideaName the legal risks and mitigate them with expert advice, standardized contracts and country-specific disclosure rules.
How (tools)Four legal risks, three mitigations, and the Burgerim case (2021), a disclosure violation.
ResultThe Burgerim case shows the cost of failure: franchisees lost money, the regulator sued, and the company filed for bankruptcy.
In plain words

Sessions 13–14 name four legal risks in franchise agreements: unclear territory boundaries; misrepresentation in the franchise disclosure document (FDD); insufficient dispute resolution mechanisms; and lack of enforcement in international jurisdictions. The risks touch the same subjects as the deck's earlier slides: the territory clause, the FDD, the dispute resolution clause and the legal frameworks of different countries. The slides give three mitigations: consult legal experts, use standardized contract templates, and follow country-specific disclosure rules.

The case is FTC v. Burgerim (2021), brought by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC). Burgerim was a United States (US)-based fast-food franchise that marketed customizable mini-burgers. Between 2016 and 2019 it aggressively sold more than 1,500 franchise units, including to first-time business owners and military veterans. The issue was a violation of franchise disclosure requirements. Burgerim failed to provide accurate and complete FDDs to potential franchisees as federal law required, and omitted critical information: financial performance data, realistic costs and failure rates, and the operating difficulties prior franchisees had experienced. It also overstated the earning potential and implied that franchisees would succeed quickly. This violated the FTC Franchise Rule, which mandates full disclosure before a franchise agreement is signed.

The outcome was severe. Numerous franchisees lost thousands of dollars, many without ever opening a restaurant. In 2021 the FTC filed a lawsuit against Burgerim and its founder, Oren Loni, alleging deceptive business practices. The company later filed for bankruptcy, and its operations declined significantly. The case shows the second legal risk, misrepresentation in the FDD, in practice, and why the slides' third mitigation, following country-specific disclosure rules, matters.

  1. Unclear territory boundaries
  2. Misrepresentation in FDD
  3. Insufficient dispute resolution mechanisms
  4. Lack of enforcement in international jurisdictions

Mitigation

  • Experts: consult legal experts
  • Templates: use standardized contract templates
  • Disclosure: follow country-specific disclosure rules

Case example: FDD violation, FTC v. Burgerim (2021)

Case overview

  • Business: US-based fast-food franchise marketing customizable mini-burgers.
  • Sales: between 2016 and 2019 aggressively sold over 1,500 franchise units, including to first-time business owners and military veterans.

Issue: violation of franchise disclosure requirements

  • Inaccurate FDDs: failed to provide accurate and complete FDDs to potential franchisees as required by federal law.
  • Omissions: financial performance data; realistic costs and failure rates; operating difficulties experienced by prior franchisees.
  • Overstatement: overstated the earning potential and implied franchisees would succeed quickly.
  • Rule broken: the FTC Franchise Rule, which mandates full disclosure before signing a franchise agreement.

Outcome

  1. LossesNumerous franchisees lost thousands of dollars, many without ever opening a restaurant
  2. LawsuitIn 2021 the FTC sued Burgerim and its founder, Oren Loni, alleging deceptive business practices
  3. BankruptcyThe company later filed for bankruptcy, and its operations significantly declined

Analyzing a company's entry strategy: the assignment frameworks

ProblemEach session pair teaches its own lists and tables, but a real company has to be analyzed with several of them at once.
Big ideaThe course's seven assignments show how the topics are meant to be combined: strategy, reason, advantages and disadvantages in context, and result.
How (tools)The structure of each assignment, from the two-company comparison of Sessions 1–2 to the clause review of Sessions 13–14.
ResultA repeatable line of analysis that draws on the right chapter at each step.
In plain words

Every deck in the course ends with an assignment, and the assignments are the materials' own guide to combining the topics. The first, in Sessions 1–2, sets the basic line of analysis. For each of two companies that used different strategies to enter global markets, students explain the strategy used, the reason for choosing it, its advantages and disadvantages in the context of the company, and its result or impact: whether it succeeded or failed, and why.

The later assignments ask for the same kind of analysis in more depth for one strategy. Sessions 3–4 ask for a licensing company's overview, its type of licensing, its agreement highlights, its strategic rationale compared with franchising, exporting or a joint venture (JV), its outcomes and challenges, and the student's own view. Sessions 5–6 ask for the strategic reason for licensing, partner selection, risks and their management, and whether the strategy succeeded. Sessions 7–8 ask for a country's intellectual property (IP) types, its regulatory authority, one rule that differs from or adds to the TRIPS Agreement (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights) or European Union (EU) or United States (US) law, and a licensing case. Sessions 9–10 ask for a franchise's type and the duties of each party. Sessions 11–12 ask which development model a brand used, why it fitted, and what alternative could be justified. Sessions 13–14 ask for the function of five clauses in a franchise agreement, the risk if each is vague, missing or biased, and one improvement.

Laid side by side, the assignments use the chapters of this book as their tools. The "reason" step draws on the strategy comparison and the objectives of licensing; the "advantages and disadvantages" step draws on the benefit and limitation lists and on direct versus indirect entry; the clause and risk steps draw on the agreement chapters; and the "result" step draws on the cases, where Disney, ARM and Domino's India are presented as successes and Burgerim as a failure.
