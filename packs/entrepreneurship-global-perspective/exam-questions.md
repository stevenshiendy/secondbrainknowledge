## Q1. Comprehensively explain the concept of international entrepreneurship and innovation along with their significance to the global economy. In your answer, include: The definition of international entrepreneurship according to Zahra & George (2002) and Oviatt & McDougall (2005). The difference between International New Ventures (INVs) and Born Globals. At least two statistical data showing the contribution of immigrant entrepreneurs to the economy (e.g., from USBA, NVCA, or OECD). Real examples of companies founded by immigrants and their impact on global innovation.

**Title:** International entrepreneurship, INVs and immigrant founders

**Weight / CILO:** 25 marks · LO1 (CILO 1)

### What the question is really asking
Define international entrepreneurship in two named ways, say what innovation is, separate two kinds of fast-internationalizing firm, and prove the field matters with immigrant-entrepreneur statistics and named companies. It tests Session 1 (chapters `s1-significance-definitions` and `s1-concept-drivers`).

### Key terms
- **International entrepreneurship (Zahra & George, 2002)** — the process of creatively discovering and exploiting opportunities that lie outside a firm's domestic market in the pursuit of competitive advantage.
- **International entrepreneurship (Oviatt & McDougall, 2005)** — the discovery, enactment, evaluation and exploitation of opportunities across national borders to create future goods and services.
- **International new venture, INV (Oviatt & McDougall, 1994)** — a business organization that, from inception, seeks significant competitive advantage from the use of resources and the sale of outputs in multiple countries.
- **Born global** — named on the slides as a type of small firm that does not internationalize in stages; defined outside the slides as "a venture launched to exploit a global niche from the first day of its operations" (Tanev, 2012, p. 5). **[outside the slides]**
- **Innovation (Drucker, 1993)** — the sum of creativity and commercialization; the means by which entrepreneurs create new wealth-producing resources or enhance existing ones.

### Remember it as a line
**The line:** Border → Engine → Day One → Numbers → Names.

**In one breath:** Two definitions open the border. Innovation makes crossing it pay. Some firms cross on day one. Immigrant numbers prove it matters. Immigrant names prove it is real.

**Keep the two definitions apart:** Zahra & George say *outside* (where) and *competitive advantage* (why). Oviatt & McDougall say *across* and give four verbs in order (how): discover, enact, evaluate, exploit.

**Keep INV and born global apart:** an INV is identified by where it uses resources and sells. A born global is identified by the global niche it is built around.

**Cite from memory:** Zahra & George (2002) and Oviatt & McDougall (2005) for the definitions; Oviatt & McDougall (1994) for INVs and Tanev (2012) for born globals; Drucker (1993) and Schumpeter for innovation; NVCA (2019) and USBA (2018) for the statistics. Everything else is Pruthi & Mitra (2023).

### Reasoning staircase
1. **Border.** The field comes from entrepreneurship meeting international business, so open with the two named definitions: Zahra & George tell you where and why, Oviatt & McDougall tell you how. This raises the next question: what makes a cross-border opportunity create wealth?
2. **Engine.** Innovation does (Drucker: creativity plus commercialization; Schumpeter: new demand destroys old markets and creates new ones). Because the field carries this innovation across borders, it matters to the global economy. Some firms carry it abroad straight away, which leads to the third step.
3. **Day One.** INVs and born globals both internationalize early and rapidly. An INV is defined by multi-country resources and sales from inception; a born global by a global niche from the first day. Having said which firms these are, you still have to show that the activity matters.
4. **Numbers.** Prove it with at least two immigrant statistics, citing the source named on the slides. Statistics show the size of the effect, but not who produced it.
5. **Names.** Name immigrant-founded companies and state their impact on innovation and jobs.
6. **Close.** Link back to the drivers: migration, one of the field's five drivers, is a major source of this innovation.

### Model answer
International entrepreneurship joins two ideas. Entrepreneurship is the pursuit of opportunities to create future goods and services. International business is the activity of organizations that cross national borders. The field emerged where these two research streams meet (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Two definitions are central. Zahra and George (2002) define international entrepreneurship as the process of creatively discovering and exploiting opportunities that lie outside a firm's domestic market in the pursuit of competitive advantage. Oviatt and McDougall (2005) define it as the discovery, enactment, evaluation and exploitation of opportunities across national borders to create future goods and services. The first definition tells us where the opportunity lies (outside the home market) and why it is pursued (competitive advantage). The second tells us how it happens: it sets out the whole opportunity process and stresses its cross-border character.

Innovation is the engine inside this process. Firms innovate when they use unexploited opportunities by developing new products and business models, improving processes or combining existing components in new ways. Drucker (1993) calls innovation the sum of creativity and commercialization. It is the means by which entrepreneurs create new wealth-producing resources or give existing resources greater wealth-creating potential. Schumpeter adds the consequence: innovations create new demand, which destroys existing markets and creates new ones. This is why international entrepreneurship matters to the global economy: it carries this wealth-creating innovation across borders.

A third view treats the field as the phenomenon of early and rapid internationalization of new and small firms, and two labels describe such firms. International new ventures (INVs), in Oviatt and McDougall's (1994) definition, are business organizations that, from inception, seek significant competitive advantage from the use of resources and the sale of outputs in multiple countries. So the defining feature of an INV is that its resources and sales are international from the start. Born globals are named on the course slides as one type of small firm that the stages model cannot explain, alongside traditional exporters and late starters, because they do not internationalize step by step. Tanev (2012) defines a born-global firm as "a venture launched to exploit a global niche from the first day of its operations" (p. 5). Such firms typically export within a couple of years of founding and may export a quarter or more of their output. **[outside the slides]** The difference is therefore this. An INV is identified by using resources and selling in several countries from inception. A born global is identified by being built from day one around a global niche, which is usually seen in how quickly and how much it exports. Tanev (2012) treats "international new venture" as one of several labels for early internationalizers, and argues that entering foreign niches early does not on its own make a firm born global. **[outside the slides]**

The data on immigrant entrepreneurs show how significant this activity is. In the United Kingdom, migrant-founded enterprises employ 1.16 million people and contribute 14% of job creation in small and medium-sized firms (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). In the United States, innovative start-ups founded by immigrant entrepreneurs, such as Uber and eBay, account for as many as 50% of gross jobs created annually between 1980 and 2010 (NVCA, 2019). In Southern California, firms founded by Chinese Americans and Taiwanese Americans employ more than 440,000 people and generate $70 billion in taxable revenues (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). More broadly, small businesses in the USA created 55% of all jobs and 66% of net new jobs in 2018 (USBA, 2018). In some OECD countries, immigrants are about 1.5 times as likely as the native-born to be self-employed (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

Named companies show the impact on global innovation. Uber and eBay, both founded by immigrant entrepreneurs, are innovative start-ups that built new markets. The Chinese and Taiwanese American firms did more than create jobs in California. They also expanded business opportunities and contributed to the development of China's information and communication technology industries, which is innovation flowing back across borders. Immigrants who return home to start up also bring innovation, competition and an entrepreneurial culture to their home countries (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Venture-backed companies represented 63% of market capitalization and 85% of R&D among U.S. public companies (NVCA, 2019).

In conclusion, international entrepreneurship, whether defined through opportunities outside the home market (Zahra & George, 2002) or across national borders (Oviatt & McDougall, 2005), is significant because it spreads innovation and creates jobs and wealth across countries. Immigrant founders, from Uber and eBay to the Chinese and Taiwanese American firms of California, show that migration, one of the five drivers of the field, is a major source of that innovation.

## Q2. Analyze the role of small firms in a country's economy using entrepreneurship theories (Personality Theory, Psycho-sociological Theory, Cognitive Theories). In your answer: Explain the difference between entrepreneurship and small business. Elaborate on the advantages and disadvantages of small firms from quantitative and qualitative aspects. Provide arguments on why small firms are often called the backbone of job creation in the United States, including data from USBA (2018). Relate it to Schumpeter's concept of creative destruction.

**Title:** Small firms, entrepreneurship theories and job creation

**Weight / CILO:** 25 marks · LO1 (CILO 1)

### What the question is really asking
Explain why people start small firms (three theories), separate entrepreneurship from small business, weigh what small size helps and hurts, prove small firms' job role with USBA (2018) data, and connect it all to Schumpeter. It tests Session 2 (chapters `s2-entrepreneurs`, `s2-small-firms`, `s2-significance`).

### Key terms
- **Entrepreneurs** — individuals who start new business ventures, unlike employees who work for someone else's business.
- **Small business** — fewer than 500 employees and under $5 million in annual sales; the focus is on established small firms.
- **Personality theory** — entrepreneurs are "born", not "made"; personality shapes opportunity recognition.
- **Psycho-sociological theory** — "disgruntlement" or job dissatisfaction can push people into entrepreneurship.
- **Cognitive theories** — entrepreneurs perceive market gaps or unnoticed opportunities.
- **Creative destruction (Schumpeter)** — entrepreneurs bring innovations to market, which create new demand, destroying existing markets and creating new ones.

### Remember it as a line
**The line:** Shop → Three Doors → Balance → Backbone → Wrecking Ball.

**In one breath:** Entrepreneurship starts; small business stays. People go in because they are born to, pushed or notice a gap. Being small cuts both ways. Even so, small firms provide 55% of all jobs and 66% of net new jobs. And each new market wrecks an old one.

**Keep the three theories apart:** Personality = *born*. Psycho-sociological = *pushed*. Cognitive = *noticed*.

**Why the balance tips both ways:** the same smallness that gives drive, informality and closeness to the market also means no scale, tight money, tight people and no market control.

**Cite from memory:** USBA (2018) for the job data and Schumpeter for creative destruction. Everything else, including the three theories, is Pruthi & Mitra (2023).

### Reasoning staircase
1. **Shop.** Separate the two words first, because the rest of the answer depends on them: entrepreneurship is the start-up context and its innovative effort; small business is the established small firm. This raises the question of why anyone starts one.
2. **Three Doors.** Three theories answer it: personality (born), dissatisfaction (pushed) and cognition of gaps (noticed). So many ways in help explain why small firms are so numerous. If there are that many, what does being small do to them?
3. **Balance.** Set out the quantitative and qualitative features, then show that the advantages and the disadvantages both follow from those features. The disadvantages raise an obvious doubt: can such constrained firms really carry an economy?
4. **Backbone.** Yes: argue the backbone-of-jobs claim with USBA (2018) and related U.S. data. That shows how many jobs they provide, but not what they do to markets.
5. **Wrecking Ball.** Link to creative destruction: small firms bring innovations that create demand and new markets, and they feed innovation into large firms.
6. **Close.** State the overall role in one sentence: employment plus renewal.

### Model answer
Small firms play a central role in a country's economy: they are the vehicles through which entrepreneurs create jobs, wealth and innovation. To analyze that role, first separate two terms. Entrepreneurs are individuals who start new business ventures; unlike employees, they build their own business. Entrepreneurship focuses on the context, such as the start-up process, where innovative effort is required. Small business focuses on established small firms, such as a corner grocery or bakery, a neighbourhood restaurant, a laundromat or a dry-cleaner (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). A small business is defined as having fewer than 500 employees and selling less than $5 million of products and services a year. Every small firm begins with an entrepreneurial act, but not every small business remains entrepreneurial.

Three theories explain why people found these firms. Personality theory holds that individual personality characteristics influence opportunity recognition, so people become entrepreneurs because of their personality: entrepreneurs are "born", not "made". Psycho-sociological theory points out that not all entrepreneurs start for positive, pull-based reasons: "disgruntlement", or dissatisfaction with a job, can be the trigger. Cognitive theories argue that entrepreneurs perceive market gaps or identify opportunities others have not noticed (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Together, the theories show that small firms arise from who people are, from what pushes them out of employment and from what they see in the market. This variety helps explain why small firms are so numerous: USBA (2018) counts 30.2 million small businesses in the USA, 99.9% of all U.S. firms.

Small firms differ from large ones in two ways. Quantitatively, they have few employees, small initial capital, a small share of their industry, low turnover and a small market. Size bands run from the self-employed (one person) to micro-businesses (up to 19 employees), small businesses (20–99) and medium-sized businesses (100–499) (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). The European Union instead treats firms under 50 employees as SMEs and under 250 as medium-sized. Qualitatively, small firms have an informal culture, the family often plays a role, and the owner-founder is also the manager, personally responsible for the firm's functions, whereas in large firms ownership is separated from control. These features cut both ways. The advantages are entrepreneurial motivation and dynamism, an informal structure and organization, and closeness to the market, with control over customers' purchasing based on informal relationships. The disadvantages are no economies of scale or scope; financial resource constraints; human resource constraints, such as centralization, lack of delegation, owners personally procuring supplies and monitoring quality, and small-batch production; and lack of market control, with publicity left to word of mouth and no concrete sales and marketing strategy.

Despite these constraints, small firms are often called the backbone of job creation in the United States, and the data support the claim. According to USBA (2018), the 30.2 million small businesses make up 99.7% of U.S. firms with paid employees, employ 47.5% of private sector employees and pay 40.8% of private sector payroll. In 2018, small businesses provided 55% of all jobs and 66% of all net new jobs. In 2015 they employed 58.9 million people and created 1.9 million net jobs, with firms of fewer than 20 employees adding 1.1 million of them. Small businesses accounted for 64% of net new jobs between 1993 and 2011 (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023), and new entrepreneurial firms have added 8 million jobs since 1990 (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). More than 25.1 million microenterprises with five or fewer employees provide 22% of private employment, and franchises accounted for 40% of retail sales and some 8 million jobs in 2014.

Schumpeter's concept of creative destruction explains why this job creation also renews the economy. The chain has four links. An invention is a new idea from creativity. It becomes an innovation when it is used in a new commercial product or service. Entrepreneurs, as owner-managers who start businesses to exploit inventions, bring innovations to market, and the innovations create new demand. New demand then destroys existing markets and creates new ones, generating wealth for the entrepreneur and employment for others (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). So small firms are not only employers but agents of change. Their wealth potential is large: the self-employed are fewer than 20% of workers but almost two-thirds of millionaires, and founders such as Sergey Brin and Larry Page (Google) and Bill Gates (Microsoft) grew small firms into global businesses. Small firms also feed innovation into large firms, which buy them to enter new markets, as when Facebook bought WhatsApp to enter the mobile market and Microsoft bought Skype to enter internet communications.

In conclusion, small firms arise from personality, dissatisfaction and opportunity recognition. They are agile but resource-constrained. They provide most U.S. jobs and net new jobs according to USBA (2018). Through Schumpeter's creative destruction, they keep replacing old markets with new ones. That combination of employment and renewal is their role in the economy.

## Q3. Based on the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) 2018 data, answer the following: Explain what the Total Entrepreneurial Activity (TEA) Index is and how it is measured. Analyze the differences in entrepreneurial motivation (opportunity vs necessity entrepreneurs) in low-, middle-, and high-income countries. Elaborate on the U-shaped relationship between the level of entrepreneurial activity and a country's economic development. Discuss how political, economic, socio-cultural, and technological factors influence the development of international entrepreneurship, referring to Hofstede's cultural dimensions.

**Title:** GEM data: TEA, motivation and the U-shaped curve

**Weight / CILO:** 25 marks · LO1 & LO2 (CILO 1 and 2)

### What the question is really asking
Explain GEM's main measure, compare motivations across income levels, explain the U-shape, and use the four external influences, with Hofstede's dimensions for culture, to explain why countries differ. It tests Session 3 (chapters `s3-gem`, `s3-external-environment`, `s3-culture-technology`).

### Key terms
- **GEM** — Global Entrepreneurship Monitor: long-term research measuring and explaining differences in entrepreneurial activity among countries.
- **TEA index** — the percentage of the adult population that has taken some action towards creating a new business in the past year, or owns and manages an active business less than 42 months old.
- **Opportunity entrepreneurs** — start a business to exploit an opportunity.
- **Necessity entrepreneurs** — start a business to sustain themselves because other work options are absent or unsatisfactory.
- **Hofstede's dimensions** — power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism/collectivism, masculinity/femininity, long-term orientation (Hofstede, 1991).

### Remember it as a line
**The line:** Gauge → Motive → The U → Four Winds → Five Dials.

**In one breath:** TEA counts how many. Motive says why. Put the two motives together and you get the U. Four winds and five dials explain where a country sits, and no single setting wins.

**Why the curve is a U:** on the left, necessity is high, then falls as jobs become available as a fall-back. On the right, opportunity starts to increase as incomes rise further. And GEM finds no causal link with GDP, so the U is a shape and not a cause.

**Hofstede's five dials as questions:** Power distance: how much unequal power is accepted? Uncertainty avoidance: how threatening is ambiguity? Individualism: independence of action or group loyalty? Masculinity: how oriented to money and things? Long-term orientation: how far ahead?

**Cite from memory:** GEM (2018) for the data and Hofstede (1991) for the dimensions. Everything else is Pruthi & Mitra (2023).

### Reasoning staircase
1. **Gauge.** Define GEM and the TEA index, then its two components and the 42-month cut-off. A gauge tells you how many people are starting businesses, but not why.
2. **Motive.** So give the motive: the opportunity shares (37%, 42%, 51%) show a shift from necessity to opportunity as income rises. Motive is what explains the shape of the next step.
3. **The U.** Necessity falls as jobs appear, then opportunity lifts activity again, and together these make the U. Stress that GEM finds no causal link with GDP. If income does not cause activity, something else has to explain why countries differ.
4. **Four Winds.** Political: the political system and government intervention (conditions versus direct measures). Economic: development level (push versus pull) and the state of the economy. Technological: ICT reaches customers abroad. Socio-cultural: attitudes, self-perceptions and affiliations, which leads straight to culture.
5. **Five Dials.** Use Hofstede's dimensions to describe culture, with the countries at each end.

### Model answer
The Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) is long-term, longitudinal research. It sets out to measure differences in entrepreneurial activity among countries, uncover the factors that determine national levels, and identify policies that could raise them. It began by comparing the G7 countries with Denmark, Finland and Israel. It now uses phone surveys and, in developing countries such as China, India and Uganda, face-to-face interviews. GEM defines entrepreneurship broadly as any attempt to create a new business or to expand an existing one, by an individual, a team or an established business (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

GEM's central measure is the Total Entrepreneurial Activity (TEA) index. It is the percentage of the adult population that has taken some action towards creating a new business in the past year, or who are owner-managers of an active business less than 42 months old. It is measured by adding two rates: the nascent start-up rate, covering people in the process of setting up a business, and the new business ownership rate, covering those actually owning and running early-stage businesses. GEM 2018 data for 49 economies show TEA rates ranging from a few per cent in Japan and much of Europe to around 40% in Angola (GEM, 2018). GEM 2018 also shows variation by type (18.6% of TEA is in family businesses and 8.0% are solo), by sector, and by gender, with men twice as likely as women to start a business.

TEA shows how many people are starting businesses, but not why, and motivation differs sharply by income level. GEM distinguishes opportunity entrepreneurs, who start a business to exploit an opportunity, from necessity entrepreneurs, who start one because all other work options are absent or unsatisfactory. The share of opportunity entrepreneurs rises from an average of 37% in low-income economies to 42% in middle-income and 51% in high-income economies (GEM, 2018). In other words, most entrepreneurs in low-income economies are driven by necessity, while in high-income economies just over half are driven by opportunity. This matches the push and pull pattern of Session 3: developing economies show push motivations, higher business birth rates and social entrepreneurship, while developed economies show pull motivations and a goal of long-term ownership.

These findings produce the U-shaped relationship. GEM finds no causal relationship between the level of entrepreneurial activity and a country's GDP, per capita income or growth. Instead, the relationship with economic development is U-shaped, and motivation explains each arm. In low- and middle-income countries, entrepreneurial activity is relatively high and much of it is necessity-driven, but it falls as income levels rise and jobs become available as a fall-back. In high-income countries necessity entrepreneurship is low, but opportunity entrepreneurship starts to increase as incomes rise further, which lifts activity again (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

Four external influences explain why countries differ. Politically, the type of system matters (free market versus central planning), and so does government intervention. Government either sets the conditions for enterprise, through tax reforms and regulations such as the time to register a business, low interest rates and listing rules for small firms, or supports the process directly through finance, specialist advice and training, and investment in R&D. Economically, the level of development shapes motivation, and the state of the economy matters: U.S. TEA fell after the 2009/2010 financial crisis, while in China activity has diminished as sustained growth let companies grow larger. Technologically, information and communication technology connects entrepreneurs with international customers and lets them coordinate at a distance, so they can sell outside their home market, which is directly relevant to international entrepreneurship.

Socio-culturally, GEM measures societal attitudes (status of entrepreneurs, career choice, media attention), self-perceptions (ease of starting a business) and affiliations with entrepreneurs, which supply role models, investors and mentors. National culture is described by Hofstede's dimensions (Hofstede, 1991). Power distance, the acceptance of unequal power, is highest in Malaysia and Mexico and lowest in Germany and Austria. Uncertainty avoidance, the sense of threat from ambiguity, is highest in Greece and Japan and lowest in the USA and Singapore; low uncertainty avoidance suits the risk of starting a venture. Individualism, valuing independence of action over group loyalty, is highest in the USA and Canada and lowest in Mexico and Guatemala, and entrepreneurs in developed European countries are described as the most individualistic and self-confident. Masculinity, an orientation to money and things, is highest in Japan and Mexico. Long-term orientation is highest in Hong Kong and Japan. Universities add to the cultural environment by providing knowledge, conditioning mindsets and offering incubation and support networks.

However, Session 3 warns that these influences are ambiguous and interdependent. Economically successful nations score very differently on each Hofstede dimension, so there is no single "right" culture for entrepreneurship. In collectivist Asian countries, enterprise often takes the form of large organizations, such as Japan's kaisha and Korea's chaebol. In conclusion, GEM 2018 shows that how much and why people start businesses depends on a country's income level, and that political, economic, socio-cultural and technological factors together, not any one of them, shape the development of international entrepreneurship.

### Key terms
- **Region** — a unit tied to the governance of policies for economic development, social cohesion and environmental welfare; usually sub-national, but it can span countries, as in the European Region.

- **Social capital** — the networks and relationships of people beyond the individual entrepreneur.

### Model answer
International entrepreneurship is often discussed at the level of countries, but Session 4 argues that it takes root in regions. Businesses trade, form strategic alliances, network, invest overseas and set up offshore operations on the basis of information about attractive regions. Supply and value chains are increasingly international, and the more global a region is economically, the more likely it is also a powerful local economy (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). A region is associated with the governance of policies and institutions that promote economic development, social cohesion and environmental welfare. Its meaning varies by country. It usually refers to a sub-national administrative unit, but it can also cover several countries, as in the European Region or Community. A region's identity rests on the beliefs, values and cultural perceptions of its people. Regions differ significantly both between and within countries, which is why entrepreneurs and policy makers watch business formation at regional level.

Knowledge spillovers explain much of how regions evolve. Knowledge spillovers (KSs) are knowledge externalities bounded in space. Knowledge created in one organization is "leaky", so rival organizations nearby can appropriate some of it without creating it themselves. Firms located close to key sources of knowledge, intelligence and data can therefore introduce innovations faster than rivals elsewhere. Spillovers flow through four channels: employees changing jobs, knowledge exchange with universities, informal collaborations among firms, and the sharing of know-how along value chains. Their effect depends on physical, institutional, social and socio-psychological factors that create framework conditions for entrepreneurship and wealth creation. Regions can be distinguished by how much knowledge they create and spill over, and this explains their levels of innovation and new firm creation.

The people and relationships of a region determine how well it uses that knowledge. Some authors argue that human capital is the key to economic growth, especially in urban areas. A place's capacity to attract talent and tolerate the diversity of talented people fuses with its capacity for invention and new technology, and the cultural diversity of urban networks brings in different kinds of human capital that promote innovation, accelerate information flows and raise new firm formation. Others argue that social capital plays a more integral role, because it harnesses the contributions of people other than the individual entrepreneur, namely their networks and relationships. Entrepreneurial capital brings these together. It is a composite form of capital defined at the economic (region and industry), organizational and personal (team and individual) levels, and the amalgam of institutions, talent and technology can be called creative entrepreneurial capital. Institutions, good governance and the generation and attraction of talent give regions a local edge over their neighbours, and the mix and strength of human, social, technological and entrepreneurial capital is a good measure of regional competitiveness (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

Growth corridors connect such regions across borders. Corridors are bundles of infrastructure, including rail links for passengers or freight and highways, that connect two or more urban areas in one country or across adjacent countries, enhanced by ICT, power lines and pipes for water, gas, oil and sewage. Growth corridors are instruments for boosting development in developing economies, based on World Economic Forum deliberations and the World Bank's World Development Reports. A corridor develops in stages. The economic potential of a basic transport route drives it. Hard infrastructure creates a transport corridor. Growing freight and passenger volumes then call for soft infrastructure, meaning logistics, legal, institutional and management support. Finally, the ripple effect of efficiency stimulates further activity and attracts investment, producing an economic corridor. The first was the Maputo Development Corridor (MDC), set up in 1996, whose broad success led the New Partnership for Africa's Development (NEPAD) to replicate the model throughout Africa (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Corridors can run across urban and rural areas in more than one country and can draw funding from governments, private firms, public-private partnerships, multilateral agencies and NGOs, so they link local regions with cross-border regions. As efficiency and investment build along the route, the connected regions function together as a transnational region, which is the process named in the session's learning objective LO4. Session 4 also notes that interest has since shifted towards global value chains, which integrate the international value chains of connected sectors.

Regional clusters of entrepreneurship show the result. Silicon Valley is the course's example of a regional cluster of entrepreneurship (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023), a place where the spillovers and capital described above concentrate. Session 3 adds that entrepreneurship varies regionally in other ways: Taiwan has lower start-up rates than its neighbours but high established-business and employee entrepreneurship rates, and attitudes to entrepreneurship vary more within regions than across them. Southern California, where firms founded by Chinese and Taiwanese Americans employ more than 440,000 people and helped develop China's ICT industries, shows how a regional economy can also link to a region abroad.

In conclusion, regions, and especially cities, become international hubs when spillovers, a strong mix of human, social and entrepreneurial capital, good institutions and connecting infrastructure create critical mass. As Session 4 summarizes, that critical mass is partly a function of internationalization: such regions bring the world outside into their local environment.

### Model answer
International migration has become a major source of entrepreneurship. Since the International Conference on Population and Development in 1994, the link between migration and development has risen steadily on the international agenda, and the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development includes migration-related targets with regular reviews of progress (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). The economic reasoning runs like this. Migrants transfer knowledge and skills from their country of origin and acquire new capabilities in their country of residence. This helps the labour market and economy of the country of residence. Using those skills also mobilizes resources and raises the personal motivation to set up enterprises in both the destination country and the country of origin. Migration flows have grown rapidly, with over 60% of international migrants living in Asia or Europe, and settlement is shaped by geography, history and growing awareness of migrants' technical and entrepreneurial skills. Developed economies such as the United States, the United Kingdom and Germany now have increasing numbers of successful start-ups and growing firms run by migrant entrepreneurs. In most OECD countries immigrants start businesses at the same rate as the native-born, and in some they are about 1.5 times as likely to be self-employed, driven by both necessity and opportunity (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

The trajectory of migrant entrepreneurship has changed. Historically, immigrant businesses operated in highly competitive industries with low barriers to entry and low skill requirements, serving minority communities. Migrant entrepreneurship has now moved beyond traditional ethnic businesses into a wide range of technology sectors and innovative areas, locally and globally. Session 7 explains this shift with the concept of dual habitus. The migrant experience lies in a duality of contextual knowledge, empathy and access to hard and soft resources, which stems from the migrant's dual habitus in the country of destination and the country of origin. Knowing two contexts lets migrants see opportunities and serve customers in both, and access to resources in both widens what they can build. So global engagement enables migrants to break out of stereotypical minority business enclaves, grow their ventures and gain competitive advantage. Migrant entrepreneurs are not passive adherents to institutional constraints; they actively mould those institutions to suit their own initiatives. Across the course's cases, the interest in going international and using that experience to renew home ties is constant, and the intersection of institutional structures and human agency in the dual habitus explains both the scope of and the variation in global entrepreneurship (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

Migrant entrepreneurs still face real barriers. First, their actions are constrained by home-country endowments and institutional structures. Second, little is known about their networks, capabilities and locational dynamics in multiple institutional contexts. Third, global migrant entrepreneurship does not feature adequately in the work of policy makers, technology developers and business strategy analysts. Fourth, the systematic collection of granular data is needed to support informed decisions. Refugees face these barriers in a sharper form. Welcoming refugees is an investment with substantial economic dividends, since enterprising refugees start businesses that create wealth, employ locals, make the economy more dynamic and boost international trade and investment. Yet insecurity, legal rights and institutional issues make refugees different from other migrants, and fragility best describes the economic and social conditions that affect refugee entrepreneurship (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

The cases show global migrant networks of innovation at work. In Israel, outcomes for Israeli and Palestinian high-tech entrepreneurs depended on the unequal "capital set" of the migrants and on institutional arrangements. In Korea, the Hallyu or Korean Wave in film, television and music led to joint film production with China, drawing on the Chinese government's facilitation of cross-border flows of investors, directors and producers and creating new firms in technical service sectors, a clear case of cross-border knowledge and technology transfer. Japanese transnational entrepreneurs start "ex-pat" businesses in their late 30s to early 40s, often with savings from Japan, after exposure to overseas life during their careers. At the level of firms and regions, migrant networks generate positive externalities: migrants' specialisms create intermediary functions that serve larger multinational corporations, their networks ease the financial constraints of fast-growing firms, and firms with migrant co-owners and more migrant workers are more likely to be innovative. Migrant entrepreneurs who first enter their home country to found a global venture are more likely to found born-global ventures. Global migrant entrepreneurship contributes directly to international business creation and growth, finance and investment, R&D and innovation, and international technology transfer (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Session 1 adds concrete examples: immigrant-founded start-ups such as Uber and eBay, and firms founded by Chinese and Taiwanese Americans in Southern California that employ more than 440,000 people and helped develop China's ICT industries, a direct case of technology transfer back to a home country.

In conclusion, migration fosters entrepreneurship by moving skills and resources between countries. The dual habitus explains why migrant ventures have moved from ethnic enclaves into technology and global markets. Refugees and other migrants still face institutional, data and policy barriers. Global migrant networks, from Hallyu co-productions to California's Chinese and Taiwanese American firms, show their contribution to innovation and technology transfer. Global Migrant Entrepreneurship, by migrants with a foothold in their country of residence, now affects policy from migration to trade and deserves the attention the course argues it lacks.
