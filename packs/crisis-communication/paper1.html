Paper 1 · Experiment · Frontiers in Psychology

Why scapegoating can ruin an apology

Youk, S., & Park, H. S. (2023). A single experiment on what happens when an apology is followed by compensation, or by blame. The first chapter tells the whole article as one story; the chapters after it follow the argument in order.

Paper 1 · The whole article

The article as one story, and how to read its numbers

ProblemThe argument is spread across theory, method and statistics, so the thread is easy to lose.
Big ideaOne sentence carries the article: what follows “sorry” decides whether “sorry” is believed.
How (tools)A five-step story, a worked example using the article’s own coefficients, and a list of common misreadings.
ResultThe later chapters become details of a story already known.
In plain words

Picture the situation the participants read. The seat belts in your new car have been malfunctioning, and there has been a crash with casualties. The company says it is sorry. Everything now depends on the next sentence. If it promises to compensate the victims, the apology holds together. If it blames the seat belt subcontractor, the same apology starts to look fake.

That is the whole article. The theory explains why the next sentence should matter, the experiment isolates that sentence, and the statistics show the route the damage takes: the sentence changes how appropriate the apology seems, and that judgement then moves the company’s reputation. The damage is also not equal for everyone. It is largest among readers who feel the company owes them an explanation.

The story in five steps

  1. The puzzleSouth Korean companies apologise whatever the crisis type, and close to half of those apologies were judged insincere.
  2. The ideaA company owes two things: responsibility for the crisis, and responsibility for making it understood.
  3. The test392 adults read one crisis and one apology, with one sentence changed.
  4. The answerScapegoating lowers appropriateness, and through it reputation, most of all for those expecting an explanation.
  5. The boundaryThis is one culture and one fictional crisis, and it is not a rule to always compensate.

Following the numbers through

Two coefficients combine into one effect, because the model is a chain. Swapping compensation for scapegoating changes appropriateness, and each point of appropriateness is worth 0.70 points of reputation. Multiplying along the chain gives a rough size for the damage to reputation at each level of symmetry. The calculation below uses the coefficients from the paper; the reputation figures are worked out here to aid understanding and are not quoted from it.

Drop in appropriateness = −1.16 + (−0.19 × symmetry value) Low symmetry: −1.16 + (−0.19 × −1.49) ≈ −0.88 High symmetry: −1.16 + (−0.19 × 1.17) ≈ −1.38 Effect on reputation = drop × 0.70 At the mean: −1.16 × 0.70 ≈ −0.81

Common misreadings

MisreadingWhat the article shows
Apology type does not affect reputationIt does, but only through appropriateness. The direct path is the one that is not significant.
Only people who expect an explanation punish a scapegoatThe rating drops for everyone. Expectation changes the size of the drop, from −0.88 to −1.38.
Blaming an outsider is safeThe scapegoat here was an outside subcontractor, the milder kind, and participants still punished it.
Companies should always compensateThe authors warn against this. Compensation carries legal costs, and a deny apology may be relatively fine in some conditions.
The study measures how much compensation addsIt cannot, because there is no “apology only” control. It compares only the two versions.

The same apology, two endings

The ending that held up

“We apologize for the incident regarding the seat belt malfunction.” An account of the crash follows, then a promise to compensate the victims. Both moves say the same thing, so the message is consistent.

The ending that backfired

The same apology and the same account, but the statement then blames the seat belt subcontractor. The first sentence accepts fault and the last one moves it elsewhere, so the message contradicts itself.

Paper 1 · Theory and gaps

The matching rule and its two gaps: from SCCT to CRT

ProblemCompanies in South Korea apologise whatever the crisis type, and the leading theory does not explain what happens in the mind of the reader.
Big ideaA company in a crisis carries two separate duties: one for causing it and one for making it understood.
How (tools)Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT) joined with the Theory of Communicative Responsibility (CRT).
ResultA sharper question: what follows the word “sorry”, and for whom does it matter most?
In plain words

Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT) gives a simple instruction: judge how responsible the public thinks you are, then match the response. More responsibility calls for more accommodation. A company that is itself a victim can deny, one caught in an accident can diminish, and one whose crisis was preventable must rebuild through apology and compensation.

The authors saw two problems. The first is that practice does not follow the theory. In South Korea, 78.65% of companies apologise whatever the crisis type, because apologising is a social norm: Koreans rate apologies well and copy each other’s. The “sorry” is also often mixed with deny or diminish moves, and close to half of Korean corporate apologies were judged insincere (Kim & Yang, 2012). The second is that SCCT explains which response fits, but not why it works in the mind of the receiver.

To fill the second gap the authors borrow the Theory of Communicative Responsibility (CRT). It separates the question of who caused the crisis from the question of who must make sure the crisis is understood. These are different duties, and a company can owe the second even when it owes little of the first.

The matching rule

  • Victim cluster: low responsibility (natural disaster, rumour, product tampering). Deny strategies: attack the accuser, or blame another party, which is scapegoating.
  • Accidental cluster: moderate responsibility (technical error, product malfunction). Diminish strategies: excuse, “it was uncontrollable”, “damage is small”.
  • Preventable cluster: high responsibility (misconduct, human-error recall, organisational misdeed). Rebuild strategies: apology, compensation (material and symbolic aid).
  • Evidence for the rule: in a meta-analysis (Ma & Zhan, 2016), reputation falls as responsibility rises (r = −0.54) and matched responses work better (r = 0.23).

The two gaps

Gap 1: practice differs from theory

In South Korea, 78.65% of companies apologise whatever the crisis type, often mixing the apology with deny or diminish moves. Close to half of Korean corporate apologies were judged insincere.

Gap 2: SCCT ignores the listener

SCCT explains which response fits, not why it works in the mind of the receiver. The authors borrow CRT to explain that step.

Paper 1 · Method

The experiment: one sentence changed, 392 readers

ProblemComparing real corporate apologies would mix the wording with each company’s history and reputation.
Big ideaKeep everything identical except the sentence that follows the apology.
How (tools)An online vignette experiment with a fictional company, a random split and 5-point rating scales.
ResultAny difference in ratings can be traced to compensation or scapegoating.
In plain words

A vignette experiment asks participants to read a short constructed scenario in which only the variable being tested differs between groups. Here, 392 South Korean adults from a national online panel imagined that the seat belts in their new car had been malfunctioning, then read news of a crash with casualties. They were split at random into two groups, and each read a version of the company’s response.

Both versions apologised in the same words and gave the same account of the crash. They differed in one sentence. The accommodative apology added a promise to compensate the victims. The deny apology blamed the seat belt subcontractor. Everyone then rated the same three things, which means a difference between groups points back to that one sentence.

The company was fictional, so prior attitudes toward a real brand could not leak in. The seat-belt scenario was chosen because Korean auto crises are often safety defects, and the materials had been validated in an earlier study (Im et al., 2021).

The sample

  • Size and source: 392 South Koreans from a national online panel.
  • Profile: 49.5% women, mean age 44.6, 85% with a degree.

The measures

All three measures used 5-point scales, and all were reliable. Cronbach’s α measures the internal consistency of a multi-item scale, and a value above .80 is good.

MeasureItemsα
Communicative responsibility of the company, and of the participant3 each.81
Apology appropriateness: sincerity, acceptability, effectiveness12.98
Company reputation5.87

How “symmetry” was scored

The symmetry score is the company’s communicative responsibility minus the participant’s own.

Symmetry = company's responsibility − participant's own Above 0: the company should do more of the explaining than me Exactly 0: the duty is shared equally Below 0: it is on me to understand

Paper 1 · Model

The model they tested: mediated moderation

ProblemShowing that one apology beats another does not explain how the effect reaches reputation or for whom it is strongest.
Big ideaApology type works through appropriateness, and symmetry changes the strength of the first step.
How (tools)A mediated moderation analysis using PROCESS Model 8.
ResultScapegoating cuts appropriateness by 1.16 points, and reputation moves only through appropriateness.
In plain words

Two terms carry this chapter. A mediator is the step through which one variable affects another. A moderator is a variable that changes how strong that effect is. The model combines them: apology type affects reputation through appropriateness, and the first link is stronger or weaker depending on a person’s symmetry score.

The coefficients tell the story in order. Moving from compensation to scapegoating cuts perceived appropriateness by 1.16 points on a 5-point scale. Each point of appropriateness then lifts reputation by 0.70. The direct path from apology type to reputation has a coefficient of just 0.03, which is not significant, so the whole effect travels through the judgement of the apology.

The coefficients to remember

PathBMeaning
Apology type → appropriateness−1.16***The scapegoat version is rated less appropriate
Apology type × symmetry → appropriateness−0.19**The drop is larger for people with higher symmetry scores
Appropriateness → reputation0.70***A more appropriate apology goes with a better reputation
Apology type → reputation (direct)0.03Not significant

Variance explained

  • Appropriateness: R² = .42.
  • Reputation: R² = .67.

Reading the statistics

  • B: an unstandardised effect size.
  • R²: the share of variance explained.
  • p: the probability of a result this strong if there were no real effect.
  • PROCESS Model 8: Hayes’ regression template in which a moderator affects both the first link and the direct path of a mediation.

Paper 1 · Results

What they found: every prediction supported

ProblemFour hypotheses and one research question needed testing against the data.
Big ideaEveryone rates the compensation apology about the same; the scapegoat apology splits people.
How (tools)A manipulation check, regression paths and simple slopes at three levels of symmetry.
ResultAll four hypotheses are supported, and the direct effect on reputation is absent.
In plain words

A manipulation check tests whether participants actually noticed the difference between conditions. They did: the compensation version read as accepting far more blame (mean 3.87 against 2.22). With that settled, each hypothesis held. Compensation made the apology more appropriate, appropriateness improved reputation, people expected the company to do more of the explaining, and that expectation widened the gap between the two apologies.

The most useful picture is the slope chart. Starting from the compensation apology, all three lines begin at about the same height. Moving to the scapegoat apology, all three fall, but the line for people who most expect the company to explain falls furthest. Scapegoating hurts everyone’s judgement of the apology, and it hurts most among those who feel owed an explanation.

Who holds the duty to explain

Reading the slope chart: the paper states that the moderator was mean-centered, so the symmetry values on the lines (−1.49, 0.17, 1.17) are distances from the mean of 1.49, not raw scores. A value of −1.49 is a raw score of 0, and 1.17 is about 2.66. The drops reported in Table 2 of the paper follow from this: −1.16 − 0.19 × (−1.49) ≈ −0.88, −1.16 − 0.19 × 0.17 ≈ −1.19 and −1.16 − 0.19 × 1.17 ≈ −1.38. The low line is therefore people who share the duty equally, and not people who take it on themselves.

Paper 1 · Analysis

Why scapegoating backfires, and how far the result travels

ProblemA clean result invites the over-simple advice to always compensate.
Big idea“Sorry” implies fault, so what follows must agree with it.
How (tools)The authors’ own explanation and caveats, plus a reading of the design’s strengths and limits.
ResultStrong evidence on one message decision in one culture, with named boundaries.
In plain words

Saying “sorry” implies “it is our fault”. Compensation confirms that message: two rebuild strategies, one consistent meaning. Scapegoating contradicts it: a rebuild move glued to a deny move, so the “sorry” looks fake. People who feel the company owes them a clear explanation read the contradiction as a broken expectation, and punish it more. In collectivist Korea, pushing blame onto one part of the chain, such as a subcontractor, may also clash with the norm of collective responsibility.

The authors warn that this is not a rule to always compensate. A deny apology may be relatively fine when the crisis is simple to understand, when the culture tolerates uncertainty, or when the company is clearly a victim too. Compensation also carries costs: legal liability, and a harsh crisis can make an admission of fault fuel more blame.

Strengths and limits

Strengths

  • Tests the mechanism (why), not just the outcome, as Coombs & Holladay (2002) asked for.
  • Clean manipulation: only one sentence differs between conditions.
  • Very reliable scales (α .81 to .98), a national panel, and income that matches the national median.
  • Brings a non-Western culture into Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT) and connects SCCT to linguistics (Grice, and the Theory of Communicative Responsibility).

Limits

  • One culture and one crisis (a product malfunction), which could be read as accidental or preventable.
  • A fictional company has no crisis history or prior reputation, two variables SCCT says matter.
  • Vignettes measure reactions on paper, not real behaviour such as buying or boycotting.
  • With no “apology only” control, it cannot show how much compensation adds and how much scapegoating subtracts.
Do not over-read it: the finding is that apology plus compensation beat apology plus scapegoating, for one crisis, in one culture. It is not evidence that compensation is always the right response.

Test yourself

In Paper 1, what is the difference between crisis responsibility and communicative responsibility?

Crisis responsibility is about who caused the crisis (the attribution in SCCT). Communicative responsibility is about who must make sure the crisis is understood (CRT). A company can carry high communicative responsibility even when crisis responsibility is low.

Why is the result called a “mediated moderation”?

Mediated: apology type affects reputation only through appropriateness (direct effect not significant). Moderated: the link from apology to appropriateness is stronger for people with high symmetry scores (interaction B = −0.19).
