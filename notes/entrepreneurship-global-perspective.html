In plain words

The course studies entrepreneurship that crosses national borders. Session 1 defines international entrepreneurship and explains why it matters: new and small firms, many founded by immigrants, create a large share of jobs, wealth and innovation. Session 2 steps back to the basic actors, the entrepreneur and the small firm, and sets out what makes small firms different from large ones. Session 3 compares countries using the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) and asks why some nations are more entrepreneurial than others, looking at political, economic, cultural and technological influences.

The second half of the course looks at how and where firms go international. Session 4 brings the analysis down to regions and cities, showing how knowledge spills over between nearby firms and how growth corridors link regions across borders. Session 5 presents three theories of how small firms internationalize: the stages (Uppsala) model, network theory and the resource-based theory. Session 7 turns to migration, showing how migrant entrepreneurs draw on two countries at once to build global ventures.

The two outcomes tie this together. CILO 1 asks students to apply the foundational concepts of global entrepreneurship to real cases of immigrant, ethnic, returnee and transnational entrepreneurs in different international settings. CILO 2 asks them to analyze the internationalization strategies of small firms, including entry modes, regional influences, social networks and resource-based factors. Sessions 1 to 3 list CILO 1, Sessions 4 and 5 list both, and Session 7 lists CILO 2.

PART ASESSION 1 · INTRODUCTION TO GLOBAL ENTREPRENEURSHIP AND INNOVATION
Session 1 · CILO 1

What international entrepreneurship is and why it matters: significance, definitions and origins

ProblemEntrepreneurship that crosses borders needs a clear definition before it can be studied or applied to cases.
Big ideaInternational entrepreneurship is the discovery and exploitation of opportunities across national borders, and it grew out of two research streams: entrepreneurship and international business.
How (tools)Evidence of significance from the United States, the United Kingdom and California; definitions by Zahra and George, Oviatt and McDougall, and Shane and Venkataraman.
ResultStudents can explain what the field covers, why it matters to economies, and where its ideas come from.
In plain words

Session 1 opens with evidence that new and small firms, many founded by immigrants, carry real weight in economies. In the United States of America (USA), small businesses created most new jobs in 2018. In the United Kingdom (UK), migrant-founded enterprises employ more than a million people. Innovative start-ups such as Uber and eBay were founded by immigrant entrepreneurs. Firms founded by Chinese Americans and Taiwanese Americans in Southern California employ hundreds of thousands of people and also helped develop China's information and communication technology industries. Immigrants who return home to start a business bring innovation, competition and an entrepreneurial culture to their home countries. Venture-backed companies make up a large share of public companies in the USA, including five of the six largest by market value at the end of 2018.

With that evidence in place, the session defines the field. The definitions differ in emphasis. Zahra and George (2002) stress creatively discovering and exploiting opportunities outside a firm's domestic market to gain competitive advantage. Oviatt and McDougall (2005) describe the discovery, enactment, evaluation and exploitation of opportunities across national borders to create future goods and services. A third view sees international entrepreneurship as the early and rapid internationalization of new and small firms, captured in Oviatt and McDougall's (1994) idea of the international new venture (INV): a firm that seeks advantage in several countries from the day it starts.

The field did not appear from nowhere. It grew where two separate but related research streams meet. Entrepreneurship studies new ventures: how, by whom and with what consequences opportunities are recognized, formed, evaluated and exploited (Shane and Venkataraman, 2000). Innovation sits inside this stream as the sum of creativity and commercialization (Drucker, 1993). International business studies organizations that cross national borders to do business in foreign countries. Put the two together and the result is the study of opportunities pursued across borders.

Significance of international entrepreneurship and innovation

Jobs and migrant founders

  • USA, 2018: small businesses created 55% of all jobs and 66% of all net new jobs (U.S. Small Business Administration, USBA, 2018).
  • UK: migrant-founded enterprises employ 1.16 million people and contribute to 14% of job creation in small and medium-sized firms (Johnson and Kimmelman, 2014; Quan et al., 2019).
  • Immigrant-founded start-ups: innovative start-ups such as Uber and eBay were founded by immigrant entrepreneurs; such firms account for as many as 50% of gross jobs created annually between 1980 and 2010 (National Venture Capital Association, NVCA, 2019).
  • Southern California: firms founded by Chinese Americans and Taiwanese Americans employ more than 440,000 people and generate $70 billion in taxable revenues. They have expanded business opportunities and contributed to the development of China's information and communication technology industries (Zhou and Hsu, 2011; Saxenian, 2002).
  • Returnees: immigrant entrepreneurs who permanently return home to start up contribute to innovation, competition and the development of an entrepreneurial culture in their home country (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, OECD, 2017; Tynaliev and McLean, 2011).

Venture-backed companies

  • Initial public offerings: 42% of U.S. companies that went public between 1974 and 2015 were venture-backed (NVCA, 2019).
  • Weight: these companies represented 63% of market capitalization and 85% of the total research and development (R&D) of all public companies.
  • End of 2018: venture-backed Microsoft ($780b), Apple ($746b), Amazon ($737b), Alphabet ($727b) and Facebook ($374b) were five of the six largest publicly traded companies by market capitalization.

  • Innovation: firms' endeavours to use unexploited opportunities by developing new products and business models, improving processes, or generating novelty by creating new combinations from existing components. The scope of international entrepreneurship: concept, drivers and the book's structure

ProblemIt is easy to assume international entrepreneurship only concerns small start-ups exporting abroad.
Big ideaThe field includes cross-national comparisons and entrepreneurial activity in large firms too, and it is pushed forward by five drivers.
How (tools)The two kinds of cross-national comparison, the five drivers, and the scope, key topics and structure of the textbook.
ResultStudents can say what the field covers and why it has grown.
In plain words

International entrepreneurship covers more than one kind of study. Some research compares domestic entrepreneurship across countries, for example how rates of entrepreneurial activity differ from one country to another. Other research compares entrepreneurial internationalization, for example how the drivers that push new ventures abroad differ between countries. Session 3 of this course does the first kind of comparison in detail.

The field is also not limited to new and small ventures. Large, well-established companies carry out entrepreneurial activities internationally too, and researchers have studied knowledge-intensive firms and family businesses as settings for international entrepreneurship. These range from mini-nationals to multinationals across many industries.

Five drivers explain why international entrepreneurship has grown: political and economic developments, advances in information, communication and transportation technologies, the changing nature of competition, migration and cross-border movement of people, and the spread of equity financing outside developed markets. The textbook the course follows (Pruthi and Mitra, 2023) is built around these themes. Its aim is to examine the international development of entrepreneurship and innovation in for-profit, new and small firms, using theory and case studies from many countries.

The concept: what the field includes

  • Cross-national comparisons of domestic entrepreneurship: for example, differences in entrepreneurial activity rates across countries.
  • Cross-national comparisons of entrepreneurial internationalization: for example, cross-country differences in the drivers of new venture internationalization.
  • Not only new and small ventures: international entrepreneurial activities also occur in large, well-established companies pursuing entrepreneurial activities.
  • Research contexts: knowledge-intensive firms and family businesses, covering both mini-national and multinational companies across a range of industry sectors.

The five drivers of international entrepreneurship

PART BSESSION 2 · ENTREPRENEURSHIP AND SMALL FIRMS
Session 2 · CILO 1

Who entrepreneurs are: meaning, creative destruction, types and theories

Problem"Entrepreneur" is used loosely, so it needs a clear meaning and a way to tell different entrepreneurs apart.
Big ideaEntrepreneurs start new ventures and bring innovations to market, and in doing so they destroy old markets and create new ones.
How (tools)Schumpeter's creative destruction, the types of entrepreneur, four entrepreneurial contexts and three theories of why people become entrepreneurs.
ResultStudents can classify an entrepreneur in a case and explain why that person started a business.
In plain words

The term entrepreneurship was first used in an economic sense, for a class of economic actors who depended financially on others: they were either set up with capital to run their enterprise or were undertakers of their own labour without capital. Today entrepreneurs are understood as individuals who start new business ventures. Unlike employees, who earn a living working for someone else's business, entrepreneurs start their own.

Schumpeter's view links entrepreneurs to innovation. An invention is a new concept or idea that comes out of creativity. It becomes an innovation when it is used to create a new commercial product or service, and innovation is a key force in creating new demand and so new wealth. Entrepreneurs are the owner-managers who start businesses to exploit inventions, creating wealth for themselves and jobs for others. Because their innovations create new demand, they destroy existing markets and create new ones. This is creative destruction.

Entrepreneurs differ. Some work alone and others found ventures in teams; some are starting for the first time while others have owned several firms; some build from the ground up while others buy, franchise, license or inherit. They also work in different contexts, inside large companies, in high-technology industries, for social missions or for the environment. Three theories offer reasons why people become entrepreneurs: their personality, their dissatisfaction with a job, or their ability to see gaps others miss.

Invention, innovation and entrepreneurs

  • Invention: the origination of a new concept or idea as a result of the process of creativity.
  • Innovation: use of an invention to create a new commercial product or service; a key force in creating new demand and thus new wealth.
  • Entrepreneurs: owner-managers who start new businesses to exploit inventions; in the process they create wealth for themselves and employment for others.

Schumpeter's creative destruction

  1. Bring innovationsentrepreneurs bring innovations to the market.
  2. Create demandinnovations create new demand.
  3. Destroy and createthis destroys existing markets and creates new ones.

  • Corporate entrepreneurship: formal or informal activities aimed at creating new products or businesses, new business models or international organization structures in established companies.
  • Technology entrepreneurship: entrepreneurship in technologically advanced industries.
  • Social entrepreneurship: solving a social problem for the wider good. Social enterprises serve a social mission but with a profit motive.
  • Green entrepreneurship: enterprise activities that avoid harm to the environment or help to protect it in some way.

Session 2 · CILO 1

What makes a firm small: definitions, quantitative and qualitative features, advantages and disadvantages

Problem"Small" means different things in different countries and industries, and small firms behave differently from large ones.
Big ideaSmall firms are defined by quantitative features (few employees, small capital, small share of their industry) and qualitative features (informal culture, the family, owners who also manage).
How (tools)Definitions of small business and SMEs, size bands, and lists of the advantages and disadvantages of small size.
ResultStudents can classify a firm by size and explain what its size makes easy and hard.
In plain words

Entrepreneurship and small business overlap but are not the same. Entrepreneurship focuses on the context, such as the start-up process, where innovative effort is needed. Small business focuses on established small firms: a corner grocery or bakery, a neighbourhood restaurant, a laundromat, a dry-cleaner or a lawn-mowing company. The session gives one definition of a small business as having fewer than 500 employees and selling less than $5 million of products and services a year, and it shows how numerous such firms are in the United States of America (USA).

Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are defined by thresholds on revenues, assets or employees, and the thresholds vary. The European Union (EU) and the USA draw the lines differently, and in the USA the line also changes by industry. These are the quantitative features of small firms. The qualitative features matter just as much. In a large firm, ownership is separated from control and owners hand responsibility to professional managers. In a small firm, the owner-founder is also the manager and runs the various functions personally. Small firms also have an informal culture, and the family often plays a role.

These features cut both ways. Being small brings motivation and dynamism, an informal structure and closeness to customers. But it also means no economies of scale or scope, limited money and people, decisions concentrated in the owner, and little control over the market, with publicity left to word of mouth and no concrete sales and marketing strategy.

Small business

  • Definition: a small business has fewer than 500 employees and sells less than $5 million worth of products and services annually.
  • Contrast with entrepreneurship: entrepreneurship focuses on the context (for example, the start-up process) where innovative effort is required; "small business" focuses on established small firms.
  • Examples: a corner grocery or bakery shop, a neighbourhood restaurant, a laundromat, a dry-cleaning shop or a lawn-mowing company.

Small businesses in the USA

  • Growth: the number of small businesses in the USA has increased 49% since 1982.
  • Space, 2014: the small business sector occupied 30–50% of all commercial space, an estimated 20–34 billion square feet.
  • Sales, 2014: 23 million small businesses accounted for 54% of all U.S. sales.
  • Proprietors, 2016: the number of proprietors increased by 2.3% over the previous year.
  • U.S. Small Business Administration (USBA), 2018: reports 30.2 million small businesses, which make up 99.9% of all U.S. firms, 99.7% of U.S. firms with paid employees, 47.5% of private sector employees and 40.8% of private sector payroll.

Quantitative features of small firms

Quantitative features refer to the small number of employees and the small initial capital requirements. SMEs are businesses whose revenues, assets or number of employees stay below a certain threshold.

  • EU: an SME is a company with fewer than 50 employees, and a medium-sized enterprise is one with fewer than 250 employees.
  • USA, manufacturing: SMEs are firms with 500 or fewer employees.
  • USA, copper and nickel ore mining: SMEs can have up to 1,500 employees.

  • Informal culture and the role of the family: the defining qualitative features.

Advantages and disadvantages of small size

Advantages

  • Motivation: entrepreneurial motivation and dynamism
  • Structure: informal structure and organization
  • Market proximity: closeness to the market and control over customers' purchasing behaviour based on informal relationships

Disadvantages

  • Finance: financial resource constraints
  • People: human resource constraints: centralization and lack of delegation; owner control over decisions (personal procurement of supplies, personal monitoring of quality); small-batch production
  • Market: lack of market control: word-of-mouth publicity and informal customer feedback; no concrete sales and marketing strategy

In plain words

Session 2 groups the significance of entrepreneurship and small firms under three headings: economic impact (wealth creation and job creation), societal impact (product and service innovations), and impact on large firms. The economic case starts with wealth. Small firms can grow into the large firms that dominate world business. The self-employed are fewer than 20% of workers, yet almost two-thirds of millionaires are self-employed, and close to half of those millionaires are entrepreneurs, such as the founders of Google, Microsoft, Facebook and Virgin. This growth and wealth potential is strongest in high-technology ventures, which often become the foundation of global businesses.

The second economic contribution is jobs. Data from the United States of America (USA) show new entrepreneurial firms adding millions of jobs, small businesses accounting for most net new jobs over long periods, and microenterprises and franchises employing large numbers of people. The slides' chart of employment by business size (numbered 2.1 in the textbook), from the U.S. Small Business Administration (USBA) Office of Advocacy, shows U.S. employment from 2000 to 2015 split into four firm-size bands: fewer than 20, 20 to 99, 100 to 499, and more than 500 employees. The three bands below 500 employees together hold roughly half of total employment across the period.

Society gains because entrepreneurs introduce innovations that help industries emerge and grow. Large firms gain because small firms are a source of innovation for them: large firms buy small ones to enter new markets. Facebook bought WhatsApp to enter the mobile market, Microsoft bought Skype to enter internet communications, and Intel of the USA acquired Mobileye of Israel.

Economic impact: wealth creation

Session 3 · CILO 1

Measuring entrepreneurship across countries: the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor and its activity index

ProblemCountries seem to differ in how entrepreneurial they are, but without a common measure the differences cannot be compared or explained.
Big ideaThe Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) measures entrepreneurial activity the same way in many countries, using the Total Entrepreneurial Activity (TEA) index.
How (tools)GEM's objectives and methodology, the TEA index and its two parts, and the GEM 2018 findings on type, sector, gender, motivation and economic development.
ResultStudents can explain how entrepreneurship is measured internationally and what GEM 2018 found.
In plain words

The Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) set out to answer three questions: does entrepreneurial activity vary between countries and by how much, does it affect a country's economic growth and prosperity, and what makes a country entrepreneurial. Its revised objectives turn these into tasks: measure the differences, uncover the factors that determine national levels of activity, and identify policies that could raise them. GEM is long-term, longitudinal research. It began by comparing the Group of Seven (G7) countries (the United States of America (USA), the United Kingdom (UK), Canada, Japan, France, Germany and Italy) with Denmark, Finland and Israel, using phone surveys, and it uses face-to-face interviews in developing countries such as China, India and Uganda. It defines entrepreneurship broadly, as any attempt to create a new business or expand an existing one, by an individual, a team or an established business.

The central measure is the Total Entrepreneurial Activity (TEA) index: the percentage of the adult population who have taken some action towards creating a new business in the past year, or who own and manage an active business less than 42 months old. It combines two rates. The nascent start-up rate counts those still setting up a business; the new business ownership rate counts those actually owning and running an early-stage business. A chart in the slides (numbered 3.2 in the textbook; Bosma and Kelley, 2018) shows TEA rates for 49 economies in four regions, split into a necessity share and an improvement-driven opportunity share; the rates run from a few per cent in Japan and much of Europe to around 40% in Angola.

GEM 2018 found that activity varies by type of business, industry sector, gender and motivation. The motivation finding is the one most used in argument. People start a business either to exploit an opportunity (opportunity entrepreneurs) or because other options for work are absent or unsatisfactory (necessity entrepreneurs). The share of opportunity entrepreneurs rises with national income, from 37% in low-income economies to 42% in middle-income and 51% in high-income economies. GEM also found no causal relationship between the level of entrepreneurial activity and a country's income or growth, but a U-shaped relationship with economic development: activity is relatively high in low- and middle-income countries, falls as income rises, and in high-income countries opportunity entrepreneurship begins to increase again while necessity entrepreneurship stays low.

GEM objectives

Original questions

  1. Does the level of entrepreneurial activity vary amongst countries and, if so, to what extent?
  2. Does the level of entrepreneurial activity affect a country's rate of economic growth and prosperity?
  3. What makes a country entrepreneurial?

Revised objectives

  1. To measure differences in the level of entrepreneurial activity among countries.
  2. To uncover factors determining national levels of entrepreneurial activity.

In plain words

Session 3 names four external influences on the international development of entrepreneurship: political, economic, sociocultural and technological factors (the PEST factors, as Session 5 calls this environment). This chapter covers the first two; the next covers culture, education and technology.

The political environment works in two ways. The first is the type of political system: a free market or central planning and communism. The second is government intervention, which can either set the conditions for enterprise or support the entrepreneurial process directly. Setting the conditions means tax reforms and regulations, such as the time it takes to set up and register a new business, low interest rates, and listing requirements for new and small firms. Direct measures mean reducing costs or providing finance, providing specialist advice, information, training and development, and investing in research and development (R&D).

The economic environment depends on the level of economic development and on the state of the economy. In developed economies, entrepreneurs tend to be pulled into business and to aim for continuity and long-term ownership. In developing economies they are more often pushed into business, business birth rates are higher (with risk avoidance and selling out), and social entrepreneurship appears. The state of the economy matters too. Total Entrepreneurial Activity (TEA) rates in the United States of America (USA) fell after the financial crisis of 2009/2010, yet high TEA rates in the late 2000s coincided with high unemployment. In China, entrepreneurial activity has fallen over time, probably because sustained growth let many companies grow larger. The availability of jobs gives people a fall-back option if their venture fails, and favourable internal market dynamics with free and open markets matter for entrepreneurs, especially those selling at home.

In plain words

The cultural environment for entrepreneurship has two layers: societal culture and national culture. Societal culture, as GEM measures it, covers three things. Societal attitudes are whether people think successful entrepreneurs have high status, whether they see starting a business as a good career choice, and how much media attention entrepreneurship receives. Self-perceptions are whether people think it is easy to start a business where they live. Affiliations are people's links to entrepreneurs in their social networks, which provide role models and a range of experienced, eager stakeholders such as investors, board members, partners and mentors.

These measures show both consistency and wide differences between regions. In plain words

Session 3 names four key issues in comparing entrepreneurship across countries. The first is ambiguity in the impact of specific factors. Economically successful nations score very differently on each of Hofstede's dimensions, which suggests there are no easily defined "right" or "wrong" conditions for entrepreneurship. Much also depends on how the outcome is defined: in Korea and Japan, for example, large-scale organizations are the focus. The second issue is interdependence. Political and economic influences are not independent of each other; less developed countries usually have unstable and unpredictable environments, which shortens business life; and political, economic and cultural factors are interrelated. Entrepreneurs in developed European countries, for instance, are the most individualistic and display self-confidence in their entrepreneurial ability.

The third issue is that entrepreneurship takes different forms and pursues different goals. In collectivist Asian countries, enterprise often shows itself through large organizations, and the presence of large firms itself creates entrepreneurial opportunities; social ventures must also be counted. The business systems of Asia differ in purpose: the Japanese kaisha serves employment, the Korean chaebol serves national development goals, and Chinese guanxi serves the creation and preservation of family fortunes through networking. The fourth issue is regional variation: societal attitudes vary more between economies within a region than across regions, Taiwan has low start-up rates but high established-business and employee-entrepreneurship rates, and some regions form clusters of entrepreneurship such as Silicon Valley. Assessing culture is itself hard, because culture has different levels, society has different layers, and entrepreneurship happens in different units.

To compare the environment rather than the activity, GEM introduces the National Entrepreneurship Context Index (NECI). It is a composite index built from twelve framework conditions, each weighted by the importance experts place on it. The results show where a country's ratings on the conditions fall short, especially compared with the importance placed on them. Where entrepreneurship happens: regions and knowledge spillovers

ProblemCountries are too large a unit to explain why some places produce far more innovation and new firms than others.
Big ideaRegions are the level at which entrepreneurship develops, and knowledge that leaks between nearby organizations, called knowledge spillovers, explains much of how regions evolve.
How (tools)The rationale for a regional view, the concept of regions, and the knowledge spillover concept with its four channels and enabling factors.
ResultStudents can explain why location matters for international entrepreneurship and how spillovers raise a region's innovation.
In plain words

Session 4 starts from a practical observation. Businesses trade, form alliances, network, invest overseas and set up offshore operations based on what they know about attractive regions elsewhere. Supply and value chains are increasingly international, and products are made and sold worldwide at a faster pace. The session adds that the more global a region is in economic terms, the more likely it is also a powerful local economy. Knowing different environments is therefore central to understanding how firms in different parts of the world engage in international entrepreneurship. The chapter in the textbook runs in four parts: the concept of regions, knowledge spillovers, the human, social and entrepreneurial capital of regions, and growth corridors.

The term "region" is linked to the governance of policies and institutions that promote economic development, social cohesion and environmental welfare. Its meaning varies by country but usually refers to a sub-national administrative unit, though it can also cover several countries, as in the European Region or Community. A region's identity rests on the beliefs, values and cultural perceptions of its people. Interest in regional innovation systems and regional competitiveness grew before and with globalization. Watching business formation in regions helps entrepreneurs judge whether they can work with dynamic, innovative firms elsewhere, and helps policy makers recalibrate local strategies. Regions differ significantly, both between and within countries.

Knowledge spillovers (KSs) are knowledge externalities bounded in space. Knowledge created in one organization is "leaky", so rivals nearby can take some of it without creating it themselves. Firms close to sources of knowledge, intelligence and data can therefore innovate faster than rivals located elsewhere. The spillovers flow through employees changing jobs, knowledge exchange with universities, informal collaboration among firms, and the sharing of know-how along value chains. How much knowledge a region creates and spills over distinguishes its level of innovation and new firm creation.

Rationale for a regional view

  • Regional information: businesses trade, make strategic alliances, network, invest overseas and set up offshore operations based on information they collect about alternative, attractive regions.
  • Global chains: there is increasing dependency on international supply and value chains, and an accelerating pace at which products are made and sold worldwide.
  • Global and local: the more global a region is in economic terms, the greater the likelihood that it is also a powerful local economy.
  • Knowledge of environments: central to understanding how firms in different parts of the world engage in international entrepreneurship and business.

The concept of regions

  • Governance: "regions" is associated with governance of policies of institutions facilitating economic development, social cohesion and environmental welfare, as part of the making and distribution of public and private goods.
  • Level: the term varies by country but tends to focus on the sub-national administrative unit.
  • Supranational regions: regions can encompass countries, as in the European Region or Community (an economic and quasi-political union).
  • Identity: a region's identity is based on arrangements and reconfigurations of beliefs, values and cultural perceptions of groups of people.

  • Use to policy makers: they need to recalibrate local strategies for attracting new firms and supporting new innovative firms.
  • Variation: there are significant variations between and within countries' regions.

Knowledge spillovers (KSs)

  • Definition: knowledge externalities bounded in space.
  • Proximity advantage: firms near key sources of knowledge, intelligence and data introduce innovations faster than rivals located elsewhere.
  • Leaky knowledge: knowledge created in one organization can leak, letting rivals appropriate some of it without having to create it.
  • Regional differences: levels of innovation and new firm creation in a region can be distinguished by the extent to which knowledge is created and spilled over.
  • Enabling factors: the KS effect is enabled by physical, institutional, social and socio-psychological factors, which help establish framework conditions for entrepreneurship and wealth creation in a region.

  1. Job mobilityemployees changing jobs.
  2. Universitiesknowledge exchange with universities.
  3. Informal collaborationinformal collaborations among firms.
  4. Value chainsthe necessity of sharing know-how across value chains.

What makes a region competitive: regional capital and growth corridors

ProblemRegions with similar resources can differ widely in competitiveness, and local regions need ways to connect beyond their borders.
Big ideaA region's mix of human, social, technological and entrepreneurial capital measures its competitiveness, and growth corridors link regions within and across countries.

In plain words

Part Three of Session 4 asks what makes a region competitive. A place's ability to attract talent and tolerate the diversity of talented people combines with its capacity for invention and new technology. Some authors argue that human capital is the key to economic growth, especially in cities. Others say social capital matters more, because it draws on people beyond the individual entrepreneur: their networks and relationships. Cultural diversity in urban networks brings in different kinds of human capital, which promotes innovation, speeds up information flows and fosters new ideas, leading to more new firms. Institutions, good governance and the generation and attraction of talent give countries and regions a local edge over their neighbours. This amalgam of institutions, talent and technology can be called creative entrepreneurial capital.

Entrepreneurial capital is a composite form of capital, defined at the economic level (region and industry), the organizational level, and the personal level (team and individual). A good regional development strategy raises a region's competitive capacity by drawing on place and space and on its mix of human, social, technological and entrepreneurial capital. The combination and strength of these assets is a good way of measuring regional competitiveness.

Part Four explains how regions connect with regions in neighbouring countries. Corridors are bundles of infrastructure, such as rail links for passengers or freight and highways, that connect two or more urban areas in one country or across adjacent countries, enhanced by ICT (information and communication technology), power lines and pipes. Growth corridors are instruments for boosting economic development in developing economies, an idea drawn from the World Economic Forum and the World Bank's World Development Reports. The first was the Maputo Development Corridor (MDC), set up in 1996, whose success led the New Partnership for Africa's Development (NEPAD) to replicate the model across Africa. A corridor develops in stages: hard infrastructure creates a transport corridor; rising traffic calls for soft infrastructure; and the efficiency that follows attracts investment, creating an economic corridor. The session adds that interest in global value chains (GVCs) has since overtaken interest in regional economic corridors.

PART ESESSION 5 · THEORIES OF SMALL FIRM INTERNATIONALIZATION
Session 5 · CILO 1 and 2

Going international: what internationalization means, how often small firms do it and why

ProblemSmall firms contribute heavily to exports, yet relatively few of them go international.
Big ideaInternationalization is a process of increasing international involvement along a continuum, and it offers small firms clear benefits.
How (tools)Luostarinen's continuum, the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor's 25% threshold, U.S., Japanese and Korean evidence, four benefits and three theories.
ResultStudents can define internationalization, describe its incidence among small firms and list why entrepreneurs pursue it.
In plain words

Internationalization is the process of increasing involvement in international operations. Luostarinen (1994) describes it as a continuum that runs from import activity, or domestic internationalization, through extra-regional expansion, to full globalization. The Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM, 2018) gives a working threshold: entrepreneurs count as international when 25% or more of their sales come from customers outside their economy. In general, internationalization of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) is greater in small, open economies and lower in large, more self-contained ones, although this is not always the case.

The evidence on incidence is mixed. In the United States of America (USA) in 2015, more than 95% of firms that exported services had fewer than 250 employees, and they accounted for almost half the value of U.S. services exports; firms with fewer than 10 employees accounted for almost a quarter. Yet small firms are underrepresented in international markets overall: only a very small share of U.S. services firms export, and SMEs investing abroad are only 0.1% of SMEs in Japan and 0.03% in Korea.

Internationalization matters because it lets entrepreneurs reach foreign markets and raise sales and profits, offset declining domestic sales and spread business risk, improve product quality, and lower production costs through economies of scale. Session 5 explains how small firms internationalize through three theories: the stages (Uppsala) model, network theory and the resource-based theory, each covered in the following chapters.

  • Internationalization: the process of increasing international involvement in international operations; a continuum from import activity or domestic internationalization (through extra-regional expansion) to full globalization (Luostarinen, 1994).
  • International entrepreneur (GEM, 2018): an entrepreneur with 25% or more of sales from customers outside their economy.
  • Economy size: SME internationalization is generally greater in small, open economies and less in large, more self-contained economies, though not always.

Step by step abroad: the stages (Uppsala) model and its critique

ProblemGoing abroad is risky and uncertain for a small firm that knows little about foreign markets.
Big ideaThe stages (Uppsala) model says small firms internationalize incrementally, in sequence, to cut risk and build market knowledge.
How (tools)Pre-export behaviour, psychic distance for market selection, the four-step establishment chain, and a critique.
ResultStudents can describe a firm's entry path with the model and say where the model fails.
In plain words

The stages model, also called the Uppsala (U) model, sees small firm internationalization as an incremental, sequential, staged process. Firms move in small steps to minimise risk and uncertainty and to gain market knowledge and experience as they go. Before exporting, a firm's behaviour comes from the interplay between the individual decision maker and the firm's environment and history, including its experience and extra-regional expansion; this is the innovation (I) model of pre-export behaviour.

The model explains both which markets firms enter and how they enter them. On market selection, firms first enter "psychically close" countries. Psychic distance is how close a market is in language, culture, political system and business factors such as industry structure and competition. Firms start where the market is easiest to understand and uncertainty feels low, then move to markets of greater psychic distance. On entry mode, firms climb an "establishment chain" within each market: from no regular export, to exporting through agents or intermediaries, to sales subsidiaries, and finally to production or manufacturing abroad.

The model has critics. The "psychic distance paradox" is that operations in culturally close countries are not always easy to manage. Experience in another foreign market, technology and global communications, and social networks and client followership can all change the path. Small firms are also heterogeneous: some are traditional exporters that do internationalize step by step, some are late starters that leapfrog stages, and some are born globals. Service sector firms and the aspirations of entrepreneurs also do not fit the model neatly.

Core idea

Small firm internationalization is an incremental, sequential, staged process intended to:

  • Minimise risk and uncertainty.
  • Gain market knowledge and experience.

Pre-export behaviour: the innovation (I) model

Pre-export behaviour is an interplay between the individual decision maker and the firm's environment and history, including experience and extra-regional expansion.

Foreign market selection: psychic distance

  • Psychic distance: the extent of proximity in language, culture, political systems and business factors such as industry structure and competitive environment.
  • Sequence: firms start with markets that are most easily understood and where perceived uncertainty is low, venturing later into markets of successively greater psychic distance.

Who you know: the network theory of internationalization

ProblemSome small firms internationalize far faster than the stages model predicts.
Big ideaEntrepreneurs' social networks shape their ability to create and grow firms, and the structure and content of those networks affect how fast they go international.
How (tools)Definition of social networks, five dimensions of network structure, strong and weak ties, personal, professional and intermediary ties, network content, and three aspects that speed internationalization.
ResultStudents can analyze a founder's network and explain how it helps or limits internationalization.
In plain words

Social networks are the pattern of relationships, or the actual set of links, between individuals, groups and organizations, and they are greater than the sum of their parts (Brass, 1992). Social network theory holds that who entrepreneurs know influences their ability to create, develop and grow their firms (Aldrich and Zimmer, 1986; Dubini and Aldrich, 1991). Social interaction builds mutual knowledge and trust between actors in international markets, and that trust makes internationalization easier.

A network can be described by its structure and its content. Structure is the pattern of relationships: the network's size, diversity, density, stability and strength. Strength separates strong ties, with people close to you, marked by trust, strong emotional bonds and frequent contact (family and friends), from weak ties, which are new, often in a different context, infrequent and without a close bond (a father's friend, a friend of a friend, a customer or supplier one is introduced to). Ties can also be grouped as personal or friendship ties, industry or professional ties, and intermediary ties formed at forums, conferences and events. Content is the social capital the network supplies: finance, technical knowledge, information and advice, emotional support, and reputation or signalling.

Three aspects of networks influence how fast small firms internationalize. First, strength of ties: strong ties are not the most important type, because there are few of them and they take a lot of investment, while cross-border weak ties positively and significantly speed up internationalization. Second, size: larger networks go with faster internationalization and a quicker increase in the number of countries served. Third, density: dense networks provide relatively efficient support for internationalization.

Definitions

  • Social networks: the pattern of relationships, or actual set of links, between individuals, groups and organizations, which is greater than the sum of its parts (Brass, 1992).
  • Social network theory: entrepreneurs' social networks (who they know) influence their ability to create, develop and grow their firms (Aldrich and Zimmer, 1986; Dubini and Aldrich, 1991); social interactions build mutual knowledge and trust between international market actors that facilitate internationalization.

What the firm has: the resource-based theory of internationalization

ProblemFirms facing the same markets make different internationalization decisions.
Big ideaThe resource-based theory (RBT) says firms are collections of resources, and internationalization decisions are made within a framework of resources, capabilities and environmental realities.
How (tools)Resources and dynamic capabilities; internal factors, characteristics of the decision maker, competitive and institutional environments, nature of the product, and firm history.
ResultStudents can explain a firm's internationalization by the resources it holds and the environment it faces.
In plain words

The resource-based theory (RBT) sees a firm as a collection of productive resources. Because firms hold different resources, each has unique, idiosyncratic characteristics, and these contribute to competitive advantage. Resources include financial and human resources. Dynamic capabilities are the firm's ability to combine and recombine resources and use its resource base in ways that give it a competitive advantage over a long period. According to RBT, the decision to internationalize is made within a coordinated framework of resources and capabilities on one side and environmental realities on the other.

Session 5 groups the factors into internal factors, external factors, the nature of the product or service, and the history of the small firm. Internal factors are financial (tangible) resources such as finance, plant and machinery, and human (intangible) resources, chiefly the characteristics of the decision maker: age, education, international experience and orientation, prior work or start-up experience, and international networks such as customers abroad. The slides note, though, that there is no clear evidence that a decision maker's age, education or personality traits influence internationalization.

External factors are the competitive and PEST (political, economic, sociocultural and technological) environment at home and abroad. At home, competitive pressure, declining sales and market saturation push firms out, as with Italy's Parmigiano-Reggiano makers; abroad, the profit motive and unique market opportunities pull them in. Governments at home can support firms, as federal grants did for small businesses in Las Vegas, Nevada, and foreign governments can offer support too. Finally, the nature of the product matters: high-technology firms are often small and international, firms in industries with short product life cycles are more likely to internationalize, and research and development (R&D) intensity and product attributes explain small firm internationalization better than size does.

Core of the resource-based theory (RBT)

  • Firms as resource collections: firms are collections of productive resources; heterogeneity of resources gives them unique, idiosyncratic characteristics that contribute to competitive advantage.
  • Resources: include financial and human resources.
  • Dynamic capabilities: firms' ability to combine and re-combine resources and use their resource base in ways that confer a competitive advantage over a long period of time.
  • Internationalization decisions: made within a coordinated framework of resources and capabilities, and environmental realities.

Migrants as entrepreneurs: migration flows, trajectories and dual habitus

ProblemMigrant entrepreneurs have often been seen as running small, ethnic businesses in crowded, low-skill industries.
Big ideaInternational migration has nurtured entrepreneurship, and migrants' "dual habitus" in two countries now lets them build ventures in technology and innovative sectors, locally and globally.
How (tools)The policy rationale, migration flow data, comparisons with native-born entrepreneurs, and the concept of dual habitus.
ResultStudents can explain how migration fosters entrepreneurship and analyze a migrant's trajectory.
In plain words

Migration and its link to development have risen steadily on the international agenda since the International Conference on Population and Development in 1994, and the United Nations (UN) 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development (UN, 2016) includes migration-related targets with regular progress reviews. The economic reasoning is that migrants transfer knowledge and skills from their countries of origin and gain new capabilities in their country of residence. This helps the labour market and the economy of the country of residence, and using those skills can mobilize resources and strengthen motivation to set up new enterprises in both the destination country and the country of origin. The textbook chapter runs in four parts: migration flows, how migrants develop entrepreneurial activities, short case studies, and challenges.

International migration has grown rapidly, and over 60% of international migrants live in Asia or Europe. Where migrants settle is shaped by geography and history, and more recently by awareness of their technical and entrepreneurial skills. Developed economies such as the United States of America (USA), the United Kingdom (UK) and Germany have growing numbers of successful start-ups and growing firms run by migrant entrepreneurs. Migrant entrepreneurs can have a substantial impact on their countries of origin through remittances, skills transfer, social remittances and social capital.

In most countries of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), immigrants set up businesses at the same rate as the native-born, and in some they are about 1.5 times as likely to be self-employed. Migrants start businesses out of both necessity and opportunity. Historically their businesses sat in highly competitive industries with low entry barriers and low skill requirements, serving minority communities; now migrant entrepreneurship has moved into technology sectors and innovative areas, locally and globally. What makes this possible is the migrant's duality of contextual knowledge, empathy and access to hard and soft resources, which stems from their "dual habitus" in the country of destination and the country of origin. Global engagement lets migrants break out of stereotypical minority business enclaves and grow, and they do not simply accept institutional constraints but actively mould them to suit their own initiatives.

Rationale

  • International agenda: since the International Conference on Population and Development in 1994, migration and development have risen steadily on the agenda of the international community.
  • 2030 Agenda: the UN 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development (UN, 2016) includes several migration-related targets and calls for regular reviews of progress.
  • Skills transfer: knowledge and skills from countries of origin, plus new capabilities acquired in the country of residence, contribute to the latter's labour market and economy.
  • Enterprise: using those skills can mobilize resources and raise personal motivation to set up enterprises in both destination country and country of origin.

Structure of the chapter

  2. Part Twoevidence on how migrants develop entrepreneurial activities.
  3. Part Threeshort case studies of global migrant entrepreneurs.
  4. Part Fourchallenges faced by migrant entrepreneurs, policy makers and researchers.

Part One: migration flows and global entrepreneurship

  • Growth: international migration shows rapid and continued growth; over 60% of all international migrants live in Asia or Europe.
  • Settlement: shaped by geography and historical patterns, plus recent awareness of migrants' technical and entrepreneurial skills.
  • Developed economies: the USA, the UK and Germany have increasing numbers of successful start-ups and growing firms run by international migrant entrepreneurs.
  • Impact on origin countries: through remittances, skills transfer, social remittances and social capital, which can be substantial.

Part Two: migrant communities and entrepreneurial trajectories

  • Rates (OECD): in most OECD countries immigrants set up businesses at the same rate as the native-born; in some they are about 1.5 times as likely to be self-employed.
  • Motives: migrants take control of their destiny by launching and growing an enterprise, out of both necessity and opportunity.
  • Historical pattern: highly competitive industries with low barriers to entry and low skill requirements, serving minority communities.
  • Current pattern: beyond traditional ethnic businesses into a wide range of technology sectors and innovative areas, locally and globally.

Dual habitus

  • Duality: the migrant experience lies in the duality of contextual knowledge, empathy, and access to hard and soft resources.
  • Source: these stem from migrants' "dual habitus" in the country of destination and the country of origin.
  • Breaking out: global engagement enables migrants to break out of stereotypical minority business enclaves, grow their ventures and gain competitive advantage.
  • Agency: migrant entrepreneurs are not passive adherents to institutional constraints; they actively mould them to suit their own initiatives.

Migrant entrepreneurs in practice: cases, refugees, networks of innovation and challenges

ProblemGeneral claims about migrant entrepreneurs need cases to show how institutions and individual effort interact in different countries.
Big ideaCases from Israel, Korea and Japan, refugee entrepreneurship and global migrant networks show both the contributions of global migrant entrepreneurship (GME) and its constraints.
How (tools)Three cases, observations across them, refugee entrepreneurship, global migrant networks of innovation, and four challenges.
ResultStudents can use case evidence to discuss contributions, barriers and policy implications of migrant entrepreneurship.
In plain words

Session 7 presents three short cases. In Israel, the outcomes for Israeli and Palestinian high-tech entrepreneurs depended on the unequal "capital set" of the migrants and on institutional arrangements. In Korea, the Korean Wave (Hallyu) in film, television and music led to joint film production with China, which drew on the Chinese government's support for cross-border flows of investors, directors and producers and created new firms in technical service sectors. Japanese transnational entrepreneurs start "ex-pat" businesses in their late 30s to early 40s, often with savings from Japan, after exposure to overseas life and culture earlier in their careers. Across the examples, two observations stand out: the interest in going international and using that experience to renew home ties is constant, and the meeting of institutional structures and human agency in the dual habitus explains both the scope and the variation of global entrepreneurship.

Refugee entrepreneurship is treated separately. Welcoming refugees is described as an investment with substantial economic dividends: enterprising refugees start businesses that create wealth, employ locals, make the economy more dynamic and boost international trade and investment. But insecurity, legal rights and institutional issues set refugees apart from other migrants, and "fragility" best describes the economic and social conditions they work in.

Global migrant networks of innovation produce many positive externalities for the regions where migrant entrepreneurs operate. Their specialisms create intermediary functions that serve large multinationals; their networks can ease financial constraints for fast-growing firms; and firms with migrant co-owners and more migrant workers are more likely to be innovative. Migrant business growth also compensates for the falling share of new firms started by native entrepreneurs, and migrants who first enter their home country to found a global venture are more likely to found born-global ventures. GME contributes directly to international business creation and growth, finance and investment, research and development (R&D) and innovation, and international technology transfer. Yet challenges remain: migrants are constrained by home-country endowments and institutions, little is known about their networks, capabilities and locational dynamics across institutional contexts, policy makers and analysts pay them too little attention, and better granular data is needed.

Model answer

International entrepreneurship joins two ideas. Entrepreneurship is the pursuit of opportunities to create future goods and services. International business is the activity of organizations that cross national borders. The field emerged where these two research streams meet (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Two definitions are central. Zahra and George (2002, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023) define international entrepreneurship as the process of creatively discovering and exploiting opportunities that lie outside a firm's domestic market in the pursuit of competitive advantage. Oviatt and McDougall (2005, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023) define it as the discovery, enactment, evaluation and exploitation of opportunities across national borders to create future goods and services. The first definition tells us where the opportunity lies (outside the home market) and why it is pursued (competitive advantage). The second tells us how it happens: it sets out the whole opportunity process and stresses its cross-border character.

Innovation is the engine inside this process. Firms innovate when they use unexploited opportunities by developing new products and business models, improving processes or combining existing components in new ways. Drucker (1993, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023) calls innovation the sum of creativity and commercialization. It is the means by which entrepreneurs create new wealth-producing resources or give existing resources greater wealth-creating potential. Schumpeter adds the consequence: innovations create new demand, which destroys existing markets and creates new ones. This is why international entrepreneurship matters to the global economy: it carries this wealth-creating innovation across borders.

A third view treats the field as the phenomenon of early and rapid internationalization of new and small firms, and two labels describe such firms. International new ventures (INVs), in Oviatt and McDougall's (1994, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023) definition, are business organizations that, from inception, seek significant competitive advantage from the use of resources and the sale of outputs in multiple countries. So the defining feature of an INV is that its resources and sales are international from the start. Born globals are named on the course slides as one type of small firm that the stages model cannot explain, alongside traditional exporters and late starters, because they do not internationalize step by step. Tanev (2012) defines a born-global firm as "a venture launched to exploit a global niche from the first day of its operations" (p. 5). Such firms typically export within a couple of years of founding and may export a quarter or more of their output. [outside the slides] The difference is therefore this. An INV is identified by using resources and selling in several countries from inception. A born global is identified by being built from day one around a global niche, which is usually seen in how quickly and how much it exports. Tanev (2012) treats "international new venture" as one of several labels for early internationalizers, and argues that entering foreign niches early does not on its own make a firm born global. [outside the slides]

The data on immigrant entrepreneurs show how significant this activity is. In the United Kingdom, migrant-founded enterprises employ 1.16 million people and contribute 14% of job creation in small and medium-sized firms (Johnson & Kimmelman, 2014; Quan et al., 2019, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). In the United States, innovative start-ups founded by immigrant entrepreneurs, such as Uber and eBay, account for as many as 50% of gross jobs created annually between 1980 and 2010 (NVCA, 2019, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). In Southern California, firms founded by Chinese Americans and Taiwanese Americans employ more than 440,000 people and generate $70 billion in taxable revenues (Zhou & Hsu, 2011; Saxenian, 2002, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). More broadly, small businesses in the USA created 55% of all jobs and 66% of net new jobs in 2018 (USBA, 2018, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). In some OECD countries, immigrants are about 1.5 times as likely as the native-born to be self-employed (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

Named companies show the impact on global innovation. Uber and eBay, both founded by immigrant entrepreneurs, are innovative start-ups that built new markets. The Chinese and Taiwanese American firms did more than create jobs in California. They also expanded business opportunities and contributed to the development of China's information and communication technology industries, which is innovation flowing back across borders. Immigrants who return home to start up also bring innovation, competition and an entrepreneurial culture to their home countries (OECD, 2017, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Venture-backed companies represented 63% of market capitalization and 85% of R&D among U.S. public companies (NVCA, 2019, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

In conclusion, international entrepreneurship, whether defined through opportunities outside the home market (Zahra & George, 2002) or across national borders (Oviatt & McDougall, 2005), is significant because it spreads innovation and creates jobs and wealth across countries. Immigrant founders, from Uber and eBay to the Chinese and Taiwanese American firms of California, show that migration, one of the five drivers of the field, is a major source of that innovation.

Model answer

Small firms play a central role in a country's economy: they are the vehicles through which entrepreneurs create jobs, wealth and innovation. To analyze that role, first separate two terms. Entrepreneurs are individuals who start new business ventures; unlike employees, they build their own business. Entrepreneurship focuses on the context, such as the start-up process, where innovative effort is required. Small business focuses on established small firms, such as a corner grocery or bakery, a neighbourhood restaurant, a laundromat or a dry-cleaner (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). A small business is defined as having fewer than 500 employees and selling less than $5 million of products and services a year. Every small firm begins with an entrepreneurial act, but not every small business remains entrepreneurial.

Three theories explain why people found these firms. Personality theory holds that individual personality characteristics influence opportunity recognition, so people become entrepreneurs because of their personality: entrepreneurs are "born", not "made". Psycho-sociological theory points out that not all entrepreneurs start for positive, pull-based reasons: "disgruntlement", or dissatisfaction with a job, can be the trigger. Cognitive theories argue that entrepreneurs perceive market gaps or identify opportunities others have not noticed (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Together, the theories show that small firms arise from who people are, from what pushes them out of employment and from what they see in the market. This variety helps explain why small firms are so numerous: USBA (2018, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023) counts 30.2 million small businesses in the USA, 99.9% of all U.S. firms.

Small firms differ from large ones in two ways. Quantitatively, they have few employees, small initial capital, a small share of their industry, low turnover and a small market. Size bands run from the self-employed (one person) to micro-businesses (up to 19 employees), small businesses (20–99) and medium-sized businesses (100–499) (Bridge et al., 2003, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). The European Union instead treats firms under 50 employees as SMEs and under 250 as medium-sized. Qualitatively, small firms have an informal culture, the family often plays a role, and the owner-founder is also the manager, personally responsible for the firm's functions, whereas in large firms ownership is separated from control. These features cut both ways. The advantages are entrepreneurial motivation and dynamism, an informal structure and organization, and closeness to the market, with control over customers' purchasing based on informal relationships. The disadvantages are no economies of scale or scope; financial resource constraints; human resource constraints, such as centralization, lack of delegation, owners personally procuring supplies and monitoring quality, and small-batch production; and lack of market control, with publicity left to word of mouth and no concrete sales and marketing strategy.

Despite these constraints, small firms are often called the backbone of job creation in the United States, and the data support the claim. According to USBA (2018, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023), the 30.2 million small businesses make up 99.7% of U.S. firms with paid employees, employ 47.5% of private sector employees and pay 40.8% of private sector payroll. In 2018, small businesses provided 55% of all jobs and 66% of all net new jobs. In 2015 they employed 58.9 million people and created 1.9 million net jobs, with firms of fewer than 20 employees adding 1.1 million of them. Small businesses accounted for 64% of net new jobs between 1993 and 2011 (Liberto, 2019, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023), and new entrepreneurial firms have added 8 million jobs since 1990 (Bamford & Bruton, 2016, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). More than 25.1 million microenterprises with five or fewer employees provide 22% of private employment, and franchises accounted for 40% of retail sales and some 8 million jobs in 2014.

Schumpeter's concept of creative destruction explains why this job creation also renews the economy. The chain has four links. An invention is a new idea from creativity. It becomes an innovation when it is used in a new commercial product or service. Entrepreneurs, as owner-managers who start businesses to exploit inventions, bring innovations to market, and the innovations create new demand. New demand then destroys existing markets and creates new ones, generating wealth for the entrepreneur and employment for others (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). So small firms are not only employers but agents of change. Their wealth potential is large: the self-employed are fewer than 20% of workers but almost two-thirds of millionaires, and founders such as Sergey Brin and Larry Page (Google) and Bill Gates (Microsoft) grew small firms into global businesses. Small firms also feed innovation into large firms, which buy them to enter new markets, as when Facebook bought WhatsApp to enter the mobile market and Microsoft bought Skype to enter internet communications.

In conclusion, small firms arise from personality, dissatisfaction and opportunity recognition. They are agile but resource-constrained. They provide most U.S. jobs and net new jobs according to USBA (2018). Through Schumpeter's creative destruction, they keep replacing old markets with new ones. That combination of employment and renewal is their role in the economy.

Model answer

The Global Entrepreneurship Monitor (GEM) is long-term, longitudinal research. It sets out to measure differences in entrepreneurial activity among countries, uncover the factors that determine national levels, and identify policies that could raise them. It began by comparing the G7 countries with Denmark, Finland and Israel. It now uses phone surveys and, in developing countries such as China, India and Uganda, face-to-face interviews. GEM defines entrepreneurship broadly as any attempt to create a new business or to expand an existing one, by an individual, a team or an established business (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

GEM's central measure is the Total Entrepreneurial Activity (TEA) index. It is the percentage of the adult population that has taken some action towards creating a new business in the past year, or who are owner-managers of an active business less than 42 months old. It is measured by adding two rates: the nascent start-up rate, covering people in the process of setting up a business, and the new business ownership rate, covering those actually owning and running early-stage businesses. GEM 2018 data for 49 economies show TEA rates ranging from a few per cent in Japan and much of Europe to around 40% in Angola (Bosma & Kelley, 2018, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). GEM 2018 also shows variation by type (18.6% of TEA is in family businesses and 8.0% are solo), by sector, and by gender, with men twice as likely as women to start a business.

TEA shows how many people are starting businesses, but not why, and motivation differs sharply by income level. GEM distinguishes opportunity entrepreneurs, who start a business to exploit an opportunity, from necessity entrepreneurs, who start one because all other work options are absent or unsatisfactory. The share of opportunity entrepreneurs rises from an average of 37% in low-income economies to 42% in middle-income and 51% in high-income economies (GEM, 2018, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). In other words, most entrepreneurs in low-income economies are driven by necessity, while in high-income economies just over half are driven by opportunity. This matches the push and pull pattern of Session 3: developing economies show push motivations, higher business birth rates and social entrepreneurship, while developed economies show pull motivations and a goal of long-term ownership.

These findings produce the U-shaped relationship. GEM finds no causal relationship between the level of entrepreneurial activity and a country's GDP, per capita income or growth. Instead, the relationship with economic development is U-shaped, and motivation explains each arm. In low- and middle-income countries, entrepreneurial activity is relatively high and much of it is necessity-driven, but it falls as income levels rise and jobs become available as a fall-back. In high-income countries necessity entrepreneurship is low, but opportunity entrepreneurship starts to increase as incomes rise further, which lifts activity again (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

Four external influences explain why countries differ. Politically, the type of system matters (free market versus central planning), and so does government intervention. Government either sets the conditions for enterprise, through tax reforms and regulations such as the time to register a business, low interest rates and listing rules for small firms, or supports the process directly through finance, specialist advice and training, and investment in R&D. Economically, the level of development shapes motivation, and the state of the economy matters: U.S. TEA fell after the 2009/2010 financial crisis, while in China activity has diminished as sustained growth let companies grow larger. Technologically, information and communication technology connects entrepreneurs with international customers and lets them coordinate at a distance, so they can sell outside their home market, which is directly relevant to international entrepreneurship.

Socio-culturally, GEM measures societal attitudes (status of entrepreneurs, career choice, media attention), self-perceptions (ease of starting a business) and affiliations with entrepreneurs, which supply role models, investors and mentors. National culture is described by Hofstede's dimensions (Hofstede, 1991, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Power distance, the acceptance of unequal power, is highest in Malaysia and Mexico and lowest in Germany and Austria. Uncertainty avoidance, the sense of threat from ambiguity, is highest in Greece and Japan and lowest in the USA and Singapore; low uncertainty avoidance suits the risk of starting a venture. Individualism, valuing independence of action over group loyalty, is highest in the USA and Canada and lowest in Mexico and Guatemala, and entrepreneurs in developed European countries are described as the most individualistic and self-confident. Masculinity, an orientation to money and things, is highest in Japan and Mexico. Long-term orientation is highest in Hong Kong and Japan. Hofstede's later work adds a sixth dimension, indulgence versus restraint (Hofstede, 2011). [outside the slides] Universities add to the cultural environment by providing knowledge, conditioning mindsets and offering incubation and support networks.

However, Session 3 warns that these influences are ambiguous and interdependent. Economically successful nations score very differently on each Hofstede dimension, so there is no single "right" culture for entrepreneurship. In collectivist Asian countries, enterprise often takes the form of large organizations, such as Japan's kaisha and Korea's chaebol. In conclusion, GEM 2018 shows that how much and why people start businesses depends on a country's income level, and that political, economic, socio-cultural and technological factors together, not any one of them, shape the development of international entrepreneurship.

Model answer

International entrepreneurship is often discussed at the level of countries, but Session 4 argues that it takes root in regions. Businesses trade, form strategic alliances, network, invest overseas and set up offshore operations on the basis of information about attractive regions. Supply and value chains are increasingly international, and the more global a region is economically, the more likely it is also a powerful local economy (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). A region is associated with the governance of policies and institutions that promote economic development, social cohesion and environmental welfare. Its meaning varies by country. It usually refers to a sub-national administrative unit, but it can also cover several countries, as in the European Region or Community. A region's identity rests on the beliefs, values and cultural perceptions of its people. Regions differ significantly both between and within countries, which is why entrepreneurs and policy makers watch business formation at regional level.

Knowledge spillovers explain much of how regions evolve. Knowledge spillovers (KSs) are knowledge externalities bounded in space. Knowledge created in one organization is "leaky", so rival organizations nearby can appropriate some of it without creating it themselves. Firms located close to key sources of knowledge, intelligence and data can therefore introduce innovations faster than rivals elsewhere. Spillovers flow through four channels: employees changing jobs, knowledge exchange with universities, informal collaborations among firms, and the sharing of know-how along value chains. Their effect depends on physical, institutional, social and socio-psychological factors that create framework conditions for entrepreneurship and wealth creation. Regions can be distinguished by how much knowledge they create and spill over, and this explains their levels of innovation and new firm creation.

The people and relationships of a region determine how well it uses that knowledge. Some authors argue that human capital is the key to economic growth, especially in urban areas. A place's capacity to attract talent and tolerate the diversity of talented people fuses with its capacity for invention and new technology, and the cultural diversity of urban networks brings in different kinds of human capital that promote innovation, accelerate information flows and raise new firm formation. Others argue that social capital plays a more integral role, because it harnesses the contributions of people other than the individual entrepreneur, namely their networks and relationships. Entrepreneurial capital brings these together. It is a composite form of capital defined at the economic (region and industry), organizational and personal (team and individual) levels, and the amalgam of institutions, talent and technology can be called creative entrepreneurial capital. Institutions, good governance and the generation and attraction of talent give regions a local edge over their neighbours, and the mix and strength of human, social, technological and entrepreneurial capital is a good measure of regional competitiveness (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

Growth corridors connect such regions across borders. Corridors are bundles of infrastructure, including rail links for passengers or freight and highways, that connect two or more urban areas in one country or across adjacent countries, enhanced by ICT, power lines and pipes for water, gas, oil and sewage. Growth corridors are instruments for boosting development in developing economies, based on World Economic Forum deliberations and the World Bank's World Development Reports. A corridor develops in stages. The economic potential of a basic transport route drives it. Hard infrastructure creates a transport corridor. Growing freight and passenger volumes then call for soft infrastructure, meaning logistics, legal, institutional and management support. Finally, the ripple effect of efficiency stimulates further activity and attracts investment, producing an economic corridor. The first was the Maputo Development Corridor (MDC), set up in 1996, whose broad success led the New Partnership for Africa's Development (NEPAD) to replicate the model throughout Africa (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Corridors can run across urban and rural areas in more than one country and can draw funding from governments, private firms, public-private partnerships, multilateral agencies and NGOs, so they link local regions with cross-border regions. As efficiency and investment build along the route, the connected regions function together as a transnational region, which is the process named in the session's learning objective LO4. Session 4 also notes that interest has since shifted towards global value chains, which integrate the international value chains of connected sectors.

Regional clusters of entrepreneurship show the result. Silicon Valley is the course's example of a regional cluster of entrepreneurship (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023), a place where the spillovers and capital described above concentrate. Session 3 adds that entrepreneurship varies regionally in other ways: Taiwan has lower start-up rates than its neighbours but high established-business and employee entrepreneurship rates, and attitudes to entrepreneurship vary more within regions than across them. Southern California, where firms founded by Chinese and Taiwanese Americans employ more than 440,000 people and helped develop China's ICT industries, shows how a regional economy can also link to a region abroad.

In conclusion, regions, and especially cities, become international hubs when spillovers, a strong mix of human, social and entrepreneurial capital, good institutions and connecting infrastructure create critical mass. As Session 4 summarizes, that critical mass is partly a function of internationalization: such regions bring the world outside into their local environment.

Model answer

International migration has become a major source of entrepreneurship. Since the International Conference on Population and Development in 1994, the link between migration and development has risen steadily on the international agenda, and the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development includes migration-related targets with regular reviews of progress (UN, 2016, as cited in Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). The economic reasoning runs like this. Migrants transfer knowledge and skills from their country of origin and acquire new capabilities in their country of residence. This helps the labour market and economy of the country of residence. Using those skills also mobilizes resources and raises the personal motivation to set up enterprises in both the destination country and the country of origin. Migration flows have grown rapidly, with over 60% of international migrants living in Asia or Europe, and settlement is shaped by geography, history and growing awareness of migrants' technical and entrepreneurial skills. Developed economies such as the United States, the United Kingdom and Germany now have increasing numbers of successful start-ups and growing firms run by migrant entrepreneurs. In most OECD countries immigrants start businesses at the same rate as the native-born, and in some they are about 1.5 times as likely to be self-employed, driven by both necessity and opportunity (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

The trajectory of migrant entrepreneurship has changed. Historically, immigrant businesses operated in highly competitive industries with low barriers to entry and low skill requirements, serving minority communities. Migrant entrepreneurship has now moved beyond traditional ethnic businesses into a wide range of technology sectors and innovative areas, locally and globally. Session 7 explains this shift with the concept of dual habitus. The migrant experience lies in a duality of contextual knowledge, empathy and access to hard and soft resources, which stems from the migrant's dual habitus in the country of destination and the country of origin. Knowing two contexts lets migrants see opportunities and serve customers in both, and access to resources in both widens what they can build. So global engagement enables migrants to break out of stereotypical minority business enclaves, grow their ventures and gain competitive advantage. Migrant entrepreneurs are not passive adherents to institutional constraints; they actively mould those institutions to suit their own initiatives. Across the course's cases, the interest in going international and using that experience to renew home ties is constant, and the intersection of institutional structures and human agency in the dual habitus explains both the scope of and the variation in global entrepreneurship (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

Migrant entrepreneurs still face real barriers. First, their actions are constrained by home-country endowments and institutional structures. Second, little is known about their networks, capabilities and locational dynamics in multiple institutional contexts. Third, global migrant entrepreneurship does not feature adequately in the work of policy makers, technology developers and business strategy analysts. Fourth, the systematic collection of granular data is needed to support informed decisions. Refugees face these barriers in a sharper form. Welcoming refugees is an investment with substantial economic dividends, since enterprising refugees start businesses that create wealth, employ locals, make the economy more dynamic and boost international trade and investment. Yet insecurity, legal rights and institutional issues make refugees different from other migrants, and fragility best describes the economic and social conditions that affect refugee entrepreneurship (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023).

The cases show global migrant networks of innovation at work. In Israel, outcomes for Israeli and Palestinian high-tech entrepreneurs depended on the unequal "capital set" of the migrants and on institutional arrangements. In Korea, the Hallyu or Korean Wave in film, television and music led to joint film production with China, drawing on the Chinese government's facilitation of cross-border flows of investors, directors and producers and creating new firms in technical service sectors, a clear case of cross-border knowledge and technology transfer. Japanese transnational entrepreneurs start "ex-pat" businesses in their late 30s to early 40s, often with savings from Japan, after exposure to overseas life during their careers. At the level of firms and regions, migrant networks generate positive externalities: migrants' specialisms create intermediary functions that serve larger multinational corporations, their networks ease the financial constraints of fast-growing firms, and firms with migrant co-owners and more migrant workers are more likely to be innovative. Migrant entrepreneurs who first enter their home country to found a global venture are more likely to found born-global ventures. Global migrant entrepreneurship contributes directly to international business creation and growth, finance and investment, R&D and innovation, and international technology transfer (Pruthi & Mitra, 2023). Session 1 adds concrete examples: immigrant-founded start-ups such as Uber and eBay, and firms founded by Chinese and Taiwanese Americans in Southern California that employ more than 440,000 people and helped develop China's ICT industries, a direct case of technology transfer back to a home country.

In conclusion, migration fosters entrepreneurship by moving skills and resources between countries. The dual habitus explains why migrant ventures have moved from ethnic enclaves into technology and global markets. Refugees and other migrants still face institutional, data and policy barriers. Global migrant networks, from Hallyu co-productions to California's Chinese and Taiwanese American firms, show their contribution to innovation and technology transfer. Global Migrant Entrepreneurship, by migrants with a foothold in their country of residence, now affects policy from migration to trade and deserves the attention the course argues it lacks.
