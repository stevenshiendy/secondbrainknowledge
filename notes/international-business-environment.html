CILO 1 asks students to identify the drivers and challenges of globalization in the post-COVID era. CILO 2 asks them to explain how cultural, legal and ethical factors influence international business decisions. The first three sessions describe the world economy in which international firms operate. Session 6 covers ethics, corporate social responsibility (CSR), famous financial scandals and the internal controls that help prevent them. Session 7 then brings the course together around a decision: which entry strategy to use, why multinational enterprises (MNEs) invest abroad, and how host countries weigh foreign direct investment (FDI).

PART AWEEK 1 · GLOBALIZATION IN THE COVID ERA
Session 1 · CILO 1

What globalization is, and where it is heading

ProblemThe COVID-19 pandemic put globalization at risk, so it matters to know exactly what globalization is and which way it is moving.
Big ideaGlobalization is the removal of barriers between countries, and its direction has changed: business now flows both ways, and the world's economic center of gravity is shifting toward emerging economies.
How (tools)A four-part definition, the 2050 ranking of the largest economies (Exhibit 1.2), and two concepts: decoupling and the multipolar world.
ResultA picture of a world economy with several engines of growth rather than one, led increasingly by China, India and other emerging economies.
In plain words

Session 1 opens with a map titled "COVID-19 Puts Globalization at Risk" (Exhibit 1.1, from The Economist, May 2021) and an icebreaker: propelled by globalization, what were the cultural, economic, political and social effects of the COVID-19 pandemic? The point of starting there is that a virus which began in one city became a world event precisely because economies are so closely connected. To judge whether globalization survives such a shock, students first need a clear definition of it.

The textbook defines globalization as the socioeconomic reform process of eliminating trade, investment, cultural, information technology and political barriers across countries. Because the barriers come down, goods, services, investment and even cultural and belief systems flow more freely, and nations become more integrated and interdependent. The definition therefore carries its own consequences: more economic growth, closer geopolitical ties, and countries that are more efficient, more interdependent and perhaps more inclusive and alike.

What has changed is the direction of the flow. Before 2000, globalization generally meant businesses from developed countries expanding into developing or emerging economies. Now business moves in both directions, and increasingly from one developing country to another, because emerging economies are adopting more open trade and free-market policies to drive their own growth. As a result the world's center of economic gravity is shifting toward emerging economies, especially China. Exhibit 1.2 makes the shift concrete: in a ranking based on nominal gross domestic product (GDP) for 2050, China moves to first place, India to third and Indonesia into fourth.

Two terms describe the world this produces. Decoupling describes a world economy with several engines of growth (the United States, the European Union, China, India, Brazil, Russia and South Africa) rather than the United States alone. A multipolar world is the global shift in which developing economies that used to depend on industrialized countries grow on their own underlying economic strengths. Exhibit 1.4 draws this future as a map of several centers of power rather than one.

The definition of globalization

  • Globalization: the socioeconomic reform process of eliminating trade, investment, cultural, information technology and political barriers across countries.
  • What it can lead to: increased economic growth and geopolitical integration and interdependence among nations.
  • What it represents: freer international trade and investment, or the free flow of goods and services (including cultural and belief systems) between countries.
  • What it includes: a process of integrating nations so that they become more economically efficient, interdependent, and perhaps relatively more inclusive and homogeneous.

The changing direction of globalization

  1. Before 2000Business generally expanded from developed countries to developing or emerging economies.
  2. NowBusiness flows in both directions, and increasingly from one developing country to another.
  3. WhyEmerging economies are implementing more open trade and free-market policies to propel growth.
  4. Where it leadsThe world's center of economic gravity is shifting toward emerging economies, especially China.

Session 1 · CILO 1

The institutions behind globalization: money, development and trade rules

ProblemOpen trade and investment need rules, stable money and finance for development, and no single country can provide them for the world.
Big ideaAfter World War II, governments of the free world created international organizations to cooperate and coordinate: the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the World Bank and the World Trade Organization (WTO).
How (tools)The IMF's four roles, the World Bank's focus areas and its five institutions (Table 1.1), and the WTO's six functions and five principles.
ResultA division of labor: the IMF keeps the monetary system stable, the World Bank funds development, and the WTO sets and enforces the rules of trade.
In plain words

Globalization did not grow on its own. It gained momentum after World War II, when the governments of the free world recognized that international cooperation and coordination were needed, and that recognition produced three major international organizations: the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the World Bank and the World Trade Organization (WTO). Each tackles a different obstacle to open trade and investment.

The IMF deals with money. Countries cannot trade freely if exchange rates swing wildly or if a country runs out of the foreign currency it needs to pay for imports, so the IMF provides a forum on international monetary problems, promotes exchange rate stability and an open system of payments, and lends foreign exchange to countries with balance of payments problems. The World Bank deals with development. Its first job was to help rebuild Europe after the war; today it works on integrating countries into global trade, advising on policy, supporting international financial standards, transferring knowledge to developing countries and fighting communicable diseases. It does this through a group of five institutions, each with its own job.

The WTO deals with the rules of trade. Its story starts in 1948 under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), which liberalized trade by lowering or removing barriers such as tariffs, quotas and subsidies through a series of negotiations. The WTO now administers trade agreements, hosts negotiations, settles disputes, reviews national trade policies, trains developing countries, and cooperates with the IMF and the World Bank, so the three institutions work as a system. Its work rests on five principles, beginning with trade without discrimination.

The International Monetary Fund (IMF)

The IMF's role in global financial stability:

  1. ForumProvides a forum for cooperation on international monetary problems.
  2. TradeFacilitates international trade that promotes job creation, economic growth and poverty reduction.
  3. StabilityPromotes exchange rate stability and an open system of international payments.
  4. LendingLends countries foreign exchange to help address balance of payments problems.

The World Bank

Its initial role was to aid the reconstruction of Europe after World War II. Its current focus areas are:

  • Global integration: through trade liberalization and expansion.
  • Policy advice: analysis and national trading policy advice to strengthen free-market institutions and infrastructure.
  • Financial standards: agreements supporting international standards in financial systems.
  • Knowledge transfer: information and knowledge transfer to developing countries to support sustainable development.
  • Health: eradicating communicable diseases.

Session 1 · CILO 1

Making globalization work: institutions, technology and the controversy

ProblemGlobalization can raise living standards, but it can also be a force for exploitation and injustice, and it creates winners and losers.
Big ideaGlobalization is sustainable only where strong, adaptive institutions and sound policies are in place, and where those it harms are supported.
How (tools)Adaptive institutions, four policy measures, the role of information technology (IT) and the digital divide, and the arguments against globalization.
ResultA balanced view: more integration offers a more hopeful future, but there is no guarantee that everyone's quality of life will rise.
In plain words

The international organizations set the global rules, but each country still decides how well it plugs into the world economy. The textbook calls the relevant national structures institutions: the rules, the enforcement mechanisms and the organizations that support market transactions. Institutions only work if a political system is also in place. The best kind are adaptive institutions, government organizations that create strong incentives for private investment and operate under a system of checks and balances, which the textbook says function best in a democratic system of government. Four policy measures promote globalization: good governance, competitive markets, property rights and anticorruption policies.

Technology speeds the process. Lower broadband prices have let emerging economies reach rural towns and villages, and the information technology (IT) revolution has spread almost instantly. Yet the same technology opens a digital divide, the perceived economic gap between countries with easy access to digital technology and those with limited access.

This is where the controversy begins. The free flow of ideas, people, goods, services and capital that integrates economies can also be a force for exploitation and injustice. Critics point to job losses and income stagnation, the loss of local control over economic policy, the disappearance of old industries and the erosion of the communities built around them. The textbook's answer is measured. Most economists agree that greater integration and openness offer the citizens of the global village a more rewarding and hopeful future, and globalization can raise the quality of life; but there is no guarantee that it will do so for everyone, or that every change it brings will be positive. Globalization creates winners and losers, which is why the session's topic list includes the case for temporarily supporting those who are hurt by it.

Institutions and policy

  • Institutions: the rules, enforcement mechanisms and organizations that support market transactions. To work appropriately, political systems must also be in place.
  • Adaptive institutions: government organizations that create strong incentives for private investment and operate under a system of checks and balances; they function best in a democratic system of government.

Four effective policy measures that promote globalization

  1. Good governance
  2. Competitive markets
  3. Property rights
  4. Anticorruption policies

Information technology and the digital divide

  • Broadband: lower broadband prices have allowed emerging economies to reach rural towns and villages.
  • Speed: the IT revolution has been relatively instantaneous.
  • Digital divide: the perceived economic gap between countries with easy access to digital technology and those with limited access.

The globalization controversy

The increasingly free flow of ideas, people, goods, services and capital that leads to closer integration and interdependence of economies and societies can be a force for exploitation and injustice.

Arguments against globalization

  2. Loss of local control over economic policies and developments
  3. Disappearance of old industries

Making globalization work for all

  • The hopeful view: most economists would agree that greater integration of the world economy and more openness to efficiency and modernization offer all citizens of the global village a more rewarding and hopeful future.
  • The caution: globalization has the potential to increase the quality of life, but there cannot be a guarantee that quality of life will increase for all people, or that all changes caused by globalization will be positive.
  • The conclusion: globalization does create winners and losers.

Why countries trade: the benefits of trade and foreign investment

In plain words

Session 2 starts from history. Its icebreaker asks how the history of economic development and exploitation affects a country or region, what the growth of international trade and investment does to a country's economy, and how economic success can look different in different places. The session's first claim answers the middle question directly: trade and foreign investment are good for society as a whole.

The argument is made from the consumer's side. International trade benefits all consumers in three major ways. It gives them a greater choice of goods and services, because they can buy what other countries make as well as what their own country makes. It lowers the prices of the goods and services they consume. Together, these raise living standards. Foreign direct investment (FDI) adds the producer's side: money that crosses borders to acquire or set up businesses in another country.

Session 2 · CILO 1

Who should make what: the major theories of international trade

ProblemCountries differ in what they can produce well, and a theory is needed to explain which goods a nation should export and why trade makes nations richer.
Big ideaTrade theory moved from mercantilism, where only a surplus counts, to specialization, where each nation gains by producing what it makes most efficiently.
How (tools)Mercantilism, absolute and comparative advantage, the Heckscher–Ohlin (H–O) theory, factor price equalization theory, and Porter's Diamond.
ResultAn explanation of trade patterns based on efficiency and factor endowments, and a model of why some nations' industries are competitive.
In plain words

The session groups the theories into three families. Mercantilism rests on the premise that a nation can gain from trading only if it runs a trade surplus. Specialization, through absolute and comparative advantage, encourages countries to produce the goods and services they make most efficiently. Factor endowments, through the Heckscher–Ohlin (H–O) theory and factor price equalization, explain that nations mainly export goods and services that make intense use of their abundant factors of production.

Mercantilism is the oldest trade theory, practiced from about 1500 to 1750 as Europe moved toward nationalism. Wealth, personal and national, was measured largely by the precious metal a country owned, so a nation became rich by exporting as much as possible and importing as little as possible. The textbook points out its flaw: mercantilists did not see the big picture. If every nation pushed exports up and imports down, the world would have a surplus of exports, prices would be depressed, exporting countries would earn less, and they would undersell one another.

The specialization theories fix this by asking what each country does best. A country has an absolute advantage when it can produce a good or service more efficiently than another country. Comparative advantage goes further: a country that has an absolute advantage in two or more goods can still produce one of them relatively more efficiently than the other, and it gains by specializing in that one. The Heckscher–Ohlin (H–O) theory then explains where comparative advantage comes from: a nation's factor endowments of land, labor, capital and technology. H–O assumes perfect competition and that factors of production cannot move between countries. Factor price equalization theory relaxes that second assumption: when factors move freely among trading nations, efficiency increases and resources are reallocated until prices reach equilibrium.

Porter's "Diamond" model asks a different question, why some nations are competitive in particular industries, and answers with four linked conditions plus two outside influences. Factor conditions bring in comparative advantage and H–O; demand conditions add the importance of domestic demand; related and supporting industries add the strength of the surrounding industry; and firm strategy, structure and rivalry add the way companies manage themselves. Chance (an external shock such as the COVID-19 pandemic) and government (which can help or hurt) act on all four.

Mercantilism

  • When: the oldest form of trade theory, practiced during 1500–1750 as Europe moved toward nationalism.
  • Measure of wealth: personal and national wealth was largely determined by the amount of precious metal owned.
  • Policy: export as much as possible and import as little as possible, so that the country has a trade surplus.
  • Flaw: if all nations increased exports and decreased imports, the surplus of exports would lead to depressed prices, lower earnings for exporting countries and underselling.

Absolute and comparative advantage

  • Absolute advantage: the ability of one country to produce a good or service more efficiently than another.
  • Comparative advantage: the ability of one country that has an absolute advantage in the production of two or more goods (or services) to produce one of them relatively more efficiently than the other.

Heckscher–Ohlin (H–O) theory

The H–O theory attributes a nation's comparative advantage to its factor endowments:

  1. Landquantity, quality, and the mineral resources beneath it
  2. Laborquantity and skills
  3. Capitalcost
  4. Technologyquality

Key assumptions of the H–O theory

  • Perfect competition in the marketplace.
  • Perfect immobility of factors of production among countries.

Factor price equalization theory

  • Claim: when factors are allowed to move freely among trading nations, efficiency increases, leading to a superior allocation of production of goods and services among countries.
  • Mechanism: free mobility of factors leads to efficient reallocation of resources (factors of production) until price equilibrium is reached.

Session 2 · CILO 1

How governments manage trade: instruments of trade policy

ProblemTrade theory favors open markets, yet governments still act to change how much flows into and out of their countries.
Big ideaTrade policy is every government action that seeks to alter the size of merchandise or service flows, and it uses a set of standard instruments justified by economic, social and political reasons.
How (tools)Tariffs (specific and ad valorem), preferential duties and the Generalized System of Preferences (GSP), most favored nation (MFN) status, and nontariff barriers (quotas, voluntary export restraints and domestic content rules).
ResultA toolkit that affects businesses, consumers and governments, and a list of the reasons countries give for "managed" trade.
In plain words

The theories in the previous chapter show why open trade makes nations richer. Governments nevertheless intervene, and the textbook defines trade policy as all government actions that seek to alter the size of merchandise and/or service flows from and to a country. The session lists four instruments: tariffs, preferential duties, most favored nation (MFN) status and nontariff barriers.

The most familiar instrument is the tariff, a customs duty on imports collected by the government agency that regulates imports. A tariff can be specific, a fixed dollar amount per physical unit, or ad valorem, a constant percentage of the value of one unit of the imported good. A government can also treat countries differently. A preferential duty is a tariff a nation applies to goods from certain countries but not to the same goods from others. The Generalized System of Preferences (GSP) is a large-scale example, in which many developed countries allow duty-free imports of a selected list of products that originate in specific countries.

Not every barrier is a duty. Nontariff barriers control trade by quantity or by sourcing rules instead of by price. Import quotas, also called quantitative restrictions (QRs), cap the number of units that can be imported. A voluntary export restraint (VER) has the exporting country itself agree to limit its exports to another country for a time. Domestic content provisions require that a certain percentage of an import's value be sourced domestically.

Why would a government restrict trade it knows to be beneficial? The session sorts the reasons behind today's "managed" trade into two groups. The socioeconomic rationale covers countertrade, export cartels, the protection of infant industries, questionable labor practices, environmental considerations, and health and safety. The geopolitical rationale covers national security, strategic industries and embargoes.

Keeping score with the world: the balance of payments

ProblemA country trades goods and services, earns and pays income, and buys and sells assets abroad, and it needs one record that shows all of these flows together.
Big ideaThe balance of payments (BOP) records every transaction between one country and the rest of the world, sorted into the current, capital and financial accounts.
How (tools)The parts of each account, net errors and omissions, and reserves, illustrated with the U.S. balance of payments (Exhibit 4.2) and U.S. trade with its partners (Exhibit 4.3).
ResultA way to read where a country's foreign money comes from and goes to, and why the total ends up as a change in foreign currency reserves.
In plain words

Session 3 opens with the U.S. dollar. Its icebreaker asks whether the dollar will remain a safe-haven and reserve currency in future crises, and whether large U.S. government deficits might reduce other countries' willingness to hold dollars. Exhibit 4.1 shows why the question is live: the euro-to-dollar exchange rate moved widely between January 1999 and June 2021, and the chart marks a period of the dollar rising against the euro. Questions like these can only be answered by tracking how money moves between countries, and the tool for that is the balance of payments (BOP), which shows all transactions between one country and the rest of the world over a given period.

The BOP is split into three accounts. The current account records the everyday activity of consumers and businesses: the trade balance in goods, the trade balance in services, primary income and secondary income. Exports bring dollars in and carry a plus sign; imports send dollars out and carry a minus sign, so a country that imports more goods than it exports runs a goods trade deficit. Exhibit 4.3 shows the U.S. pattern with its largest partners: with Mexico, for example, U.S. exports of 189.57 sit against imports of 308.55.

The capital account is the least significant item, because the amounts are usually small. It records non-produced nonfinancial assets and capital transfers, such as land sold to an embassy or the sale of leases and licenses. The financial account records investment: foreign direct investment (FDI) in factories and equipment abroad, portfolio investment in debt and equity securities, financial derivatives and other investments such as "hot money". Because the source data are imperfect, the accounts never balance exactly, and the gap is recorded as net errors and omissions. The total of the three accounts is sometimes called the overall balance of payments; once it is adjusted for errors and omissions, it shows up as the change in the country's foreign currency reserves.

Inside the current account

  • Trade balance in goods: the net of merchandise exports (+, as money comes in when merchandise is exported) and merchandise imports (−, as money leaves to pay for imports). A country that imports more than it exports has a goods trade deficit.
  • Trade balance in services: the net of service exports (+) and imports (−), including shipping, airlines, consulting, insurance, banking, tourism, software development, movies and music.
  • Primary income balance: the net of labor and investment income earned by residents from abroad (a credit, an inflow) less what they pay to the rest of the world (a debit, an outflow).
  • Secondary income account: current transfers between residents and nonresidents, showing their role in distributing income between economies.

The capital account

The least significant item in the BOP, since the amounts are usually small. It records acquisitions and disposals of non-produced nonfinancial assets, for example:

  • Land: sold to embassies, or returned when an embassy closes.
  • Leases and licenses: sales of trademarks and copyrights.
  • Capital transfers: resources provided for capital improvement, such as security and surveillance systems.

Inside the financial account

  • Risk premium: the added return investors require for the risk of a security or asset.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): purchases of fixed assets abroad (such as factories and equipment) used to make and sell goods and services abroad.
  • Portfolio investments: cross-border transactions and positions in debt or equity securities other than those included in FDI.
  • Net financial derivatives and employee stock options: relate to risk transfer rather than to the supply of BOP funds.
  • Other investments: hot money or short-term deposits seeking higher returns, currency seeking a safe haven, trade credit and advances, and other receivables and payables.

Net errors and omissions, and reserves

  • Net errors and omissions: imbalances that result from imperfections in source data and compilation. They are derived as the balance of the financial account minus the balance of the current and capital accounts, and they reflect two issues: timing and illegal trade.
  • Reserves: the part of a country's international liquidity controlled by monetary authorities for financing balance of payments needs, intervening in exchange markets and related purposes. Reserve assets must be foreign currency assets that actually exist.
  • Overall balance: the total of the current, capital and financial accounts. Adjusted for net errors and omissions, it is reflected in the change in foreign currency reserves.
  • World trade: the growth of international trade has been driven by world economic growth and the elimination of barriers to trade (Exhibit 4.4 tracks merchandise trade volumes by region, 2010–2019).

Trading currencies: the foreign exchange market and monetary systems

ProblemEvery international transaction needs one currency to be swapped for another, and the price of that swap can be fixed by rule or left to the market.
Big ideaThe foreign exchange market prices currencies, and the international monetary system has moved from fixed links to gold and the dollar toward floating rates set by supply and demand.
How (tools)Quote and forward-market terms; the gold standard, Bretton Woods (1944), the Smithsonian Agreement (1971) and the Jamaica Agreement (1976); and the language of floats, special drawing rights (SDRs), dollarization and hard and soft currencies.
ResultThe vocabulary needed to read an exchange rate and to explain why currency values now move.
In plain words

A bank that exchanges currency quotes two prices: what it will pay for a currency (the bid) and what it will sell it for (the ask). The bid-ask spread between them is the bank's fee. A rate can be quoted two ways. A direct quote gives the price of a foreign currency in dollars, the number of dollars per unit of foreign currency; an indirect quote is its reciprocal, the price of a dollar in foreign currency. Currencies can also be bought for future delivery at a forward rate, and comparing the forward rate with today's spot rate tells whether a currency sells at a discount or a premium. A firm that locks in a forward rate is using a hedge, insurance that reduces future risk.

How these prices are set depends on the monetary system, and the session traces its history. Under the gold standard, currency values were pegged to the market value of gold. The Bretton Woods Agreement of 1944 built a global system in which the U.S. dollar was pegged to gold at a fixed rate and the currencies of 43 other countries were fixed to the dollar; it also set up the International Monetary Fund (IMF) to keep the system stable. In 1971 the Smithsonian Agreement allowed the United States to devalue the dollar against other currencies, and in 1976 the Jamaica Agreement let countries adopt different exchange rate systems, including floating their currencies. The result is today's flexible rate system, in which supply and demand set currency prices, influenced by current account imbalances, economic structure and growth, inflation, interest rates and expectations.

Floating is a matter of degree. A clean float is largely market-determined, with minimal government intervention; a dirty float involves varying degrees of intervention to keep a currency within an acceptable range. Some countries go the other way and adopt dollarization, using the dollar or another foreign currency alongside or instead of their own. The session's discussion activity applies these ideas to China: until 2005 the yuan was pegged to the dollar, and today it floats within a narrow margin around a base rate set against a basket of currencies. Students are asked what would happen to the dollar-to-yuan rate if China floated the yuan freely, given how much the United States imports from China.

Explaining and forecasting exchange rates: the parity theories

ProblemFloating exchange rates change over time, and firms need a way to judge whether a currency is over- or undervalued and where it is heading.
Big ideaArbitrage links currencies to what they can buy: identical goods should cost the same everywhere, which is purchasing power parity (PPP), and bonds should earn the same return everywhere, which is interest rate parity (IRP).
How (tools)The law of one price, the Big Mac Index, the PPP and IRP equations, and two forecasting methods: the forward rate and a multiple regression model.
ResultTheories that work over the long run but only loosely in the short run, because transport costs, intervention, risk and market psychology get in the way.
In plain words

The parity theories start from a simple idea. The law of one price says that identical goods should sell for the same price in different countries once the price is converted into one currency. If they do not, traders can practice arbitrage, buying in the cheaper market and selling in the dearer one, and that buying and selling pushes the prices back together. Applied to a whole basket of goods, the idea becomes purchasing power parity (PPP): a basket of goods should cost approximately the same across countries. The Big Mac Index is the popular version, using the price of a Big Mac sandwich in each country to judge the relative values of currencies.

PPP can be written as an equation that links prices, inflation and the change in the exchange rate between the United States and Europe. It says that if prices rise faster in one place, its currency must adjust to keep the basket equally priced. The evidence is mixed. PPP appears to hold over periods longer than five years but may not hold in shorter ones, and it does not reliably explain exchange rate changes between countries with similar inflation. Transport costs, trade barriers, government intervention, multinationals with pricing power, market expectations and non-traded goods all get in the way, which is why the slide asks what could make the Big Mac Index give inaccurate estimates of PPP.

Interest rate parity (IRP) applies the same logic to bonds. Investors who buy and sell bonds to earn arbitrage profits should drive bond returns in different countries to the same level. Covered interest rate parity uses the forward exchange rate; uncovered interest rate parity uses the expected exchange rate. The IRP equation says that a dollar invested in a U.S. bond should earn the same as a dollar converted into euros, invested in a European bond and converted back. Again the evidence is mixed, because transaction costs, political risk, legal restrictions, taxes, managed floats, herd behavior and central bank intervention can stop traders from arbitraging the gap away.

These relationships give two ways to forecast exchange rates. The first uses covered IRP: the forward rate is the spot rate adjusted by the forward premium. The second is a multiple regression model that explains the change in the exchange rate by the differences between two countries' inflation rates, interest rates and gross domestic product (GDP) growth rates.

The law of one price and PPP

  • Law of one price: identical goods should sell for the same price in different countries according to local currencies.
  • Arbitrage: buying goods in a lower-priced market and selling them in a higher-priced market to make profits.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): the theory that a basket of goods should have approximately the same prices across different countries.
  • Big Mac Index: a calculation using the cost of a Big Mac sandwich to assess the relative values of currencies.

The PPP equation

PUS(1 + IUS) = (1 + p)PE(1 + IE) PUS = price index of U.S. goods in dollars PE = price index of European goods in dollars IUS = U.S. inflation rate (dollar terms) IE = European inflation rate (euro terms) p = % change in the euro = forward premium p = [(F − S)/S] × 100 F = forward dollar/euro rate; S = spot rate

Empirical tests of PPP

  • Long run: PPP appears to hold for periods exceeding five years, but may not hold in shorter periods.
  • Similar inflation: for countries with little difference in inflation rates, PPP does not reliably explain exchange rate changes.

What affects PPP predictions

  1. Transportation costs and trade barriers
  2. Government intervention in trade and exchange rates
  3. Multinational firms with pricing power
  4. Market expectations about economic factors
  5. Goods not traded but that affect internal prices

Interest rate parity (IRP)

  • Interest rate parity (IRP): the theory that bond interest rates in different countries will become the same as investors buy and sell bonds to make arbitrage profits.
  • Covered interest rate parity: forward exchange rates and spot exchange rates set interest rates on bonds in different countries equal to one another.
  • Uncovered interest rate parity: expected forward exchange rates and spot exchange rates set interest rates on bonds in different countries equal to one another.

The IRP equation

(1 + iUS) = (F/S)(1 + iE) iUS = interest rate on U.S. bond iE = interest rate on European bond F = forward dollar-to-euro rate S = spot dollar-to-euro rate If S is fairly stable over time: (iUS − iE) = (F − S)/S = p

The equation says that a dollar invested in a U.S. bond earns the same dollar return as a dollar converted to euros, invested in European bonds, with the euro returns later repatriated to dollars.

Why IRP evidence is mixed

  1. Transaction costs are one impediment to achieving IRP.
  2. Political risk, legal restrictions, tax effects and managed-float regimes can disrupt traders' ability to arbitrage away profit differentials.
  3. Market psychology (herd behavior) can move rates as traders speculate in currencies.
  4. Central bank intervention may cause IRP not to hold at all points in time.

What culture is: the eight elements of culture

ProblemA company that succeeds at home can fail abroad because it misreads the way of life of the people it sells to and employs.
Big ideaCulture is learned behavior, and the degree to which a company adapts to it is often the difference between success and failure in international markets.
How (tools)The eight elements of culture: language, religion, values and attitudes, manners and customs, material elements, aesthetics, education, and social institutions.
ResultA checklist a global firm can use to judge which aspects of culture matter most in each market and to adjust its operations to them.
In plain words

Session 4 opens by asking how cultural understanding plays a role in global business success. The textbook's answer starts with a definition: culture is learned behavior, a way of life for one group of people living in a single, related and independent community. Because it is learned, it differs from place to place, and a firm cannot assume that what works at home will work abroad. A global firm must work out how important each aspect of culture is in the markets it serves and recognize it when doing business there, and its operations must adjust to the cultural environment of each country. The degree of that adaptation is often the difference between success and failure.

To make culture manageable, the textbook breaks it into eight elements. Language comes first, and it has two layers: verbal communication, what the message actually says, and nonverbal communication, the tone, gestures, eye contact and body language that go with it. Because translation errors are easy to make, firms use backward translation, translating a message into the foreign language and then back into English to check it. Religion affects business operations, how products are made and marketed, which holidays are observed, and working days and hours. Values are the basic beliefs that run through a society and attitudes are feelings or opinions. Manners and customs are the usual way a society does things.

The remaining elements are more structural. Material elements are the direct result of technology, seen in a society's economic, social, financial and marketing infrastructure. Aesthetics, mainly color, form and music, are what a society perceives as taste and beauty. Education matters because a population's level of education is a major factor in economic growth, and host-country staff must be able to work or be trained for a range of jobs; countries also stress different specialties. Finally, social institutions describe how people relate to one another in groups, through reference groups and social stratification.

Culture and its influence on business

  • The firm's task: ascertain the level of importance of various aspects of culture in each foreign market and recognize them when doing business overseas.
  • Adjustment: companies' operations need to recognize and adjust to the cultural environment in the countries they serve.
  • The stakes: the degree to which a company adapts to a culture is often the difference between success and failure in international markets.

Session 4 · CILO 2

Culture in practice: doing business around the world

ProblemGeneral frameworks do not tell a manager how to greet a host, open a negotiation or build trust in a particular country.
Big ideaEach market has its own rules for relationships, rank, time and etiquette, and culture also shapes how a firm should manage, market and communicate there.
How (tools)Country notes for Japan, Korea, China, Arab countries, Latin America, India, Russia and South Africa; five ways culture affects management and marketing; and examples of U.S. business jargon that foreigners would not understand (Exhibit 5.4).
ResultConcrete guidance for meeting and negotiating in eight markets, and the reasons a firm must tailor its management style, products, advertising and language.
In plain words

The country notes share a common thread: in most of these markets, business follows the relationship rather than the other way round. In Korea a personal relationship must come before business matters can be discussed with foreigners. Latin American customers want to develop a personal relationship before doing business. In Russia and South Africa a third-party introduction is better than introducing yourself, and in Japan new business partners should be referred through a third party. In China the relationship takes a particular form, guanxi, a friendship among unequals built on an unlimited exchange of favors. Because those favors can make even a low-ranking person influential, a foreigner may need to win the favor of lower-level people to reach the decision makers above them.

Rank and respect form a second thread. Japanese hosts need to know a visitor's title and rank, expect visitors to arrive prepared and with decision-making authority, and treat business card etiquette as important. Korean culture respects elders, and its ideas of yangban (the noble class's culture of honor, reputation and dignity) and inwa (harmony among unequals) shape relationships between superiors and subordinates. In India formality and civility are expected even in casual meetings, and diplomacy is valued over bluntness. A third thread is etiquette and time: Arab hosts notice posture, the use of the left hand and the soles of one's shoes, while Latin Americans are more flexible about time than North Americans, and in Russia the foreigner must be punctual although the host may be late.

Culture matters beyond the meeting room. A country's culture affects its attitudes toward work, so management styles can create challenges or lead to success. New products must take management styles and other aspects of marketing into account, advertising campaigns must be carefully tailored to local cultures, and communication styles differ. Exhibit 5.4 makes the last point with a list of U.S. business expressions, such as "flying by the seat of my pants" or "reinvent the wheel", that foreign business representatives would not understand.

Country notes

Japan

  • Greeting: a slight bow and handshake are appropriate; business card etiquette is important.
  • Eye contact: it is not appropriate to look directly into the eyes of your Japanese hosts.
  • Rank: it is important for your hosts to know your title and rank.
  • Group orientation: Japanese business is group-oriented rather than individualistic.
  • Preparation: executives expect foreign representatives to arrive prepared and to have decision-making authority.
  • Referral: new potential partners should be referred to Japanese business representatives through a third party.

Korea

  • Elders: respected for their knowledge and wisdom.
  • Yangban: the noble class's culture of honor, reputation and dignity (similar to gravitas in Western cultures).
  • Inwa: harmony among unequals; loyalty is owed to parents and authority figures, yet superiors are responsible for the well-being of their subordinates.
  • Relationship first: a personal relationship needs to occur before business matters can be discussed with foreigners.

China

  • Guanxi: a philosophy denoting friendships among unequals (as between subordinates and superiors) and the unlimited exchange of favors; it is utilitarian, not based on sentiment, emotion or group orientation.
  • Uneven exchanges: often an advantage for the weaker member of the relationship.
  • Hidden influence: persons of low rank may be powerful through guanxi relationships with superiors.
  • Access: foreigners may need to seek out lower-level persons and obtain their favor to reach more powerful decision makers.

Arab countries

  • Posture: avoid sitting so that the sole of one's shoe is shown; good posture is imperative.
  • Hands: the left hand is viewed as "unclean".
  • Topics: do not inquire about the wives of Arab business representatives; do not overly praise your hosts' possessions, as this may suggest you expect them as gifts.
  • Women: Arab business representatives will probably be reluctant to do business with women; if a woman is accepted, modest dress is appropriate.
  • Conversation: discussions may frequently divert from the topic, then return to it.

Latin America

  • Relationship first: potential customers want to develop a personal relationship before doing business.
  • Time: more flexible about time than North Americans.

In plain words

Session 5 opens with two questions: how do different legal and political systems affect global business, and what legal and political risks come with doing business globally? The answer begins with the political system. In a democracy, Greek for "rule by the people", all citizens have the right to vote. In its pure Athenian form all adult citizens vote directly on community matters; in a representative democracy citizens elect representatives for a set period. A totalitarian government, such as a military dictatorship, governs without the support or consent of the citizenry.

Alongside the political system sits the economic ideology, which decides how much room private business has. Under capitalism, businesses are privately owned, strong individual incentives exist and the government plays very little role in the economy. Under socialism, the government plays a strong role in the economy and may own stakes in certain businesses. Under communism, the government or state owns and controls all major factors of production, and the society is philosophically an economically classless one. The further a country sits from capitalism, the more the state shapes what a foreign firm can do.

These settings produce risk. Economic risks are the risk that economic problems or mismanagement in a country will seriously harm business there: limits on transferring or exchanging the currency, devaluation, doubts about loan repayment, labor problems, inflation and corruption. They are not independent of political risks, which come in two sizes: micropolitical risk affects only a certain industry or set of firms, while macropolitical risk affects essentially all businesses in a country. Exhibit 6.1 lists the most dangerous countries in the world in 2021, led by Afghanistan, Syria and Iraq. Firms can mitigate these risks through careful planning, and governments offer insurance: in the United States, the Terrorism Risk Insurance Act of 2002 and the political risk insurance sold by the U.S. International Development Finance Corporation (DFC).

Corruption is the last risk in the session's list: businesses illegally altering private or public decision making through bribes, kickbacks, blackmail, extortion and related activities. Private corruption involves other businesses, individuals or groups; public corruption involves illegal payments or pressure on government officials to win contracts or approvals.

The rules of the game: legal systems, areas of law and intellectual property

ProblemWhat counts as a crime, a valid contract, a fair price or a safe product differs from country to country, and a firm abroad is bound by the local rules.
Big ideaEach country's legal system and areas of law set the terms of doing business there, and intellectual property protections give creators a limited, legally granted monopoly.
How (tools)Civil, common and theocratic law; criminal, contract, tax, antitrust, product safety and dispute settlement law; patents, trademarks and copyrights.
ResultA map of the legal questions a global firm must answer in each country, from tax location decisions to how its disputes and its inventions will be handled.
In plain words

Countries build their laws in three ways. Civil law is based on a comprehensive listing of legal rules in sets of written codes. Under common law, legislative bodies enact less specific rules and give judges and courts considerable authority to interpret them based on precedent and other factors. Theocratic law is based on a religious document and religious teachings. Within any system, a global business meets several areas of law, and the session walks through six.

Criminal law decides which violations are crimes punishable by possible imprisonment, and what counts as a crime differs between countries and changes over time: alcohol sales were unlawful in the United States during Prohibition in the 1920s and 1930s, and marijuana, once illegal everywhere there, is now legal in about half the states. Contract law governs legally enforceable agreements to exchange, and since contracts are meaningless unless they can be enforced, firms must learn the contract law of each country they work in. Tax law varies widely and plays a considerable role in where firms locate; some set up in tax havens with little tax transparency to avoid taxes. Antitrust law promotes fair competition by banning collusion, and other countries may be far less aggressive about it than the United States. Product safety law decides who carries the burden of safety: under caveat emptor it is the buyer, under caveat venditor the seller. Dispute settlement law decides whether a dispute goes to litigation in a public court or to arbitration before neutral private persons.

The session ends with intellectual property, the product of intellectual rather than physical activity. A nation protects it by granting the creator limited monopoly rights, and there are three main protections. A patent excludes others from making, using or selling an invention for a period of time; a trademark protects the name, symbol or design that identifies a business's product or service; and a copyright gives authors, artists and composers the exclusive right to publish and disseminate their work.

  • Definition: establishes which violations of a nation's laws are crimes punishable by possible incarceration.

Contract law

  • Definition: the body of law governing legally enforceable agreements between parties to engage in economic exchange.
  • Enforcement: contracts are essentially meaningless unless they can be successfully enforced.
  • Implication: companies and individuals in global business need to become familiar with the contract law of the countries where they may operate.

Tax law

  • Definition: the body of law dealing with governmental levying of taxes on individuals and corporations.
  • Location: tax rates and regulations vary widely and play a considerable role in location decisions.
  • Tax havens: countries with little or no tax transparency where some companies establish operations to avoid taxes, such as Mauritius, Belize, the Cayman Islands, Antigua, Barbuda and the Isle of Man.

Antitrust law

  • Purpose: designed to promote "fair competition" among businesses; also known as "antimonopoly laws".
  • Collusion: companies acting in a manner that secretly thwarts competition among themselves; antitrust laws typically prohibit it.
  • Monopoly: a single seller of a product in an industry with very high barriers to entry.
  • Variation: other countries may be far less aggressive than the United States in antitrust regulation.

Product safety law

Places the burden of determining product safety on consumers.

Caveat venditor ("seller beware")

Places on manufacturers or sellers the burden of making sure their products are safe, or at least clearly and explicitly warning consumers about the potential safety risks.

Dispute settlement law

  • Definition: governs how disputes arising in global business are settled.
  • Litigation: the public option, bringing a dispute to a publicly or governmentally run court of law.
  • Arbitration: an alternative dispute resolution process in which the parties designate a neutral private person or group to hear and decide the case.

In plain words

The scandals the session describes share a method: fraudulent or misleading accounting used to make a company look healthier than it was. Enron, a major global provider of electricity, natural gas, pulp and paper and communications, "cooked its books" with innovative and misleading accounting, including mark-to-market practices, to avoid disclosing unprofitable operations. WorldCom manipulated earnings to show false growth and prop up its stock price, recording "line costs" as assets instead of expenses and overstating revenues; its own internal auditors found the fraud, and its bankruptcy wiped out stockholders and bondholders. Vivendi, a French multinational, hid cash flow, liquidity and debt problems for years to boost its stock price and make acquisitions; its board discovered the fraud, the chief executive officer (CEO) resigned and paid a $1 million fine, and the company paid $50 million. Parmalat, an Italian dairy and food multinational, used risky derivatives and purchases of its own debt to disguise losses on international acquisitions, and was placed under a government commissioner after it collapsed into bankruptcy. Older cases show the pattern is not new: the Teapot Dome scandal and Charles Ponzi's 1920s scheme of paying earlier investors with later investors' money.

The textbook holds that ethics can be taught, through five fundamentals: personal integrity, the responsibility of business in society, ethical decision making, ethical leadership and corporate governance. PART GWEEK 7 · ENTRY STRATEGIES IN GLOBAL BUSINESS
Session 7 · CILO 3

Choosing how to go abroad: entry strategies from exporting to subsidiaries

ProblemA firm that wants to enter a foreign market must decide how much capital and control to commit, and every choice carries a different level of risk.
Big ideaEntry strategies sit on a line from low risk and low return to high risk and high return, and the firm chooses according to the risk it is willing to take.
How (tools)Risk profile and the risk–return trade-off; Exhibit 8.1; strategies that minimize financial risk (export-import, licensing, franchising), share it (strategic alliances, international joint ventures) or take on more of it (foreign acquisitions, wholly owned subsidiaries).
ResultA way to compare the seven entry strategies and to explain why a firm would pick one over another.
In plain words

Session 7 adds the course's third learning outcome, applying strategies for market entry, and opens with the question of risk. A firm's risk profile is the potential financial loss its owners are willing to take. The risk–return trade-off says that the greater the risk of losing invested capital, the greater the likely reward. Risk comes from three sources: ownership, operation and asset transfer. Exhibit 8.1 lines up the entry strategies on this trade-off, from export-import business at the low-risk, low-return end, through licensing, franchising, strategic alliances, joint ventures and foreign acquisitions, to wholly owned foreign subsidiaries at the high-risk, high-return end.

The three strategies that minimize financial risk keep the firm's own capital out of the foreign market. Export-import business is relatively low risk because capital is not tied up and it is easy to enter or exit; trade finance techniques reduce the remaining risk, and a firm can run with as few as three or four employees. Licensing brings the trader closer to its overseas partner, giving it the rights or technology to make and sell the product in return for a license fee, so it carries slightly more risk. A firm licenses rather than exports when making the product in the target country saves on transport, labor and raw materials, or when it lacks the money for plant abroad; penalty clauses in the license give it an exit. Franchising carries relatively more risk than licensing, because the franchisor supplies equipment, services or startup costs and must keep quality the same at every location, but local franchisees usually supply the capital.

The middle strategies share the risk with a partner. A strategic alliance is an agreement between firms to cooperate for revenue without creating a separate jointly owned entity, and its weakness is that a member can quit early. An international joint venture does create a jointly owned business, usually with at least one local firm that knows the domestic economy, culture and politics and can handle local logistics and bureaucracy; it makes sense when the investment is too large for one company. At the top of the scale, a firm can buy an established foreign firm through a foreign acquisition, gaining instant access to its production, marketing and distribution networks, or build its own wholly owned subsidiary from scratch as a "green field" plant, which needs large capital and heavy marketing because the newcomer is unknown.

Strategy choice: risk

  • Risk profile: the potential financial loss that entrepreneurs are willing to take in a business.
  • Risk–return trade-off: the greater the risk (loss of capital invested) entrepreneurs are willing to take, the greater the rewards (profit) they are likely to reap.
  • Sources of risk: ownership, operation or asset transfer.

  • Limited resources: the licensor may not have the financial resources to invest in overseas plant and equipment, or may have better uses for them.

Strategies that share financial risk

Strategic alliances

  • An agreement between two or more firms that does not create a separate entity with joint ownership, in which the firms gain revenues and maximize profits through cooperation for a given period.
  • Primarily aimed at enhancing revenues.
  • Challenge: any member could prematurely quit the alliance, harming the other partners.

International joint ventures

  • A business jointly owned and operated by two or more firms (one local, one foreign) that pool resources to penetrate the host market, share profits and share commercial risk.
  • Used to share increased risk with a local corporate entity; sensible when the capital needed is too large for one company.
  • The local partner knows the domestic economic, cultural and political environment and can help overcome country-specific logistics and bureaucracy.

Higher-risk strategies

Foreign acquisitions and cross-border mergers and acquisitions (M&A)

  • Acquisition: the purchase of established firms abroad to use their existing production, marketing and distribution networks and gain instant access to foreign markets that fit the buyer's global strategy.
  • Firms with clearly identified core competencies or competitive advantages may enter by merging with or acquiring firms overseas.
  • In an acquisition, the home country firm buys the host country firm and implements its own strategy; in a merger, both managements play an active role.

Wholly owned foreign subsidiaries

  • New facilities built and operated overseas ("green field" plants), tailored to the exact needs of the home country firm.
  • Require a large capital investment, so risks and rewards are high.
  • Can need major marketing efforts, because of cultural differences and because the entrant is new and relatively unknown.

The session's icebreaker asks how foreign acquisitions let certain businesses become more competitive in the global marketplace, and how an acquisition can be a defensive strategy.

Why firms invest abroad: multinationals, investment and the host country

ProblemForeign direct investment (FDI) is costly and risky for the investor and brings both benefits and costs to the host country.
Big ideaMultinational enterprises (MNEs) invest abroad to maximize shareholder wealth by raising revenues, cutting costs or diversifying risk, and host countries attract them with a sound investment climate.
How (tools)The definition and evolution of MNEs, the world's ten largest companies (Exhibit 8.3), three strategic motives and their strategies, Dunning's eclectic theory of FDI, the host country's benefits and costs, and the characteristics of an attractive investment climate.
ResultA complete argument about FDI, from the firm's reasons for going abroad to what the host country gains, risks and must offer.
In plain words

Multinational enterprises (MNEs) are firms headquartered in one country that own and control significant manufacturing, services, research and development (R&D) or other business entities in other countries, such as General Electric, Microsoft, Sony, Toyota, BMW, Volkswagen, Siemens and Shell. They have large resources of capital, management talent and technology, and they are willing to take large risks to operate globally. MNE growth, led by U.S. firms, accelerated only after World War II. U.S. MNEs now hold 56 places in the Global 100 by market capitalization, followed by greater China with 15 and France and Switzerland with 4 each, and Exhibit 8.3, subtitled "Multinational enterprises from the United States dominate the world business scene", lists seven U.S. companies among the world's ten largest in 2021. Yet they are being challenged by MNEs from emerging economies such as China, India, Brazil, Russia, South Korea and South Africa.

Why do they invest abroad? In a free enterprise system, the overriding objective is to maximize shareholder wealth, and the session gives three strategic motives that serve it. Firms invest to increase revenues, by entering high-growth markets, stable high-income markets, countries with monopolistic market structures or trade-restricted sectors. They invest to cut costs, through economies of scale, lower factor input costs and exchange rate movements. And they invest to diversify and minimize risk, by stabilizing global cash flows, choosing projects whose returns are not highly correlated with domestic returns, and finding foreign consumers when a product reaches maturity at home. Dunning's eclectic theory explains why the firm uses FDI rather than another route: it has ownership (firm-specific) advantages, location (country-specific) advantages and internalization (mode of entry) advantages.

The host country sees both sides. FDI brings significant financial inflows, new jobs, new technologies, transfers of management and employee skills, more domestic competition and choice, and tax revenue for development. It can also bring environmental pollution from the exploitation of natural resources, exploitation of the labor force that reduces human capital development, a lack of corporate social responsibility for the social consequences of MNE decisions, and political interference in the host country's affairs. To attract investment on good terms, a country needs proper economic reforms, a transparent governance structure and the rule of law; in return, domestic firms and MNEs invest profitably, create jobs, contribute to growth and reduce poverty.
